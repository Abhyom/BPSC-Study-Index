01Food & Its Components

  • Food = any substance consumed to provide nutritional support for an organism; usually of plant or animal origin.
  • Contains essential nutrients — carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins, minerals.
  • Ingested and assimilated by cells to provide energy, maintain life or stimulate growth.
  • Seven major components of food: (i) Carbohydrates (ii) Fats (iii) Proteins (iv) Minerals/salts (v) Nucleic acids (vi) Vitamins (vii) Water.
  • Vitamins and minerals = micronutrients — body needs only tiny amounts; sources: cereals, green vegetables, milk, fruit, meat.
  • Vitamins do NOT kill harmful bacteria — white blood cells destroy bacteria; vitamins are "invigorating elements" needed in small amounts for good health.
  • Foods rich in protein supply nutrient for growth of new tissues (cheese is the correct choice over fruit, vegetables, sweets).
  • Water contains zero calories — no fat, carbohydrate or protein.

02Carbohydrates

Basics & Role

  • Biomolecule of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), usually with H : O ratio 2 : 1 → empirical formula Cx(H2O)y.
  • Some carbohydrates also contain nitrogen, phosphorus, sulphur, but nitrogen is NOT an essential constituent (it IS essential in DNA, RNA, amino acids/proteins, chlorophyll).
  • Main component of food; source of immediate energy; supplies approx. 55–75% of total energy requirement.
  • Carbohydrate is an essential constituent of diet (not starch, glucose or cellulose individually).
  • Stored in liver and skeletal muscles as glycogen — releases energy on requirement. Fat and starch/glycogen are stored; amino acids are NOT stored.
  • Carbohydrate = major food fuel during high-intensity, long-duration exercise.
  • 1 g glucose = 4.5 kcal on oxidation; carbohydrate gives ~4.0 kcal/g.

Sugars & Disaccharides

  • Lactose = natural milk sugar; responsible for the mildly sweet taste of milk (not sucrose/carotene).
  • Three common disaccharides: Sucrose (table sugar) = glucose + fructose; Lactose (major sugar of milk) = glucose + galactose; Maltose (product of starch digestion) = glucose + glucose.
  • Lactobacillus converts lactose → lactic acid; used in making curd and cheese.

Starch & Cellulose

  • Starch = polysaccharide (polymeric carbohydrate of many glucose units joined by glycosidic bonds); plants' energy store.
  • Cellulose = long chain of glucose molecules; found in the plant cell wall.
  • Both starch and cellulose are of plant origin, are polymers, and are made of glucose (C6H12O6).
  • Iodine test detects starch — turns intense blue-black (triiodide ion → intermolecular charge-transfer complex).
  • Cellulose does NOT give colour with iodine — the false statement in the classic "starch vs cellulose" MCQ.

Food Sources & Rice Chemistry

  • Rice has the highest percentage of carbohydrate among wheat, barley, rice, maize (and is the carbohydrate source vs groundnut/apple).
  • Rice has two starches: amylose (long, straight, doesn't gelatinize) and amylopectin.
  • Basmati (long-grain) has the most amylose, least amylopectin → grains elongate on cooking, fluffy, least sticky; medium-grain has more amylopectin, short-grain even more (least/no amylose).
  • Banana — family Musaceae, Musa balbisiana, a berry; large carbohydrate content plus fat, protein, minerals, vitamins; ~89 kcal/100 g (nearest option 116 kcal chosen as "most calorie fruit").
  • Gluten = protein composite of wheat — gives dough elasticity, helps it rise and hold shape, gives bread-making quality and chewy texture.

03Fats, Fatty Acids & Cholesterol

Fats — Basics

  • Compounds of C, H and O; ratio of oxygen to hydrogen is less than in carbohydrates.
  • Formed from 1 glycerol + 3 fatty acids; triglyceride = ester of glycerol + three fatty acids, the main constituent of body fat of vertebrates and of vegetable fat; high triglycerides → obesity and high blood pressure.
  • Source of stored energy; broken into glycerol and fatty acids — glycerol is converted to glucose by the liver.
  • Complete combustion of 1 g fat = 9.0–9.3 kcal; provides ~15–20% of total energy requirement.
  • Healthy man's body fat ≈ 7% of body weight.
  • A normal 2200–2400 kcal diet should contain about 75 g fat/day.
  • Subcutaneous fat forms a barrier against heat loss (thermal insulation), stores energy, and protects underlying structures from trauma.
  • Fats classified by carbon bonds in fatty acids → Saturated and Unsaturated.

Saturated Fats

  • Fatty acid chains with all/predominantly single bonds.
  • Raise blood lipid / plasma cholesterol; PUFA lowers it → lower heart-disease risk. Worldwide advice: keep saturated intake low (cardiovascular risk).
  • Not essential — body can synthesize them.
  • Examples: Butyric acid (butter); Lauric (coconut oil, palm-kernel oil, breast milk); Myristic (cow's milk, dairy); Palmitic (butter, palm oil, meat); Stearic (meat, cocoa butter).
  • Vanaspati ghee contains saturated/trans fats; doctors recommend cooking in oil (unsaturated fats) instead.

Unsaturated Fats & Essential Fatty Acids

  • At least one double/triple bond; monounsaturated = one double bond; polyunsaturated (PUFA) = more than one.
  • Found in fish, corn, walnuts, sunflower seeds, soyabeans, cotton seeds, olive oil, sunflower oil.
  • Diet high in unsaturated, low in saturated fat → low serum cholesterol; healthier choices lower blood cholesterol and cardiovascular risk (still to be taken in moderation).
  • Lower melting point; most are liquid at room temperature.
  • More reactive than saturated fats — because of double bonds (the reason "only single bonds" is false).
  • Examples: Palmitoleic (macadamia nuts); Oleic (olive, canola); Myristoleic (whale blubber); Linoleic (peanut oil, chicken fat, olive oil); Linolenic (linseed oil).
  • Unsaturated fatty acids cannot be synthesized by the body → essential; primary essential fatty acids in humans: linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid. Absence of essential FAs → deficiency diseases; cells synthesize most other fatty acids (via acetyl coenzyme A from glucose oxidation).
  • Linseed (flaxseed) oil = richest oilseed source of α-linolenic acid (18-C omega-3); also in canola, soyabean, walnut oil. Omega-3 prevents fat deposition in blood vessels.
  • Arachidonic acid = polyunsaturated omega-6 essential fatty acid; found in liver, brain, glandular organs; part of phosphatides; formed from dietary linoleic acid; precursor of prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes.
  • Sunflower oil — good-quality edible oil, best for heart patients: high unsaturated FAs (mainly linoleic (PUFA) + oleic (MUFA)), low saturated fat, richest vegetable oil in vitamin E; reduces cardiovascular risk. Sunflower = Helianthus, family Asteraceae
  • Fish flesh is healthier than other animal flesh because of polyunsaturated fatty acids that reduce LDL and lower heart-disease and stroke risk.

Hydrogenation & Trans Fats

  • Hydrogenation converts unsaturated vegetable oils into saturated fats with desirable physical properties (melt at ~30–40°C).
  • Trans fats = mostly artificial; made by adding hydrogen to liquid vegetable oil to solidify it (hydrogenated oils, plus animal fats/oils are forms of trans fat).
  • Trans fat raises bad LDL and LOWERS good HDL cholesterol → higher risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes.
  • "No trans-fat" advertising signals: food is not made of hydrogenated oils, not made of animal fats/oils, oils unlikely to harm cardiovascular health.
  • HDL = "good" cholesterol, scavenges harmful cholesterol from the bloodstream; high HDL lowers heart-disease risk.

Cholesterol

  • Belongs to the steroid family of lipids — a sterol (unsaturated alcohol) = fatty alcohol found in animal fat; NOT a vitamin, enzyme or insecticide.
  • Essential for animal cells — fundamental element of cell membranes; precursor of adrenal & gonadal steroid hormones and bile acids.
  • Excess → atherosclerosis (arterial plaque narrowing/blocking arteries) → angina, heart attack, stroke. Abnormal level is linked to arterial blockage, not veins/kidney stones/cirrhosis.
  • Fat–oil pairs: animal fats – cholesterol; vegetable fats – phytosterol; mineral oil – petroleum; mustard oil is NOT a lubricant (wrong pair).

04Proteins & Amino Acids

Proteins — Basics

  • Essential nutrients; building blocks of body tissue; also a fuel — 4.0 kcal/g.
  • Structural unit = amino acids; proteins are polymers of amino acids, contain C, H, O, N (traces of S, I, Fe etc.).
  • Needed for growth and repair of tissues; form structure, function and regulation of tissues & organs; the primary building block of muscle, bone, skin.
  • Proteins ≈ 17% of body weight (older text) / ~20% per NCERT — the "30%" statement is wrong.
  • After water, proteins are the most abundant constituent of protoplasm; among organic compounds of living material protein is the most abundant (water is the most abundant overall).
  • α-Keratin = main protein of horn, nail, hoof, hair, skin.
  • Gluten (wheat), casein (milk) are proteins.
  • Denaturation: heat, X-rays, heavy-metal salts denature protein; infrared rays do NOT.
  • Protein deficiency → slow body growth; marasmus and kwashiorkor (Protein Energy Malnutrition).

Essential & Non-Essential Amino Acids

  • 20 biologically active amino acids → 10 essential + 10 non-essential.
  • Essential (not synthesized; must come from diet): phenylalanine, valine, threonine, tryptophan, methionine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, histidine, arginine. Arginine & histidine are essential only for juveniles (adults synthesize them).
  • Non-essential (synthesized in body): alanine, asparagine, aspartic acid, cysteine, glutamic acid, glutamine, glycine, proline, serine, tyrosine.
  • Hence a diet need NOT compulsorily contain glycine, serine, tyrosine (A false, R true).
  • Tyrosine: helps form neurotransmitters adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine; boosts physical vigilance/energy, helps with stress and depression; does NOT protect against free radicals/ageing.
  • Complete protein foods (all essential amino acids): meat, poultry, fish, egg, milk, yogurt, quinoa, soyabean; rice is not.
  • Animal protein (milk, eggs, cheese, fish, meat) has all essential amino acids; vegetable protein in pulses, cereals, beans, nuts, oilseeds.

Protein Sources & Content

  • Main sources: pulses, soyabeans, groundnut, milk, cheese, eggs, foodgrains.
  • Legumes: highly nutritious due to protein; low in fat, no cholesterol, high in folate, potassium, iron, magnesium; healthy substitute for meat.
  • Soyabean = richest plant-protein source — ~40% protein, 20–23% fat, ~30% carbohydrate; both an oilseed and a pulse; soya milk resembles cow's milk.
  • Groundnut — rich in both protein and fat: 25.3% protein, 48.1% fat; protein ≈ 1.3× meat, 2.5× eggs, 8× fruits; richest protein among rice/groundnut/apple.

Daily Requirement

  • Babies ~10 g; school-age ~19–34 g; teen boys up to 52 g; teen girls ~46 g; adult men ~56 g.
  • Adult / moderately active woman ≈ 45–46 g; pregnant or breast-feeding (nursing/lactating) = 65–70 g (≥65 g).
  • Protein needs of elderly are higher than the young (biological, environmental, social factors).

06Vitamins — Basics, Discovery & Storage

Definition

  • Vitamins = organic compounds required in small amounts; deficiency causes specific diseases; organism cannot synthesize sufficient quantity so must obtain from diet.
  • Called "accessory dietary / accessory food factors" and "essential food factors"; they are essential for proper growth.
  • Plants can synthesize almost all vitamins; most vitamins cannot be synthesized in human body — so human body does NOT make all it needs.
  • About 30 vitamins, minerals, fatty acids and amino acids are essential micronutrients (cannot be synthesized at all or in sufficient quantity).

Discovery

  • Kazimierz (Casimir) Funk — formulated the concept of vitamins; coined 'vital amines' → vitamines.
  • Umetaro Suzuki — in 1910 extracted a water-soluble micronutrient complex from rice bran, named 'aberic acid'.
  • Frederick Gowland Hopkins — first to elucidate the 'accessory food factor' (trace essential substances in food).
  • Elmer Verner McCollum (with Marguerite Davis) — discovered Vitamin A; also helped discover vitamins B and D and studied trace elements. (The spelling "Maculan" in the question is wrong.)
  • James Lind (Scottish doctor) — vitamin C; found citrus fruits cure scurvy.
  • Paul Müller (Swiss chemist) — NOT linked to vitamin D; discovered insecticidal property of DDT for malaria and yellow-fever vector control.

Classification & Storage

  • Fat-soluble: A, D, E, K — stored in liver and adipose tissue (long periods). Vitamin K is fat-soluble, not water-soluble.
  • Water-soluble: B-group and C — must be supplied regularly; readily excreted in urine; cannot be stored except B12.
  • The liver stores all fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) AND B12; it does NOT store vitamin C (so "A, B, C & D" is not correct → option "None of these").
  • Vitamin C is not stored — bonds with water and is flushed out by urine.

Synthesis in the Body

  • Gut flora (intestinal microorganisms) make vitamin K and B7 (biotin); B12 and K both synthesized by intestinal bacteria.
  • Vitamin D synthesized in skin cells by UV light of sunlight.
  • Vitamin A made from beta-carotene (precursor); Niacin (B3) from amino acid tryptophan.
  • Some vitamins act as hormones (vitamin D) — a true statement; vitamin A is classed as "not formed in our body" in the PYQ key (vs protein, enzyme, hormone, which are).

07Fat-Soluble Vitamins — A · D · E · K

Vitamin A (Retinol)

  • Group of fat-soluble retinoids: retinol, retinal, retinyl esters; roles in immune function, vision, reproduction, cell communication, cell growth/differentiation; formation & maintenance of heart, lungs, kidneys and other organs.
  • Vision: retinol + protein opsin → rhodopsin (light-absorbing molecule) → needed for low-light (scotopic) and colour vision; supports conjunctival membranes and cornea.
  • Deficiency: night blindness (nyctalopia) — first sign; severe form → xerophthalmia (dry, damaged cornea → permanent vision loss/blindness); also dry scaly skin, conjunctivitis.
  • Stored mainly in the liver.
  • Carrot = richest vegetable source; mango = richest among apple/papaya/guava/mango (also rich in C and E); papaya, milk, butter, egg, cod-liver oil. Orange is NOT a vitamin A source (it is C, thiamin, folate, Ca, K).
  • Beta-carotene in green vegetables is converted to A; stopping green vegetables may cause deficiency but not necessarily night blindness (A false, R true).
  • Milk & ghee: carotene in cow's feed → part becomes vitamin A in milk; one PYQ key calls milk NOT a natural source (contains very small amount).
  • Retinol deficiency is NOT linked to rheumatism or colour blindness (colour blindness is genetic, sex-linked).

Vitamin D (Calciferol)

  • Two forms: D2 = ergocalciferol, D3 = cholecalciferol; both inactive until modified in the body → active form calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D); hence considered a hormone.
  • Sunlight (UV) → synthesized in dermal/skin cells → released to blood; dietary: butter, egg yolk, liver, kidney, cheese, fish, cod-liver oil.
  • Helps absorption of calcium and magnesium.
  • Deficiency: rickets (children), osteomalacia (adults); with calcium deficiency → osteoporosis in older adults.
  • NOT the antisterility factor (that is E) and not linked to colour blindness or Paul Müller.

Vitamin E (Tocopherol)

  • Antisterility factor; deficiency → sterility, weakening of reproductive organs and muscles; fat-soluble antioxidant.
  • Wheat-germ oil has the highest vitamin E (germ is only ~2.5% of kernel weight); also vegetable oils, wheat, soyabean, sunflower oil, mango.
  • Not related to night blindness or beri-beri (those are A and B1).

Vitamin K (Naphthoquinone)

  • Blood-clotting / coagulation factor — antihaemorrhagic factor; deficiency → slow clotting, uncontrolled bleeding, increased clotting time; patients cannot be operated easily (excess bleeding risk).
  • Used as antidote to anticoagulant poisons.
  • Sources: green leaves, egg, liver, alfalfa, intestinal bacteria.
  • K deficiency ≠ sterility (E); K is fat-soluble.

08B-Complex Vitamins

Naming Key (Vitamin ↔ Chemical Name)

  • B1 Thiamin · B2 Riboflavin · B3 Niacin · B5 Pantothenic acid · B6 Pyridoxine · B7 Biotin (Vitamin H) · B9 Folic acid · B12 Cobalamin.
  • Thyroxine is a hormone (thyroid gland — biggest endocrine gland in the neck), NOT a vitamin.
  • PYQ key labels Niacin as "B5" in one answer, but Niacin is B3; B5 is pantothenic acid.
  • Thymine is not a vitamin B1 name (decoy in matching).

Vitamin B1 — Thiamin

  • Water-soluble; needed for carbohydrate and amino-acid metabolism; main sources rice bran, whole wheat flour, eggs, yeast, meat.
  • Deficiency → beri-beri: multiple neuritis (neuritides), general debility, heart failure; also Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.
  • Orange is only a low source of B1 (main vitamin C).

Vitamin B2 — Riboflavin

  • Deficiency = ariboflavinosis: skin disorders, hyperaemia & oedema of mouth/throat, angular stomatitis, cheilosis (swollen cracked lips), hair loss, reproductive problems, sore throat, itchy red eyes, degeneration of liver & nervous system. Often occurs with other nutrient deficiencies.
  • Sources: eggs, green vegetables, milk/dairy, meat, mushrooms, almonds; NOT cod-liver oil.
  • Riboflavin–beri-beri is a wrong pair.

Vitamin B3, B6, B9

  • Niacin (B3) → pellagra; synthesized from tryptophan.
  • Pyridoxine (B6) → mental illness (matching); sources rice bran; B6, B12 and folate may improve quality and recall of dreams (B6 = keeps dream in memory longer).
  • Folic acid (B9) → anaemia.

Vitamin B12 — Cobalamin

  • Water-soluble, contains metal cobalt (Co); formula C63H88CoN14O14P; cyanocobalamin = manufactured form used to treat B12 deficiency.
  • Co-factor for enzymes in amino-acid and fatty-acid metabolism, DNA synthesis; needed for new cell synthesis, normal blood formation (maturation of developing RBC in bone marrow), and nervous system function via myelin synthesis.
  • Deficiency → pernicious anaemia, nervous system abnormality; called antianaemic factor.
  • Stored in liver ~3–5 years even though water-soluble — but "water-soluble" is NOT the explanation of storage (A true, R true, R not explanation).
  • Made by intestinal bacteria; found in meat, fish, egg, curd.

09Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid)

  • Chemical name ascorbic acid (not thiamine, citric acid, tartaric acid); water-soluble; antiscorbutic factor; deficiency → scurvy (bleeding gums, joint pain, loose teeth, skin haemorrhages).
  • Roles: normal growth & development; healthy skin; wound healing; collagen formation (cartilage, joints, skin, blood vessels); healthy immune system; important antioxidant (protects against cancer, heart disease, stress); needed for sperm production.
  • Helps absorption of IRON (vitamin D helps Ca/Mg).
  • Richest source: Indian gooseberry (AMLA); other good sources — orange, tomato, lemon, papaya, peas, green leafy vegetables, citrus fruits (lemon, orange). Raw & fresh/citrus fruits are the main source — not milk, ghee, pulses (milk/ghee = fat/vit A; pulses = protein).
  • Among vegetables, chilli has most vitamin C — per 100 g: chilli 143.7 mg, pumpkin 9 mg, pea 40 mg, radish 14.8 mg.
  • Papaya — main vitamin is C; one medium (200 g) papaya ≈ 226 mg (2–3× recommended daily dose); also folate, fibre, vitamin A, Mg, K, Cu, pantothenic acid; Columbus called it "fruit of the angel".
  • Karonda — rich in iron (used for anaemia) and has vitamin C (antiscorbutic).
  • Scurvy-prone diet: milk + egg + bread only (no C source).
  • Orange vs guava/carrot/mango: the PYQ keys mark orange as "highest" in the option sets given, and "orange" as richest among potato/sugarcane/sugarbeet; but amla is the absolute richest.

10Minerals & Trace Elements

Macro vs Micro

  • Living organisms need ≥ 27 elements, of which 15 are metals.
  • Macro-minerals / macronutrients (larger amounts): phosphorus, sulphur, magnesium, potassium, calcium, sodium, chlorine (plus C, H, O, N). Major metals needed: K, Na, Mg, Ca.
  • Micro-minerals / trace elements: <0.01% of body weight, needed at ≈ 1 ppm or less: iron, zinc, copper, selenium, chromium, manganese, iodine, molybdenum, nickel, cobalt, fluorine, boron, chloride.
  • Iodine is micro; phosphorus, sulphur, magnesium are macro; sulphur is NOT a micronutrient.
  • Body content order in a 70-kg man (increasing): Copper < Iron < Sodium < Potassium.

Iron

  • Part of haemoglobin and cytochrome enzymes; transports oxygen; deficiency → anaemia; chronic deficiency → organ failure.
  • Green vegetables / spinach are rich sources; others: liver, sunflower seeds, nuts, beef, lamb, beans, whole grains, dark chocolate, tofu.
  • Spinach: 180 g boiled ≈ 6.43 mg iron vs 170 g hamburger patty ≈ 4.42 mg; also rich in calcium. Carrot ≠ iron; spinach's iron aids haemoglobin and oxygen transport.
  • Vitamin C aids iron absorption; karonda is an iron-rich fruit.
  • Milk has only trace iron; mother's milk lacks iron.

Other Minerals

  • Potassium — electrolyte mineral; maintains healthy heart rhythm via muscle action. Apples are valuable for heart patients because they are a rich source of potassium (trees absorb K more than any other nutrient) and phytochemical antioxidants.
  • Muscle contraction needs calcium, iron, sodium (also magnesium, potassium) plus protein and calories.
  • Phosphorus (as phosphate) — PYQ key: maintains acid-base (acidity/alkalinity) balance in the body.
  • Milk contains calcium and potassium along with proteins and carbohydrates.
  • Metal ↔ molecule: B12 – cobalt; haemoglobin – iron; chlorophyll – magnesium; brass – copper; chalcopyrite – copper ore.

11Antioxidants, Probiotics & Nutraceuticals

Antioxidants

  • Molecule that inhibits oxidation of other molecules; oxidation reactions produce free radicals that start damaging chain reactions (cell damage/death).
  • Role: neutralize free radicals produced during metabolism / protect cells by terminating chain reactions — they are oxidized themselves (reducing agents: thiols, ascorbic acid, polyphenols).
  • NOT: promote vitamin synthesis, prevent oxidation of carbs/fats/proteins "wastage of energy", or activate ageing-delay genes.
  • Good source: fresh fruits and vegetables; apples (phytochemicals), apple known for antioxidants.

Probiotics

  • Live microorganisms — both bacteria and yeasts — with health benefits; "good bacteria".
  • Common groups: Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium; yeast Saccharomyces boulardii.
  • Probiotic organisms DO naturally occur in the human gut (trillions of microbes; ~500 million "good and bad" bacteria) — so "do not occur naturally in gut" is false.
  • Maintain gut flora, boost immunity, aid absorption of calcium, iron, chromium and vitamins A, D, E, K.
  • Help digest milk sugars: fermentation with live bacteria yields a lactose form that is easier to digest; many strains produce β-galactosidase (lactase-like enzyme); L. acidophilus breaks lactose → lactic acid.

Nutraceuticals

  • Any food/food supplement offering nutrient + medicinal effect — medical/health benefit beyond simple nutrition; may prevent disease or recurrence.

12Milk — Composition, Colour & Digestion

Composition

  • Water ≈ 87%; also fat, proteins, carbohydrate (lactose), calcium, potassium → "Complete Diet", globally accepted food for all ages; contains vitamin A, B1, B2, B3, B6, B9, B12.
  • Milk DOES contain B vitamins; poor source of vitamin C (very small amounts) and only trace iron.
  • Buffalo milk fat ≈ 7–7.5%; cow milk fat ≈ 3.5–4.0%.
  • Milk proteins: casein (~80%), lactoglobulin, lactalbumin. Casein = phosphoprotein (bound phosphate) — gives milk its white colour.
  • Yellow tint of cow milk = carotene (hydrocarbon C40Hx from hay, grass, green leaves, carrots); cow converts part to vitamin A, rest passes to milk. Buffalo milk is whiter (no carotene). Vitamin A itself imparts no colour. Cow milk = carotene + casein.
  • Milk sweetness = lactose.

Souring, Curd & Digestion

  • Lactobacillus spoils milk — uses lactose, makes lactic acid → sour taste; same bacteria convert milk to curd, cheese (bacteria, not enzyme/vitamin); also helps control cholesterol; found in intestinal tract.
  • Rennin digests milk protein — produced by stomach cells of young mammals, secreted in large amounts after birth then declines; prorenin activated by HCl → rennin curdles milk into curds and whey so it stays in stomach long enough for protein digestion.
  • Rennin & lactase begin disappearing at age 2–3 years and fully disappear between 6–15 years in most humans → lactose intolerance (official key marked "*"/no single option).

Mother's Milk & Breast-feeding

  • Almost complete diet for newborn but lacks iron.
  • Composition: fat 4.2%, protein 1.1%, carbohydrate 7.5%, minerals 0.2%; contains zinc, calcium, vitamin A, B6, B12, potassium, magnesium, phosphorus.
  • Colostrum (first days after delivery) — rich in protein and antibodies → passive immunity; helps newborn's digestive system develop.
  • Breast-fed vs bottle-fed baby: less obese, greater disease resistance (antibodies), more vitamins & protein; abnormal height growth is NOT a feature.

13Malnutrition & Miscellany

  • Malnutrition = too little OR too much of nutrients: overnutrition + undernutrition + imbalanced nutrition (all three).
  • Protein-energy malnutrition → marasmus, kwashiorkor; lack of essential amino acids from the diet can lead to death.
  • Nitrogen is an essential constituent of DNA, RNA, chlorophyll and proteins — not of simple carbohydrates.
  • Amino acids are not stored (replenish daily); glycogen, fat and tocopherol are stored.
  • Disease/nutrition links: Folic acid–anaemia, Riboflavin–cheilosis, Niacin–pellagra, Cobalamin–pernicious anaemia, Calciferol–osteomalacia.
  • Frozen "role names": B12 = antianaemic; C = antiscorbutic; K = antihaemorrhagic; E = antisterility.
