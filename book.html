01 Historians & Chronicles

Draws on Q1, Q14–Q17, Q38–Q40, Q87.

The Greek author Herodotus is often called the "Father of History." His famous book Historica / The Histories describes Indo-Persian relations during the 5th century BCE.

Kalhana's Rajatarangini

Kalhana, a Kashmiri Brahmin, authored Rajatarangini ("River of Kings") in Sanskrit in the 12th century — a metrical historical chronicle of Kashmir, particularly its kings. He completed this pioneering work during the reign of his contemporary ruler Jai Singh / Jaisimha (1128–1149). The text comprises eight Tarangas and 7,826 verses; the first three Tarangas narrate the conventional ancient history of Kashmir.

Rajatarangini was later continued: Jonaraja extended the narration down to the reign of Sultan Zainul Abedin (calling his continuation, like Kalhana, Rajavali); after Jonaraja's death, his pupil Srivara carried the history of Kashmir further.

Other historical chronicles

Gaudavaho, a work comparable to Rajatarangini, was composed by Vakpati — a Prakrit epic poem from the court of King Yasovarman of Kanauj, giving a detailed account of Yasovarman's digvijaya (conquests).

⚠ Trap — commonly mismatched historical-work pairs

Life of Hiuen Tsang was written by Hui-li — correct. "The Natural History" was written by Pliny the Elder, NOT by Ptolemy (a frequent wrong pairing). "Historia Philippica" is correctly attributed to Pompeius Trogus. "The Histories" is correctly attributed to Herodotus.

02 Gupta-Era Golden Age Classics

Draws on the main theory block plus Q3–Q5.

Sanskrit literature reached its peak during the Gupta period — an era known for major achievements in both prose and poetry.

Gupta-period classics — author ledger

  • Amarakosha — Amarsimha (a lexicon).
  • Kamasutra — Vatsyayana.
  • Meghdoot (Meghadutam) — Kalidasa.
  • Mudrarakshasa — Vishakhadatta.

Mudrarakshasa, a Sanskrit drama by Vishakhadatta, was itself written during the Gupta period even though its plot is set earlier — it describes how Chandragupta (Maurya), with the help of Kautilya, overthrew the Nandas, and offers a picture of the socio-economic conditions under the Mauryas. Vishakhadatta also authored Devichandraguptam.

03 Kalidasa — Life & Works

Draws on Q9–Q11, Q53–Q56.

Kalidasa was a Sanskrit poet and dramatist, probably the greatest Indian writer of any epoch. Seven works are identified as genuinely his:

Kalidasa's seven authenticated works

  • Abhijnanashakuntalam (Shakuntalam)
  • Vikramorvashiyam
  • Malavikagnimitram
  • Raghuvamsham
  • Kumarasambhavam
  • Meghadutam
  • Ritusamharam

His first play was Malavikagnimitram — five acts telling the love story of King Agnimitra of Vidisha (of the Shunga dynasty) and Malavika; the protagonist is Agnimitra himself (son of Pushyamitra Shunga, the dynasty's founder).

⚠ Trap — works wrongly attributed to Kalidasa

Janaki-haranam is NOT by Kalidasa — it was written by Kumaradasa. Shringar Shatak is NOT by Kalidasa — it belongs to the Shataka-traya, generally attributed to Bhartrihari. Uttararamacharitam is NOT by Kalidasa — it was written by Bhavabhuti. Mrichchhakatikam is NOT by Kalidasa — it was composed by Sudraka.

⚠ Trap — frequently mismatched author-work pairs

Kadambari is by Banabhatta, NOT by Subandhu — a recurring wrong pairing in MCQs. Karpuramanjari is by Rajshekhara, NOT by Harsha. Correctly matched (for contrast): Kalidasa–Raghuvansa, Bhasa–Svapna Vasavadattam, Harsha–Ratnavali, Mudrarakshasa–Vishakhadatta, Saundarananda–Ashvaghosha, Malavikagnimitram–Kalidasa.

Harshavardhana as an author

Harshavardhana was himself a great patron of education and learning, credited with authoring three plays: Ratnavali, Priyadarshika, and Nagananda. Historian Rama Shankar Tripathi observed that "Harsha was a patron of letters... he appears to have wielded the pen with no less dexterity and effect than the sword." The royal author, sometimes called "Harshadeva," has been identified with Harsha of Kannauj, since no other sovereign of that name fits the requirements of the case.

05 Mahabharata-Themed Epic Poetry

Draws on Q19–20.

Sanskrit works drawing their theme from the Mahabharata

  • Naishadhiyacharita — by Sriharsha; the story of Nala and Damayanti.
  • Kiratarjuniyam — by Bharavi; an epic describing combat between Arjuna and Lord Shiva (Shiva disguised as a kirata, or mountain-dwelling hunter) at the Indrakeeladri Hills — considered the most powerful poem in Sanskrit.
  • Sisupalavadha — by Magha; based on the episode where Sisupala insults Lord Krishna, who beheads him in the ensuing duel.
  • Dashkumarcharitam — by Dandi; the adventures of ten princes (grouped with the above in the source, though its theme is not itself drawn from the Mahabharata).

06 Buddhist & Pali Literature

Draws on Q29–Q34, Q36, Q41, Q47.

The Milindapanha is a Buddhist text in Pali, structured as a dialogue between the Indian monk Nagasena and the Indo-Greek king Milinda (Menander). It is also notable as a religious text that explains the eight different phases of agricultural work.

Ashvaghosha, a noted poet instrumental in the propagation of Buddhism, wrote the court poem Saundarananda Kavya — later discovered and edited by Pandit Haraprasada Shastri. He is also the author of Buddhacharita, a poetic narrative of the Buddha's life.

⚠ Trap — Buddhacharita's author

Buddhacharita was NOT composed by Vasubandhu — it is correctly attributed to Ashvaghosha.

Among Jain vs Buddhist text classification: Parishishtaparvan (by Hemchandra) and Trishashtilakshana Mahapurana (by Chavund Rai / Chamund Rai) are Jain texts, while Nettipakarana and Avadanashataka are Buddhist texts.

Nitisara, an early book of politics, was written by Kamandaka — not Kautilya — and became one of the most influential works of political thought across South and South-East Asia.

"Zero" was discovered by an anonymous Indian (around the 2nd century BC, per one source note); the Arabs learned of it from India and carried it to Europe — the Arabs are recorded as first using zero in 873 AD.

08 Law, Polity & Dharmashastra

Draws on Q72, Q81–83, plus the Mitakshara/Dayabhaga and Q71 matching sets.

Manusmriti (also called Manav Dharmashastra) is mainly concerned with the social system — the earliest metrical work on Brahminical Dharma in Hinduism, and one of the 18 major Smritis. Manu is considered the originator/first law-giver of India.

Arthashastra was written by Chanakya (Kautilya).

Mitakshara vs Dayabhaga

Mitakshara is a commentary (vivarti) on the Yajnavalkya Smriti, written by Vijnanesvara (Vigyaneshwara). Dayabhaga is a Hindu law treatise written by Jimutavahana. Both deal with laws of inheritance.

⚠ Trap — the caste and gender misconceptions

It is NOT true that Mitakshara was civil law for upper castes and Dayabhaga for lower castes — that pairing is incorrect. What IS correct: under Mitakshara, sons can claim a right to property during the father's lifetime; under Dayabhaga, sons can claim that right only after the father's death. Also, it is Mitakshara — not Dayabhaga — that covers property held by BOTH male and female family members; Dayabhaga's scope is limited to property held by male members only (the reverse of what is sometimes assumed).

Chandragupta-II's court was famously adorned by nine gems (Navratnas); Kalidasa was the most prominent of them, and Amarsimha — the lexicographer behind the Amarakosha — was also among the Navratnas.

⚠ Trap — Panini's period and patronage

Panini is NOT associated with Pushyamitra Shunga — he belonged to roughly the 7th century BC and authored the Ashtadhyayi. It is Patanjali who is associated with Pushyamitra Shunga (as author of the Mahabhashya).

11 Matching Sets, Quotes & Miscellany

Draws on Q47, Q50, Q63–68, Q78, Q80, Q84, Q88 and the theory block on Panchatantra/Natyashastra.

Panchatantra

Panchatantra is a collection of Indian animal fables with extensive circulation both within India and worldwide — translated into 15 Indian languages and at least 50 foreign languages. Compiled in Sanskrit, it is attributed to Pandit Vishnu Sharma. In the Mughal era, Abul Fazl translated it into Persian under the title Ayar-e-Danish.

Natyashastra

Called the "Bible" of North Indian classical music/performing arts, the Natyashastra is a Sanskrit treatise covering drama, dance and music, traditionally attributed to Bharata Muni.

Quotations traced to their source texts

Famous lines and where they come from

  • "Thy right is to work only, but never with its fruits" — from the Shrimad Bhagavad Gita.
  • "What is here is also found elsewhere, but if not here is found nowhere else" — from the Mahabharata.
  • The ancient name "Nilotpala" for the Mahanadi river is stated in the Vayu Purana.

Sangam Sahitya is notably silent about the trade routes of ancient India — unlike the Milindapanha or the Jataka Tales, which do touch on trade.

The most ancient musical instrument among common options is the Veena — it has been part of Indian culture and music since ancient times, whereas the Sitar, Sarod and Tabla were all introduced in the medieval era.

Mattavilasa Prahasana was written by the versatile Pallava ruler and scholar Mahendravarman-I, describing contemporary social and religious life.

⚠ Trap — a cluster of "which pair is wrongly matched" questions

  • Devichandragupta is by Vishakhadatta, NOT by Bilhana (a wrong pairing sometimes given in the question stem).
  • Milinda-panha's interlocutor/author-figure is Nagasena, NOT Nagarjuna.
  • Correctly matched for contrast: Hammira Mahakavya — Nayachandra Suri; Nitivakyamrita — Somadeva Suri.
  • Of a four-pair set built around these, only two of four are genuinely correct.
