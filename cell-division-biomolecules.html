01Unit 3 Introduction — Reductionist Biology

Chapters in this Unit

  • Unit 3 — Cell: Structure and Functions — comprises Chapter 8: Cell: The Unit of Life, Chapter 9: Biomolecules and Chapter 10: Cell Cycle and Cell Division.
  • The chapters give a description of cell structure and cell growth by division.
  • In Chapter 9 a brief description of biomolecules is provided.

Cell Theory — Unity & Mystery

  • Biology is the study of living organisms; detailed description of their form and appearance only brought out their diversity.
  • It is the cell theory that emphasised the unity underlying this diversity — i.e., the cellular organisation of all life forms.
  • Cell theory also created a sense of mystery around living phenomena — physiological and behavioural processes.
  • This mystery was the requirement of integrity of cellular organisation for living phenomena to be demonstrated or observed.

Physico-chemical Approach

  • To understand physiological and behavioural processes one can take a physico-chemical approach and use cell-free systems to investigate.
  • This enables description of the processes in molecular terms.
  • The approach is established by analysis of living tissues for elements and compounds — telling us what types of organic compounds are present in living organisms.
  • Next stage: What are these compounds doing inside a cell? and in what way do they carry out gross physiological processes — digestion, excretion, memory, defense, recognition, etc.
  • In other words: what is the molecular basis of all physiological processes? It can also explain abnormal processes during any diseased condition.
Key Term
  • This physico-chemical approach to study living organisms is called 'Reductionist Biology' — concepts and techniques of physics and chemistry are applied to understand biology.

02Scientist Profile — G.N. Ramachandran

G.N. Ramachandran (1922 – 2001)

  • G.N. Ramachandran — an outstanding figure in the field of protein structure; lived 1922 – 2001.
  • Founder of the 'Madras school' of conformational analysis of biopolymers.
  • Discovered the triple helical structure of collagen — published in Nature in 1954.
  • Analysed the allowed conformations of proteins through the 'Ramachandran plot' — both rank among the most outstanding contributions in structural biology.

Life

  • Born October 8, 1922 in a small town not far from Cochin on the southwestern coast of India.
  • His father was a professor of mathematics at a local college — shaped his interest in mathematics.
  • Graduated in 1942 as the top-ranking student in B.Sc. (Honors) Physics, University of Madras.
  • Received a Ph.D. from Cambridge University in 1949.
  • At Cambridge he met Linus Pauling and was deeply influenced by his publications on models of the α-helix and β-sheet structures — which directed his attention to solving the structure of collagen.
  • Passed away at the age of 78, on April 7, 2001.

03What is a Cell?

Living vs Non-living

  • What makes an organism living — what an inanimate thing does not have — is the presence of the basic unit of life: the cell, in all living organisms.
  • All organisms are composed of cells.
  • Unicellular organisms — composed of a single cell.
  • Multicellular organisms — composed of many cells (like us).

Cell as the Fundamental Unit

  • Unicellular organisms are capable of (i) independent existence and (ii) performing the essential functions of life.
  • Anything less than a complete structure of a cell does not ensure independent living.
  • Hence the cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms.

Early Discoveries

  • Antonie von Leeuwenhoek — first saw and described a live cell.
  • Robert Brown — later discovered the nucleus.
  • Invention of the microscope and its improvement leading to the electron microscope revealed all the structural details of the cell.

05An Overview of the Cell

What We See in Cells

  • The onion cell — a typical plant cell — has a distinct cell wall as its outer boundary and, just within it, the cell membrane.
  • The human cheek cells have an outer membrane as the delimiting structure.
  • Inside each cell is a dense, membrane-bound structure — the nucleus — containing chromosomes, which contain the genetic material, DNA.

Eukaryotic vs Prokaryotic

  • Cells with a membrane-bound nucleus = eukaryotic.
  • Cells that lack a membrane-bound nucleus = prokaryotic.
  • In both, a semi-fluid matrix called cytoplasm occupies the volume of the cell — the main arena of cellular activities in both plant and animal cells; various chemical reactions occur in it to keep the cell in the 'living state'.

Organelles

  • Besides the nucleus, eukaryotic cells have other membrane-bound organelles: endoplasmic reticulum (ER), golgi complex, lysosomes, mitochondria, microbodies and vacuoles.
  • Prokaryotic cells lack membrane-bound organelles.

Non-membrane-bound Organelles

  • Ribosomes — non-membrane-bound organelles found in all cells — eukaryotic as well as prokaryotic.
  • Within the cell, ribosomes occur in the cytoplasm, and also within the two organelles — chloroplasts (in plants) and mitochondria — and on rough ER.
  • Centrosome — another non-membrane-bound organelle in animal cells; helps in cell division.

Size

  • Cells differ greatly in size, shape and activities.
  • Mycoplasmas — the smallest cells: only 0.3 µm in length.
  • Bacteria — could be 3 to 5 µm.
  • Largest isolated single cell — the egg of an ostrich.
  • Human red blood cells — about 7.0 µm in diameter.
  • Nerve cells — some of the longest cells.

07Prokaryotic Cells — General Features

Representatives & Nature

  • Represented by bacteria, blue-green algae, mycoplasma and PPLO (Pleuro Pneumonia Like Organisms).
  • Generally smaller and multiply more rapidly than eukaryotic cells.
  • May vary greatly in shape and size.
  • Four basic shapes of bacteria: bacillus (rod-like), coccus (spherical), vibrio (comma-shaped), spirillum (spiral).

Organisation

  • Organisation is fundamentally similar even though prokaryotes show a wide variety of shapes and functions.
  • All have a cell wall surrounding the cell membrane — except mycoplasma.
  • Semi-fluid matrix filling the cell = cytoplasm.
  • No well-defined nucleus; genetic material is basically naked — not enveloped by a nuclear membrane.
  • No organelles like those in eukaryotes — except ribosomes; they have unique inclusions.

Genetic Material & Plasmids

  • Genomic DNA = the single chromosome / circular DNA.
  • Many bacteria have small circular DNA outside the genomic DNA = plasmids.
  • Plasmid DNA confers certain unique phenotypic characters — one such character is resistance to antibiotics.
  • In higher classes: plasmid DNA is used to monitor bacterial transformation with foreign DNA.
  • Nuclear membrane is found in eukaryotes — absent here.

08Cell Envelope and its Modifications (8.4.1)

Three-layered Envelope

  • Most prokaryotic cells, particularly bacterial cells, have a chemically complex cell envelope.
  • It is a tightly bound three-layered structure: outermost glycocalyx → cell wall → plasma membrane.
  • Each layer performs a distinct function, yet they act together as a single protective unit.

Gram Staining

  • Bacteria are classified into two groups based on differences in the cell envelopes and their response to the staining procedure developed by Gram.
  • Gram positive — those that take up the Gram stain.
  • Gram negative — those that do not take up the stain.

Layer-by-Layer

  • Glycocalyx — differs in composition and thickness among bacteria; a loose sheath called the slime layer in some; thick and tough called the capsule in others.
  • Cell wall — determines the shape of the cell and provides strong structural support to prevent the bacterium from bursting or collapsing.
  • Plasma membrane — selectively permeable, interacts with the outside world; structurally similar to that of eukaryotes.

09Mesosome and Chromatophores

Mesosome

  • A special membranous structure formed by extensions of the plasma membrane into the cell; essentially infoldings of cell membrane.
  • Extensions are in the form of vesicles, tubules and lamellae.
  • A specialised differentiated form of cell membrane — characteristic of prokaryotes.
  • Functions: cell wall formation, DNA replication and its distribution to daughter cells, respiration, secretion processes, to increase the surface area of the plasma membrane and enzymatic content.

Chromatophores

  • In some prokaryotes like cyanobacteria, there are other membranous extensions into the cytoplasm called chromatophores, which contain pigments.

10Flagella, Pili and Fimbriae

Bacterial Flagella

  • Bacterial cells may be motile or non-motile.
  • If motile, they have thin filamentous extensions from their cell wall called flagella; bacteria show a range in the number and arrangement of flagella.
  • Bacterial flagellum has three parts: filament, hook and basal body.
  • The filament is the longest portion and extends from the cell surface to the outside.

Pili and Fimbriae

  • Surface structures of bacteria — do not play a role in motility.
  • Pili — elongated tubular structures made of a special protein.
  • Fimbriae — small bristle-like fibres sprouting out of the cell; in some bacteria they help to attach the bacteria to rocks in streams and to host tissues.

11Ribosomes and Inclusion Bodies (8.4.2)

Prokaryotic Ribosomes

  • Associated with the plasma membrane of the cell.
  • Size about 15 nm by 20 nm.
  • Made of two subunits — 50S and 30S — which together form 70S prokaryotic ribosomes.
  • Ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis.
  • Several ribosomes attached to a single mRNA form a chain called polyribosomes or polysome; the ribosomes of a polysome translate the mRNA into proteins.

Inclusion Bodies

  • Reserve material in prokaryotic cells is stored in the cytoplasm as inclusion bodies.
  • Not bound by any membrane system; lie free in the cytoplasm.
  • Examples: phosphate granules, cyanophycean granules and glycogen granules.
  • Gas vacuoles are found in blue-green and purple and green photosynthetic bacteria.

General Features

  • Have a variety of complex locomotory and cytoskeletal structures.
  • Genetic material is organised into chromosomes.

14Cell Membrane (8.5.1)

Discovery & Composition

  • Detailed structure studied only after the advent of the electron microscope in the 1950s.
  • Meanwhile, chemical studies on the cell membrane — especially in human red blood cells (RBCs) — enabled scientists to deduce the possible structure.
  • Mainly composed of lipids and proteins; later also found to possess carbohydrate.
  • Major lipids are phospholipids, arranged in a bilayer.
  • Lipids are arranged with the polar head towards the outer sides and the hydrophobic tails towards the inner part — this protects the nonpolar tail of saturated hydrocarbons from the aqueous environment.
  • In addition to phospholipids, membrane contains cholesterol.
  • The ratio of protein and lipid varies considerably in different cell types; human erythrocyte membrane has about 52% protein and 40% lipids.

Membrane Proteins

  • Classified on ease of extraction as integral and peripheral.
  • Peripheral proteins lie on the surface of the membrane.
  • Integral proteins are partially or totally buried in the membrane.

Fluid Mosaic Model

  • Improved model proposed by Singer and Nicolson in 1972 — widely accepted as the fluid mosaic model.
  • The quasi-fluid nature of lipid enables lateral movement of proteins within the overall bilayer.
  • This ability to move within the membrane is measured as its fluidity.
  • Fluidity is important for: cell growth, formation of intercellular junctions, secretion, endocytosis, cell division, etc.

Transport Across the Membrane

  • One of the most important functions of the plasma membrane is transport of molecules across it; the membrane is selectively permeable.
  • Passive transport — many molecules move across without energy; neutral solutes move by simple diffusion along the concentration gradient — higher to lower concentration.
  • Osmosis — movement of water by diffusion from higher to lower concentration.
  • Polar molecules cannot pass through the nonpolar lipid bilayer — they need a carrier protein to facilitate transport.
  • Active transport — a few ions/molecules move against the concentration gradient (lower to higher); an energy-dependent process in which ATP is utilised — e.g., Na+/K+ Pump.

15Cell Wall (8.5.2)

Cell Wall

  • A non-living rigid structure forming an outer covering for the plasma membrane of fungi and plants.
  • Functions: gives shape to the cell; protects from mechanical damage and infection; helps in cell-to-cell interaction; provides a barrier to undesirable macromolecules.
  • Algae: cellulose, galactans, mannans and minerals like calcium carbonate.
  • Other plants: cellulose, hemicellulose, pectins and proteins.

Primary Wall, Secondary Wall & Middle Lamella

  • Primary wall — of a young plant cell; capable of growth, which gradually diminishes as the cell matures.
  • Secondary wall — formed on the inner (towards membrane) side as the cell matures.
  • Middle lamella — a layer mainly of calcium pectate; holds or glues neighbouring cells together.
  • The cell wall and middle lamellae may be traversed by plasmodesmata — connect the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells.

16Endomembrane System (8.5.3)

Endomembrane System

  • Many membranous organelles are considered together as an endomembrane system because their functions are coordinated.
  • Includes: endoplasmic reticulum (ER), golgi complex, lysosomes and vacuoles.
  • Mitochondria, chloroplast and peroxisomes are NOT part of the endomembrane system — their functions are not coordinated with the above components.

17Endoplasmic Reticulum (8.5.3.1)

ER

  • A network or reticulum of tiny tubular structures scattered in the cytoplasm, seen by electron microscopy.
  • ER divides the intracellular space into two compartments — luminal (inside ER) and extra-luminal (cytoplasm).
  • Often shows ribosomes attached to the outer surface.
  • Rough ER (RER) — bears ribosomes; frequently observed in cells actively involved in protein synthesis and secretion; extensive and continuous with the outer membrane of the nucleus.
  • Smooth ER (SER) — no ribosomes; the major site for synthesis of lipid; in animal cells lipid-like steroidal hormones are synthesised in SER.
  • Summary: ER contains tubules or cisternae; helps in transport of substances and synthesis of proteins, lipoproteins and glycogen.

18Golgi Apparatus (8.5.3.2)

Structure

  • Camillo Golgi (1898) first observed densely stained reticular structures near the nucleus; later named Golgi bodies after him.
  • Consist of many flat, disc-shaped sacs or cisternae of 0.5 µm to 1.0 µm diameter, stacked parallel to each other; varied number of cisternae per complex.
  • Cisternae are concentrically arranged near the nucleus with a distinct convex cis (forming) face and a concave trans (maturing) face.
  • The cis and trans faces are entirely different but interconnected.

Function

  • Principally packaging of materials — to be delivered to intra-cellular targets or secreted outside the cell.
  • Materials packaged as vesicles from the ER fuse with the cis face and move toward the maturing face — which explains why the Golgi remains in close association with the ER.
  • Proteins synthesised by ribosomes on the ER are modified in the Golgi cisternae before release from the trans face.
  • Important site of formation of glycoproteins and glycolipids.

19Lysosomes (8.5.3.3)

Lysosomes

  • Membrane-bound vesicular structures formed by the process of packaging in the golgi apparatus.
  • Rich in almost all types of hydrolytic enzymes (hydrolases — lipases, proteases, carbohydrases).
  • Hydrolases are optimally active at acidic pH.
  • Capable of digesting carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids.
  • Summary: lysosomes are single membrane structures with enzymes for digestion of all types of macromolecules.

20Vacuoles (8.5.3.4)

Vacuoles

  • The vacuole is the membrane-bound space found in the cytoplasm; contains water, sap, excretory product and other materials not useful for the cell.
  • Bound by a single membrane called the tonoplast.
  • In plant cells, vacuoles can occupy up to 90 per cent of the cell volume.
  • In plants, the tonoplast facilitates transport of ions and other materials against concentration gradients into the vacuole — so their concentration is significantly higher in the vacuole than in the cytoplasm.
  • Amoeba — the contractile vacuole is important for osmoregulation and excretion.
  • In many cells, as in protists, food vacuoles are formed by engulfing food particles.

21Mitochondria (8.5.4)

Structure

  • Not easily visible under the microscope unless specifically stained.
  • Number per cell is variable, depending on the physiological activity of the cells; shape and size also variable.
  • Typically sausage-shaped or cylindrical: diameter 0.2–1.0 µm (average 0.5 µm); length 1.0–4.1 µm.
  • Each mitochondrion is a double membrane-bound structure — outer membrane and inner membrane — dividing its lumen into two aqueous compartments: the outer compartment and the inner compartment.
  • Inner compartment is filled with a dense homogeneous substance, the matrix.
  • The outer membrane forms the continuous limiting boundary of the organelle.
  • The inner membrane forms infoldings called cristae (sing.: crista) towards the matrix — they increase the surface area.
  • The two membranes have their own specific enzymes associated with mitochondrial function.

Function & Genetic Features

  • Sites of aerobic respiration; produce cellular energy as ATP — hence 'power houses' of the cell.
  • Summary: help in oxidative phosphorylation and generation of ATP; outer membrane smooth, inner folds into several cristae.
  • Matrix possesses a single circular DNA molecule, a few RNA molecules, 70S ribosomes and components required for protein synthesis.
  • Mitochondria divide by fission.

22Plastids (8.5.5)

General & Types

  • Found in all plant cells and in euglenoides; large, easily observed under the microscope.
  • Bear specific pigments, imparting specific colours to plants.
  • Based on the type of pigments, classified into chloroplasts, chromoplasts and leucoplasts.
  • Summary: plastids are pigment-containing organelles found in plant cells only.

Chloroplast, Chromoplast, Leucoplast

  • Chloroplasts — contain chlorophyll and carotenoid pigments — trap light energy essential for photosynthesis.
  • Chromoplasts — contain fat-soluble carotenoid pigments like carotene, xanthophylls and others — giving yellow, orange or red colour.
  • Leucoplasts — colourless plastids of varied shapes and sizes with stored nutrients.
  • Amyloplasts store carbohydrates (starch), e.g., potato.
  • Elaioplasts store oils and fats; aleuroplasts store proteins.

Chloroplast Structure

  • Majority of chloroplasts of green plants are in the mesophyll cells of leaves.
  • Lens-shaped, oval, spherical, discoid or even ribbon-like; length 5–10 µm, width 2–4 µm.
  • Number: 1 per cell in Chlamydomonas (green alga) to 20–40 per cell in the mesophyll.
  • Like mitochondria, double membrane bound; the inner chloroplast membrane is relatively less permeable.
  • The space limited by the inner membrane = stroma.
  • Number of organised flattened membranous sacs — thylakoids — are present in the stroma; stacked like piles of coins = grana (sing.: granum) or intergranal thylakoids.
  • Flat membranous tubules — stroma lamellae — connect the thylakoids of different grana.
  • The thylakoid membrane encloses a space = lumen; chlorophyll pigments are present in the thylakoids.
  • Stroma contains enzymes for synthesis of carbohydrates and proteins, small double-stranded circular DNA and ribosomes.
  • Chloroplast ribosomes are 70S — smaller than cytoplasmic ribosomes (80S).
  • Summary: grana — site of light reactions; stroma — site of dark reactions.

23Ribosomes (8.5.6)

Eukaryotic Ribosomes

  • Granular structures first observed under the electron microscope as dense particles by George Palade (1953).
  • Composed of ribonucleic acid (RNA) and proteins; not surrounded by any membrane.
  • Eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S; prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S.
  • Each ribosome has two subunits — larger and smaller: 80S = 60S + 40S; 70S = 50S + 30S.
  • 'S' (Svedberg's Unit) stands for the sedimentation coefficient; indirectly a measure of density and size.
  • Summary: ribosomes are involved in protein synthesis; occur freely in the cytoplasm or are associated with ER.

24Cytoskeleton (8.5.7)

Cytoskeleton

  • An elaborate network of filamentous proteinaceous structures in the cytoplasm — microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments — collectively the cytoskeleton.
  • Functions: mechanical support, motility, maintenance of the shape of the cell.

25Cilia and Flagella (8.5.8)

Cilia and Flagella

  • Hair-like outgrowths of the cell membrane.
  • Cilia (sing.: cilium) — small structures that work like oars, causing movement of either the cell or the surrounding fluid.
  • Flagella (sing.: flagellum) — comparatively longer; responsible for cell movement.
  • Prokaryotic bacteria also possess flagella but these are structurally different from eukaryotic flagella.

Internal Structure — the 9+2 Array

  • Covered with plasma membrane.
  • The core is the axoneme, with many microtubules running parallel to the long axis.
  • Axoneme usually has nine doublets of radially arranged peripheral microtubules and a pair of centrally located microtubules — the 9+2 array.
  • Central tubules are connected by bridges and enclosed by a central sheath, which is connected to one tubule of each peripheral doublet by a radial spoke — thus nine radial spokes.
  • Peripheral doublets are interconnected by linkers (interdoublet bridge).
  • Both cilium and flagellum emerge from centriole-like structures called basal bodies.

26Centrosome and Centrioles (8.5.9)

Centrosome & Centriole Structure

  • Centrosome — an organelle usually containing two cylindrical structures called centrioles, surrounded by amorphous pericentriolar material.
  • The two centrioles lie perpendicular to each other; each has an organisation like a cartwheel.
  • Made up of nine evenly spaced peripheral fibrils of tubulin protein; each peripheral fibril is a triplet; adjacent triplets are also linked.
  • The central part of the proximal region is proteinaceous — the hub — connected with tubules of the peripheral triplets by radial spokes made of protein.

Function

  • Centrioles form the basal body of cilia or flagella.
  • Form spindle fibres that give rise to the spindle apparatus during cell division in animal cells.
  • Centrioles are absent in almost all plant cells.

27Nucleus (8.5.10)

Discovery & Interphase Nucleus

  • First described by Robert Brown as early as 1831.
  • The material stained by basic dyes was named chromatin by Flemming.
  • The interphase nucleus (nucleus of a non-dividing cell) has highly extended and elaborate nucleoprotein fibres — chromatin, nuclear matrix and one or more spherical bodies called nucleoli (sing.: nucleolus).

Nuclear Envelope & Pores

  • Consists of two parallel membranes with a space between (10 to 50 nm) called the perinuclear space; forms a barrier between nuclear materials and the cytoplasm.
  • The outer membrane usually remains continuous with the ER and also bears ribosomes.
  • At places the envelope is interrupted by minute nuclear pores, formed by fusion of its two membranes.
  • Nuclear pores are the passages for movement of RNA and protein molecules in both directions between nucleus and cytoplasm.
  • Summary: the nucleus is enclosed by a double-membrane nuclear envelope with nuclear pores; the inner membrane encloses the nucleoplasm and chromatin; it controls the activities of organelles and plays a major role in heredity.

Number of Nuclei

  • Normally one nucleus per cell; variations in number are frequently observed (e.g., multinucleate cells).
  • Some mature cells even lack a nucleus — erythrocytes of many mammals and sieve tube cells of vascular plants.

Nucleolus

  • Spherical structures in the nucleoplasm; the nuclear matrix / nucleoplasm contains nucleolus and chromatin.
  • Not a membrane-bound structure — its content is continuous with the rest of the nucleoplasm.
  • A site for active ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis.
  • Larger and more numerous nucleoli are present in cells actively carrying out protein synthesis.

28Chromatin and Chromosomes

Chromatin

  • Interphase nucleus has a loose and indistinct network of nucleoprotein fibres = chromatin; during cell division, cells show structured chromosomes in place of the nucleus.

  • On the sides of the centromere are disc-shaped structures called kinetochores.
  • The centromere holds two chromatids of a chromosome.

32NCERT Summary — Key Points

Summary Points

  • All organisms are made of cells or aggregates of cells; cells vary in shape, size and activities/functions.
  • Based on the presence or absence of a membrane-bound nucleus and other organelles, cells and hence organisms are named eukaryotic or prokaryotic.
  • A typical eukaryotic cell consists of cell membrane, nucleus and cytoplasm; plant cells have a cell wall outside the cell membrane.
  • The plasma membrane is selectively permeable and facilitates transport of several molecules.
  • The endomembrane system includes ER, golgi complex, lysosomes and vacuoles; all cell organelles perform different but specific functions.
  • Centrosome and centriole form the basal body of cilia and flagella; in animal cells centrioles also form the spindle apparatus.
  • Nucleus contains nucleoli and chromatin network; it controls the activities of organelles and plays a major role in heredity.
  • ER — rough and smooth; helps in transport of substances, synthesis of proteins, lipoproteins and glycogen.
  • Golgi body — membranous organelle of flattened sacs; secretions of cells are packed in them and transported from the cell.
  • Lysosomes — single membrane structures with enzymes for digestion of all types of macromolecules.
  • Ribosomes — protein synthesis; free in cytoplasm or associated with ER.
  • Mitochondria — oxidative phosphorylation and ATP; double membrane; outer smooth, inner folds into cristae.
  • Plastids — pigment-containing organelles in plant cells only; chloroplasts trap light energy; grana: light reactions; stroma: dark reactions; green plastids = chloroplasts (chlorophyll); other coloured plastids = chromoplasts (carotene, xanthophyll).
  • The nucleus is enclosed by a nuclear envelope — a double membrane with nuclear pores; the inner membrane encloses nucleoplasm and chromatin.
  • Thus, the cell is the structural and functional unit of life.

Cell Cycle and Cell Division

01Introduction — Growth by Division

Why Cells Divide

  • All organisms, even the largest, start their life from a single cell.
  • Growth and reproduction are characteristics of cells — indeed of all living organisms.
  • All cells reproduce by dividing into two; each parental cell gives rise to two daughter cells each time they divide.
  • Daughter cells grow and divide, forming a new cell population from the growth and division of a single parental cell and its progeny.
  • Such cycles of growth and division allow a single cell to form a structure of millions of cells.

02Cell Cycle — Definition

Definition

  • During cell division, DNA replication and cell growth also take place; all three must occur in a coordinated way to ensure correct division and progeny cells with intact genomes.
  • The sequence of events by which a cell duplicates its genome, synthesises the other constituents and eventually divides into two daughter cells = cell cycle.
  • Cell growth (cytoplasmic increase) is a continuous process, but DNA synthesis occurs only during one specific stage of the cell cycle.
  • The replicated chromosomes (DNA) are distributed to daughter nuclei by a complex series of events during cell division — these events are under genetic control.

03Phases of the Cell Cycle (10.1.1)

Duration

  • A typical eukaryotic cell cycle is illustrated by human cells in culture — dividing once in approximately every 24 hours.
  • Duration varies from organism to organism and cell type to cell type.
  • Yeast can progress through the cell cycle in only about 90 minutes.

Two Basic Phases

  • Interphase — the phase between two successive M phases.
  • M Phase (Mitosis phase) — the phase when actual cell division or mitosis occurs.
  • In the 24-hour cycle of a human cell, cell division proper lasts only about one hour; interphase lasts more than 95% of the cycle.
  • M phase starts with nuclear division — separation of daughter chromosomes (karyokinesis) — and usually ends with division of cytoplasm (cytokinesis).
  • Interphase is called the resting phase — yet the cell is preparing for division by undergoing cell growth and DNA replication in an orderly manner.

04Quiescent Stage (G0) & Who Divides

G0

  • Some adult animal cells do not appear to divide — e.g., heart cells; many divide only occasionally, to replace cells lost due to injury or cell death.
  • Cells that do not divide further exit G1 to enter an inactive stage — the quiescent stage (G0).
  • Cells in G0 remain metabolically active but no longer proliferate unless called on to do so by the organism's requirement.

Mitosis in Haploid vs Diploid Cells

  • In animals, mitotic division is seen only in diploid somatic cells — few exceptions where haploid cells divide by mitosis, e.g., male honey bees.
  • Plants can show mitotic divisions in both haploid and diploid cells.
  • Questions in text: plants and animals grow all their lives due to meristematic tissues (see significance of mitosis).

05M Phase — Overview

Features

  • The most dramatic period of the cell cycle — a major reorganisation of virtually all components of the cell.
  • Since chromosome number in parent and progeny cells is the same, it is called equational division.
  • For convenience mitosis is divided into four stages of nuclear division (karyokinesis): Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase.
  • Cell division is a progressive process — very clear-cut lines cannot be drawn between stages.

06Prophase

Prophase

  • The first stage of karyokinesis; follows the S and G2 phases of interphase.
  • In S and G2 the new DNA molecules are not distinct but intertwined.
  • Marked by the initiation of condensation of chromosomal material; the material becomes untangled during chromatin condensation.
  • The centrosome — duplicated during S phase — begins to move towards opposite poles.

Characteristic Events

  • Chromosomal material condenses to form compact mitotic chromosomes; each is composed of two chromatids attached together at the centromere.
  • Each centrosome radiates out microtubules called asters; the two asters together with spindle fibres form the mitotic apparatus.
  • At the end of prophase the cell does not show golgi complexes, endoplasmic reticulum, nucleolus and the nuclear envelope.

07Metaphase

Metaphase

  • Complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope marks the start; chromosomes spread through the cytoplasm.
  • Condensation is completed; chromosomes are clearly observable — the stage at which morphology of chromosomes is most easily studied.
  • Each metaphase chromosome = two sister chromatids held together by the centromere.
  • Small disc-shaped structures at the surface of the centromeres = kinetochores — sites of attachment of spindle fibres to the chromosomes.
  • All chromosomes come to lie at the equator: one chromatid of each connected by its kinetochore to spindle fibres from one pole, its sister chromatid to fibres from the opposite pole.
  • The plane of alignment = the metaphase plate.
Key Features of Metaphase
  • Spindle fibres attach to kinetochores of chromosomes.
  • Chromosomes are moved to the spindle equator and aligned along the metaphase plate through spindle fibres to both poles.

08Anaphase

Anaphase

  • At the onset, each chromosome at the metaphase plate splits simultaneously; the two daughter chromatids — now called daughter chromosomes of future daughter nuclei — migrate to opposite poles.
  • As each chromosome moves away from the equatorial plate, the centromere is directed towards the pole (leading edge) while the arms trail behind.
Key Events of Anaphase
  • Centromeres split and chromatids separate.
  • Chromatids move to opposite poles.

09Telophase

Telophase

  • The final stage of karyokinesis; chromosomes that reached the poles decondense and lose their individuality; each set of chromatin collects at one of the two poles.
Key Events of Telophase
  • Chromosomes cluster at opposite spindle poles; their identity is lost as discrete elements.
  • Nuclear envelope develops around the chromosome clusters at each pole forming two daughter nuclei.
  • Nucleolus, golgi complex and ER reform.

10Cytokinesis

Cytokinesis

  • Mitosis accomplishes segregation of duplicated chromosomes into daughter nuclei (karyokinesis) and division of the cell into two daughter cells by separation of cytoplasm = cytokinesis; cell division is complete at its end.
  • Animal cell: a furrow in the plasma membrane appears, gradually deepens and joins in the centre, dividing the cytoplasm into two.
  • Plant cell: enclosed by a relatively inextensible cell wall, so a different mechanism — wall formation starts in the centre and grows outward to meet the existing lateral walls.
  • New wall begins with a simple precursor, the cell-plate, which represents the middle lamella between the walls of two adjacent cells.
  • At cytoplasmic division, mitochondria and plastids get distributed between the two daughter cells.
  • In some organisms karyokinesis is not followed by cytokinesis → multinucleate condition → syncytium, e.g., liquid endosperm in coconut.

11Significance of Mitosis (10.3)

Significance

  • Mitosis (the equational division) is usually restricted to diploid cells only; however, in some lower plants and some social insects haploid cells also divide by mitosis.
  • Usually results in diploid daughter cells with identical genetic complement.
  • Growth of multicellular organisms is due to mitosis.
  • Cell growth disturbs the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio; division restores it.
  • Cell repair — a very significant contribution: cells of the upper layer of the epidermis, lining of the gut and blood cells are constantly replaced.
  • Mitotic divisions in meristematic tissues — apical and lateral cambium — result in continuous growth of plants throughout life.

12Meiosis — Definition & Key Features

What is Meiosis?

  • Sexual reproduction involves fusion of two gametes, each with a complete haploid set; gametes are formed from specialised diploid cells.
  • The specialised cell division that reduces the chromosome number by half producing haploid daughter cells = meiosis (the reduction division).
  • Meiosis ensures the haploid phase in the life cycle; fertilisation restores the diploid phase.
  • Occurs during gametogenesis in plants and animals → haploid gametes.
Key Features of Meiosis
  • Two sequential cycles of nuclear and cell division — meiosis I and meiosis II — but only a single cycle of DNA replication.
  • Meiosis I is initiated after parental chromosomes have replicated to produce identical sister chromatids at S phase.
  • Involves pairing of homologous chromosomes and recombination between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
  • Four haploid cells are formed at the end of meiosis II.

  • The stage between the two meiotic divisions = interkinesis; generally short-lived.

  • Followed by prophase II — a much simpler prophase than prophase I.
  • Summary: each pole receives half the chromosome number of the parent cell.

15Meiosis II (10.4.2)

Meiosis II

  • In contrast to meiosis I, meiosis II resembles a normal mitosis.
  • Prophase II — initiated immediately after cytokinesis, usually before chromosomes have fully elongated; nuclear membrane disappears by the end; chromosomes again become compact.
  • Metaphase II — chromosomes align at the equator; microtubules from opposite poles attach to kinetochores of sister chromatids.
  • Anaphase II — begins with simultaneous splitting of the centromere of each chromosome (holding sister chromatids together), letting them move to opposite poles by shortening of microtubules attached to kinetochores.
  • Telophase II — two groups of chromosomes again enclosed by a nuclear envelope; cytokinesis follows forming a tetrad of cells — four haploid daughter cells.

16Significance of Meiosis (10.5)

Significance

  • The mechanism by which conservation of the specific chromosome number of each species across generations is achieved in sexually reproducing organisms — even though the process paradoxically reduces the chromosome number by half.
  • Also increases the genetic variability in the population from one generation to the next.
  • Variations are very important for the process of evolution.

02How to Analyse Chemical Composition? (9.1)

  • Grind a living tissue (vegetable, liver, etc.) in trichloroacetic acid (Cl3CCOOH) using a mortar and pestle → a thick slurry.

  • One extracts the compounds, subjects the extract to separation techniques till a compound is separated from all others — i.e., isolates and purifies it.
  • Analytical techniques then give the molecular formula and probable structure.
  • All the carbon compounds obtained from living tissues are called 'biomolecules'.

03Amino Acids

Structure

  • Organic compounds with an amino group and an acidic group as substituents on the same carbon — the α-carbon → α-amino acids.
  • They are substituted methanes: four substituent groups occupy the four valency positions — hydrogen, carboxyl group, amino group and a variable R group.
  • Based on the nature of the R group there are many amino acids; those occurring in proteins are only twenty types.
  • R group examples: hydrogen → glycine; methyl → alanine; hydroxy methyl → serine (three of the twenty shown in Fig. 9.1).

Classification & Properties

  • Chemical and physical properties are essentially those of the amino, carboxyl and R functional groups.
  • Based on number of amino and carboxyl groups: acidic (glutamic acid), basic (lysine), neutral (valine).
  • Aromatic amino acids: tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan.
  • A particular property: the ionizable nature of –NH2 and –COOH groups — so in solutions of different pH the structure changes; form B is the zwitterionic form.

04Lipids — Fatty Acids, Glycerol, Fats & Phospholipids

Fatty Acids & Glycerol

  • Lipids are generally water insoluble; they could be simple fatty acids.
  • A fatty acid has a carboxyl group attached to an R group; R could be methyl (–CH3), ethyl (–C2H5) or higher number of –CH2– groups (1 carbon to 19 carbons).
  • Palmitic acid — 16 carbons including carboxyl carbon; Arachidonic acid — 20 carbon atoms including the carboxyl carbon.
  • Fatty acids may be saturated (without double bond) or unsaturated (one or more C=C double bonds).
  • Glycerol — another simple lipid; trihydroxy propane.

Glycerides, Fats & Oils

  • Many lipids have both glycerol and fatty acids — the fatty acids are found esterified with glycerol → monoglycerides, diglycerides and triglycerides.
  • Also called fats and oils based on melting point; oils have lower melting point (e.g., gingelly oil) and hence remain as oil in winters.
  • Summary: fats and oils are glycerides in which fatty acids are esterified to glycerol.

Phospholipids

  • Some lipids have phosphorous and a phosphorylated organic compound — phospholipids; found in the cell membrane.
  • Example: Lecithin.
  • Some tissues — especially neural tissues — have lipids with more complex structures.
  • Summary: phospholipids contain, in addition, a phosphorylated nitrogenous compound.

05Nitrogen Bases, Nucleosides & Nucleotides

Heterocyclic Compounds

  • Living organisms have carbon compounds with heterocyclic rings; some are nitrogen bases — adenine, guanine, cytosine, uracil and thymine.
  • Nucleoside — base attached to a sugar.
  • Nucleotide — a nucleoside with a phosphate group esterified to the sugar.
  • Nucleosides: adenosine, guanosine, thymidine, uridine, cytidine.

Primary Metabolites

  • A list of biomolecules would have thousands of organic compounds including amino acids, sugars, etc.; these can be called metabolites.

07Biomacromolecules (9.3)

Micro- vs Macromolecules

  • All compounds in the acid-soluble pool have molecular weights from about 18 to around 800 daltons (Da).
  • The acid-insoluble fraction has only four types of organic compounds: proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides and lipids.
  • Except lipids, these have molecular weights of ten thousand daltons and above.
  • Micromolecules / biomolecules — molecular weight less than one thousand dalton; macromolecules / biomacromolecules — found in the acid-insoluble fraction.
  • The insoluble-fraction molecules except lipids are polymeric substances.

Why Lipids Appear in the Macromolecular Fraction

  • Lipids have molecular weights not exceeding 800 Da — not strictly macromolecules.
  • They are arranged into structures like the cell membrane and other membranes.
  • Grinding disrupts cell structure; membranes break into pieces and form vesicles which are not water soluble — so they separate with the acid-insoluble pool.

Composition

  • The acid-soluble pool represents roughly the cytoplasmic composition; macromolecules from cytoplasm and organelles form the acid-insoluble fraction; together they represent the entire chemical composition of living tissues.
  • Water is the most abundant chemical in living organisms (Table 9.4).

08Proteins (9.4)

Proteins

  • Proteins are polypeptides — linear chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds.
  • As there are 20 types of amino acids (e.g., alanine, cysteine, proline, tryptophan, lysine), a protein is a heteropolymer — not a homopolymer (which has only one type of monomer repeating 'n' times).
  • Amino acids can be essential — must be supplied through the diet (dietary proteins are the source) — or non-essential — those our body can make.

Functions & Records

  • Proteins: transport nutrients across cell membrane; fight infectious organisms; some are hormones; some are enzymes.
  • Collagen — the most abundant protein in the animal world.
  • Ribulose bisphosphate Carboxylase-Oxygenase (RuBisCO) — the most abundant protein in the whole biosphere.

09Polysaccharides (9.5)

Polysaccharides

  • Another class of macromolecules in the acid-insoluble pellet; long chains of sugars — 'threads (literally a cotton thread)' of different monosaccharide building blocks.
  • Cellulose — polymeric polysaccharide of only one type of monosaccharide, glucose → a homopolymer.
  • Starch — a variant, the store house of energy in plant tissues.
  • Glycogen — the variant in animals.
  • Inulin — a polymer of fructose.
  • In a polysaccharide chain (say glycogen): right end = reducing end; left end = non-reducing end; it has branches (Fig. 9.2).

Starch–Iodine & Cellulose

  • Starch forms helical secondary structures and can hold I2 molecules in the helical portion — starch–I2 is blue in colour.
  • Cellulose does not contain complex helices and hence cannot hold I2.
  • Plant cell walls are made of cellulose; paper from plant pulp and cotton fibre are cellulosic.

Complex Polysaccharides

  • Building blocks include amino-sugars and chemically modified sugars — e.g., glucosamine, N-acetyl galactosamine.
  • Chitin — complex polysaccharide in the exoskeletons of arthropods.
  • These complex polysaccharides are mostly homopolymers.
  • Summary: polysaccharides are components of the cell wall in plants, fungi and of the exoskeleton of arthropods; also storage forms of energy (starch, glycogen).

10Nucleic Acids (9.6)

Nucleic Acids

  • The other macromolecule in the acid-insoluble fraction; polynucleotides.
  • Together with polysaccharides and polypeptides they comprise the true macromolecular fraction of any living tissue or cell.
  • Building block = nucleotide, which has three chemically distinct components: a heterocyclic compound, a monosaccharide and a phosphoric acid / phosphate.
  • Nitrogenous bases: adenine, guanine, uracil, cytosine and thymine; adenine and guanine are substituted purines; the rest are substituted pyrimidines; the skeletal heterocyclic rings are called purine and pyrimidine.
  • Sugar: either ribose (a monosaccharide pentose) or 2' deoxyribose.
  • Contains deoxyribose → DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid); contains ribose → RNA (ribonucleic acid).
  • Summary: there are 5 types of nucleotides; nucleic acids carry hereditary information passed from parental generation to progeny.

11Structure of Proteins (9.7)

Four Levels of Protein Structure

  • In inorganic chemistry structure = molecular formula (NaCl, MgCl2); organic chemists write 2-D views (benzene, naphthalene); physicists conjure 3-D views; biologists describe protein structure at four levels.
  • Primary structure — the sequence of amino acids (positional information: first, second, …); the protein is imagined as a line — left end = first amino acid = N-terminal amino acid; right end = last amino acid = C-terminal amino acid.
  • Secondary structure — the thread is folded in the form of a helix (like a revolving staircase) in some portions — only right-handed helices are observed in proteins; other regions fold into other forms.
  • Tertiary structure — the long chain folded upon itself like a hollow woolen ball → 3-dimensional view; absolutely necessary for many biological activities of proteins.
  • Quaternary structure — some proteins are an assembly of more than one polypeptide / subunits; the manner in which folded polypeptides are arranged relative to each other (linear string of spheres, spheres one upon another in a cube or plate, etc.) = the architecture of the protein.

Haemoglobin

  • Adult human haemoglobin (Hb) consists of 4 subunits — two α type and two β type; two are identical to each other.

12Enzymes — Nature & Properties

What are Enzymes?

  • Almost all enzymes are proteins.
  • Some nucleic acids behave like enzymes — called ribozymes.
  • An enzyme, like any protein, has primary, secondary and tertiary structure.
  • In the tertiary structure the chain criss-crosses, making many crevices or pockets; one such pocket is the active site — a crevice into which the substrate fits.
  • Enzymes, through the active site, catalyse reactions at a high rate.

Enzymes vs Inorganic Catalysts

  • Inorganic catalysts work efficiently at high temperatures and high pressures.
  • Enzymes get damaged at high temperatures (say above 40°C).
  • Enzymes from thermophilic organisms (hot vents, sulphur springs) are stable and retain catalytic power up to 80°–90°C — thermal stability is an important quality.

13Chemical Reactions & Rate (9.8.1)

Physical vs Chemical Change

  • Physical change — change in shape without breaking of bonds; change in state of matter (ice → water → vapour) is also a physical process.
  • Chemical reaction — bonds are broken and new bonds formed.
  • Inorganic example: Ba(OH)2 + H2SO4 → BaSO4 + 2H2O; organic example: hydrolysis of starch into glucose.

Rate

  • Rate = amount of product formed per unit time = δP/δt; called velocity if the direction is specified.
  • Rates are influenced by temperature among other factors; rule of thumb: the rate doubles or decreases by half for every 10°C change in either direction.
  • Catalysed reactions proceed at rates vastly higher than uncatalysed ones.

Carbonic Anhydrase Example

  • CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 (carbonic acid), catalysed by carbonic anhydrase (present within the cytoplasm).
  • Without enzyme: about 200 molecules of H2CO3 per hour; with the enzyme: about 600,000 molecules per second.
  • The enzyme accelerates the reaction by about 10 million times.

Metabolic Pathway

  • A multistep chemical reaction, each step catalysed by the same enzyme complex or different enzymes = metabolic pathway.
  • Example: Glucose → 2 Pyruvic acid (C6H12O6 + O2 → 2C3H4O3 + 2H2O) through ten enzyme-catalysed reactions (studied in Chapter 12).
  • Different conditions give different end products: skeletal muscle under anaerobic conditions → lactic acid; normal aerobic conditions → pyruvic acid; yeast fermentation → ethanol (alcohol).

14How Enzymes Work (9.8.2 & 9.8.3)

Substrate, ES Complex, Transition State

  • The chemical converted into a product = the substrate (S); enzymes convert S → P.
  • The substrate binds the enzyme at its active site within a cleft/pocket; there is an obligatory formation of an 'ES' complex — a transient phenomenon.
  • While bound, a new structure called the transition state structure is formed; after bond breaking/making the product is released.
  • All other intermediate structural states are unstable; stability relates to the energy status of the structure.

Energy Profile & Activation Energy (Fig. 9.4)

  • y-axis = potential energy content; x-axis = progression through the transition state.
  • If P is at a lower level than S → exothermic reaction — no energy need be supplied (by heating) to form product (spontaneous); otherwise endothermic (energy requiring).
  • In either case S must pass through a much higher energy state — the transition state.
  • The difference in average energy content of S from that of the transition state = activation energy.
  • Enzymes lower the energy barrier (activation energy), making the transition of S to P easier.

Nature of Enzyme Action

  • E + S ⇌ ES → EP → E + P — highly reactive, short-lived ES complex; formation of the ES complex is essential for catalysis.
The Catalytic Cycle — Four Steps
StepEvent
1Substrate binds to the active site of the enzyme, fitting into it
2Binding induces the enzyme to alter its shape, fitting more tightly around the substrate
3Active site, in close proximity, breaks the chemical bonds of the substrate; new enzyme–product complex formed
4Enzyme releases the products; free enzyme ready to bind another substrate molecule and run the cycle again

15Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity (9.8.4)

General

  • Activity can be affected by changes that alter the tertiary structure: temperature, pH, change in substrate concentration, binding of specific chemicals that regulate its activity.

Temperature and pH

  • Enzymes generally function in a narrow range of temperature and pH.
  • Each enzyme shows highest activity at a particular temperature and pH = optimum temperature and optimum pH; activity declines both below and above the optimum.
  • Low temperature preserves the enzyme in a temporarily inactive state; high temperature destroys activity because proteins are denatured by heat.

Concentration of Substrate

  • With increasing substrate concentration the velocity rises at first, then reaches a maximum velocity (Vmax) which is not exceeded by any further rise in substrate.
  • Reason: enzyme molecules are fewer than substrate molecules; after saturation there are no free enzyme molecules to bind additional substrate.
  • (Fig. 9.5c marks Vmax, Vmax/2 and Km.)

Inhibition

  • Binding of a chemical that shuts off enzyme activity = inhibition; the chemical = inhibitor.
  • Competitive inhibitor — closely resembles the substrate in molecular structure; competes with the substrate for the substrate-binding site; the substrate cannot bind and enzyme action declines.
  • Example: inhibition of succinic dehydrogenase by malonate, which closely resembles the substrate succinate.
  • Such competitive inhibitors are often used in the control of bacterial pathogens.

17Co-factors (9.8.6)

Co-factors & Apoenzyme

  • Enzymes are composed of one or several polypeptide chains; in many cases non-protein constituents called co-factors bind to the enzyme to make it catalytically active.
  • The protein portion of such enzymes = apoenzyme.
  • Three kinds of cofactors: prosthetic groups, co-enzymes and metal ions.
  • Catalytic activity is lost when the co-factor is removed — testifying to their crucial role.

Exam Traps — Biomolecules
  • Living matter vs earth's crust: same elements; carbon & hydrogen relatively higher in living tissue (C 18.5% vs 0.03%; H 9.5% vs 0.14%).
  • Acid-soluble pool = filtrate (MW 18–800 Da, cytoplasmic composition); acid-insoluble = retentate (proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides, lipids). Lipids are not true macromolecules.
  • Water is the most abundant chemical (70–90%); then proteins (10–15%).
  • Collagen — most abundant protein in animals; RuBisCO — most abundant in the biosphere.
  • Cellulose — homopolymer of glucose; inulin — fructose; starch–I2 = blue; chitin — arthropod exoskeleton.
  • Adenine and guanine = purines; cytosine, uracil, thymine = pyrimidines. Ribose vs 2' deoxyribose.
  • Proteins: only right-handed helices; tertiary structure essential for activity; Hb = 2α + 2β.
  • Enzymes: almost all proteins; ribozymes are nucleic acids; damaged above ~40°C (except thermophiles up to 80–90°C).
  • Competitive inhibition: malonate vs succinate (succinic dehydrogenase).
  • Prosthetic group — tightly bound (haem); co-enzyme — transient (NAD/NADP; niacin); metal ion — Zn in carboxypeptidase.

NCERT Summary — Key Points

  • Despite bewildering diversity, chemical composition and metabolic reactions are remarkably similar; relative abundance of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen is higher in living systems than in inanimate matter.
  • Thousands of small molecular weight (< 1000 Da) biomolecules; there are 20 types of amino acids and 5 types of nucleotides.
  • Only three types of macromolecules — proteins, nucleic acids and polysaccharides — exist in living systems; lipids separate in the macromolecular fraction due to membrane association.
  • Biomacromolecules are polymers with different building blocks; they have a hierarchy of structures — primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary.
  • Proteins serve varied functions: enzymes, antibodies, receptors, hormones, structural proteins.
  • Enzymes: substrate specificity, optimum temperature and pH; denatured at high temperatures; lower activation energy and greatly enhance reaction rate.
