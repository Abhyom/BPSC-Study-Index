01 Discovery & Nomenclature

Draws on the intro plus Q2, Q7–Q11, Q40, Q59–Q64, Q80.

The Indus Valley Civilization is also known as the Harappa Civilization. Archaeologists use the term "culture" for a group of objects, distinctive in style, usually found together within a specific geographical area and period. In the Harappan case, these distinctive objects include seals, beads, weights, stone blades and baked bricks — the Harappan seal is possibly the civilization's single most distinctive artefact.

John Marshall, Director-General of the ASI, announced the discovery of a new civilization in the Indus Valley to the world in 1921. Marshall served as DG-ASI from 1902–1928, and appointed Dayaram Sahni, whose excavation on the left bank of the Ravi led to the Harappa findings in 1921; Rakhal Das Banerjee (R.D. Banerjee) dug out Mohenjodaro's ruins in 1922.

The vast mounds at Harappa were first reported by Charles Masson in 1826. The first archaeologist to excavate Harappa but fail to recognise its significance was Alexander Cunningham, who visited in 1853 and 1856 AD, making small excavations and noting pottery, stone tools and a seal without grasping their importance.

Excavation timeline at Harappa & Mohenjodaro

  • January 1921 — Dayaram Sahni surveys Harappa (Montgomery/Sahiwal district, Punjab); excavation begins continuously.
  • 1922 — R.D. Banerjee excavates Mohenjodaro (Larkana district, Sindh).
  • 1926–27 to 1933–34 — Madho Sarup Vats excavates Harappa.
  • 1946 — Mortimer Wheeler excavates Harappa.

Also contributing to discovery: K.N. Dikshit, Ernest Mackay, Aurel Stein, A.N. Ghosh, J.P. Joshi. V.A. Smith was NOT associated with the Harappan discovery — he was an Irish Indologist/historian, a recurring MCQ trap (Q63).

The first artefact uncovered at Harappa was a unique stone seal carved with a unicorn and an inscription. Although the Harappan script remains undeciphered, evidence from archaeological excavations (seals, terracotta, utensils, jewellery, weapons, tools) is the major/main source of knowledge about Harappan culture — NOT rock edicts, religious books, or (per the dedicated MCQ on inhabitants) scripts alone; over 3,000 seals have been found.

⚠ Trap — origin-of-civilization matching (Q10)

E.J.H. Mackay → migration of people from Sumer. Mortimer Wheeler (with D.H. Gordon) → migration of the idea of civilization from Western Asia. Amalananda Ghosh → pre-Harappan culture matured into Harappan civilization. M. Rafique Mughal is WRONGLY paired with "inspired by Mesopotamia" — he actually argued the opposite: that Harappan civilization developed independently in the Ravi region at Harappa and refuted Mesopotamian-inspiration theories.

Mohenjodaro is a Sindhi-language term meaning "Mound of the Dead." A recurring excavator-matching MCQ (Q11) pairs: Harappa → Daya Ram Sahni (1923–25); Hastinapur → B.B. Lal (1950–52); Takshashila → John Marshall (1913–34); Kaushambi → N.G. Majumdar (1936–37).

Separately (Q61): Surkotada was excavated by J.P. Joshi (confirmed again in Q64), NOT by B.K. Thapar; Dholavira was excavated by R.S. Bisht, not Thapar — a recurring MCQ trap.

02 Chronology & Dating

Draws on the intro plus Q3, Q66, Q91, Q95–Q96.

Carbon-14 (C14) dating places the Harappan period at 2600 BC to 1700 BC — the most widely accepted range. The civilization's growth peaked between 2300 BC and 1900 BC. Mesopotamian and Egyptian civilizations are contemporary to the Indus civilization.

Indus civilization existed in the Proto-historic age (Q3) — writing existed but remains undeciphered, distinguishing it from both prehistoric (no writing) and historic (deciphered writing) ages.

Chronological order of key Indian developments (Q66)

  1. Urban culture — Harappan Civilization, Bronze Age
  2. Iron plough — known from post-Vedic period, ~1000 BC
  3. Punch-marked silver coins — 7th–6th century BC
  4. Gold coins — introduced by Indo-Greek rulers, 2nd century BC

Per an IIT Kharagpur study, continuous low/negligible rainfall for 900 years is cited as a cause of the civilization's fall (see also Decline Theories, Section 13). Mesopotamia (Iraq) is generally considered the world's oldest civilization, with Egyptian civilization emerging after it.

03 Geographical Extent

Draws on the intro plus Q35, Q56, Q70.

The Harappan culture covered parts of Afghanistan, Punjab, Sindh, Baluchistan (Pakistan) and, in India, Gujarat, Rajasthan, Haryana, the fringes of western Uttar Pradesh, J&K, and western Maharashtra. It extended from Manda (J&K) in the north to Daimabad (Maharashtra) in the south, and from Sutkagen Dor (Baluchistan) in the west to Alamgirpur (Meerut, UP) in the east — confirmed separately (Q35) as: eastern boundary = Alamgirpur; western = Sutkagendor; northern = Manda; southern = Daimabad.

The area formed a rough triangle spanning about 13 lakh square kilometres — larger than Pakistan and bigger than ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia.

Geographically, the civilization straddled the rivers Sindhu (Indus), Chenab and Jhelum (Q56) — spreading from the Jhelum in the north to the Hindon river (a Yamuna tributary) at Alamgirpur in the south; the Ganga was NOT part of this river system.

Banawali (Fatehabad, Haryana) is located in the valley of the Ghaggar and its tributaries (Q70) — as is Kalibangan.

Population

Evidence suggests the population of Mohenjodaro was a mixture of at least four races: Proto-Australoids (Caucasian), Mediterraneans, Alpines and Mongoloids.

Theorised "founders" of the Indus Valley Civilization

  • Dr. Laxman Swaroop — Arya (Aryans).
  • V. Gordon Childe and Wheeler — Sumerians.
  • Rakhal Das Banerji — Dravidians (Dravid).

Excavated Indus Valley cities fall into three categories: Central cities, Coastal cities and dockyards, and Other cities and towns. The four major cities/large towns were Harappa, Mohenjodaro, Banawali and Dholavira.

04 Site Gazetteer — Punjab & Sindh (Pakistan)

Draws on the intro plus Q16, Q24–Q26, Q38–Q41, Q45, Q68–Q69, Q73.

Harappa

Located in the Montgomery district (now Sahiwal district), Punjab province, Pakistan, on the left bank of the Ravi River. Per Stuart Piggott, Harappa and Mohenjodaro were "Twin capitals of one United Kingdom." Harappa had two mounds — the eastern city mound and the western Citadel mound. Its Granary comprised 12 rooms in two rows of six.

Mohenjodaro

Located on the right bank of the Indus, Larkana district, Sindh, Pakistan. Discovered by R.D. Banerjee in 1922. Its most important public place is the Great Bath — used for ritual bathing (Q24–Q25). Northeast of the Great Bath stands a large ruined building, 70.1 × 23.77 m, perhaps the residence of a high priest or a college of priests.

Mohenjodaro's main quality was its roads: the main road, 9.15 m wide, is called "Rajpath." Straight roads intersected at right angles, forming square/quadrangle blocks. Key artefacts: mother goddess idol, Dancing Girl statue, seated male soapstone Priest-King, Pashupati (Shiva) seal, well skeletons, horse teeth, cotton cloth evidence, and the famous humped bull seal (Q85).

Chanhudaro

About 130 km south of Mohenjodaro; discovered by N.G. Majumdar in 1931, excavated on a large scale by E.J.H. Mackay in 1935–36 (Q40). Remnants of the Jhukar and Jhangar cultures found here; likely an industrial hub for shell-making, bangle-making and bead-making, with bead-making furnaces found by Mackay. Finds: ornamented elephant, toys, a dog-following-cat figurine, and lipsticks. Chanhudaro has NO rock-cut architecture — that distinction belongs to Dholavira (Q73).

Ropar

Located on the left bank of the Satluj, now called Rupnagar, Punjab. Discovered by B.B. Lal in 1950; excavated by Yagyadatt Sharma in 1953–55.

⚠ Trap — sites confused with Sindh (Q16, Q38–Q39)

Not a Harappan site: Sohgaura (Rapti river, Gorakhpur, UP) — this is a Mauryan-period copper-plate site about storehouses, not Harappan; Chanhudaro, Kotdiji and Desalpur ARE genuine Harappan sites (Chanhudaro/Kotdiji in Sindh; Desalpur in Kachchh, Gujarat). Among Harappa/Mohenjodaro/Chanhudaro/Surkotada, only Mohenjodaro and Chanhudaro are actually in Sindh — Harappa is in Punjab and Surkotada is in Gujarat.

05 Site Gazetteer — Gujarat

Draws on the intro plus Q17, Q30–Q32, Q42, Q44–Q49, Q68, Q73–Q78.

Lothal

Situated on the Bhogava River in Ahmedabad district, Gujarat; discovered by S.R. Rao in 1954. Along its eastern side, a brick basin has been identified by its excavator as a dockyard — Lothal is the only Indus site with clear port/dockyard evidence, situated near the Gulf of Khambhat/Cambay for trade with Western Asia. On planning and material goods, it resembles a "mini Harappa" or "mini Mohenjodaro."

Evidence found at Lothal: rice husk, metal-workers, shell-ornament makers, bead-makers, a shop, fire altars, a terracotta horse figurine, a terracotta ship model, a Persian seal, painted jars, and an ivory scale (Q68). Three double burials have also been found here (Q79).

Dholavira

The second-largest Harappan site in India (after Rakhigarhi), located in the Rann of Kachchh. Excavated by R.S. Bisht and colleagues in 1990–91. Divided into three parts — Citadel, Middle Town and Lower Town (Q75–Q76). Famous for a sophisticated water management system: a series of dams channelling water into connected reservoirs, including a rock-cut reservoir about 79 m × 12 m, 7 m deep (Q77–Q78).

Surkotada, Rangpur, Padri, Desalpur

Surkotada (Kachchh district) has yielded evidence of horse bones and pot burials; discovered/excavated by J.P. Joshi. Rangpur lies in the Saurashtra region on the Bhadar river; vegetative remains suggest cultivation of Rice, Bajra and Sorghum. Padri is also a Gujarat Harappan site (Q37 matching set). Desalpur, in Kachchh, is a genuine Harappan site (cross-referenced in the Q16 trap above).

⚠ Trap — Kalibangan is NOT in Gujarat or Haryana

A recurring MCQ (Q30) wrongly lists Kalibangan–Haryana; Kalibangan is in Hanumangarh district, Rajasthan. Discovered by Italian Indologist Luigi Pio Tessitory; excavated by A. Ghosh (1950–52) and by B.B. Lal & B.K. Thapar starting 1960–61 per the intro.

06 Site Gazetteer — Rajasthan & Haryana

Draws on the intro plus Q18–Q19, Q30, Q33–Q34, Q36–B37, Q54–Q55, Q92.

Kalibangan

Located in Hanumangarh district, Rajasthan, on the bank of the Ghaggar (Saraswati) River. Excavation started by B.B. Lal & B.K. Thapar in 1960–61. Kalibangan has given the earliest evidence of a ploughed agricultural field discovered anywhere in the civilization (Q18–Q19), plus Mesopotamian seals, toy-cart wheels, and fire altars. A triangular terracotta cake with a carved horned deity on one side and a goat being dragged on the other was also recovered (Q92), pointing to an early sacrificial tradition.

Rakhigarhi

Situated in Hisar district, Haryana, in the Ghaggar-Hakra river plain. This is the largest Indus Valley site in India (Q54–Q55).

Banawali & Balu

Banawali (Fatehabad district, Haryana) has yielded clay figures of the mother goddess and a terracotta replica of a plough (see matching ledger, Section 8). Balu is also located in Haryana (Q37 matching set).

Bhagwanpura

Located in Kurukshetra district, Haryana, on the southern bank of the river Saraswati; excavated by J.P. Joshi.

07 Site Gazetteer — UP, J&K & Maharashtra

Draws on the intro plus Q31, Q33, Q80–Q81.

Alamgirpur & Hulas (Uttar Pradesh)

Alamgirpur is in Meerut district, UP, on the Hindon River — marking the civilization's eastern boundary. A gold-coated terracotta bead-like structure and cloth evidence were found here, plus utensils with triangle, peacock and squirrel impressions. Hulas is in Saharanpur district, UP: hand-made and wheel-made pottery with black geometric/naturalistic designs, chert blades, bone points. Per Q31, of Kalibangan/Lothal/Alamgirpur/Hulas, only Alamgirpur and Hulas lie in Uttar Pradesh (Kalibangan is Rajasthan, Lothal is Gujarat).

Mandi & Sinauli (Uttar Pradesh)

Mandi is in Muzaffarnagar district, UP — famous for the 2000 discovery (and looting) of a hoard of late-Harappan gold jewellery by villagers (Q33, Q81). Sinauli, in Baraut Tehsil, Baghpat district, UP, has yielded 125 graves oriented North–South, mostly primary human burials, some with animal bones, gold, copper and bangles (Q80).

Manda (Jammu & Kashmir)

Situated on the Chenab river; excavated by J.P. Joshi and Madhubala (the source dates this to "1776-77," almost certainly a typo for 1976–77). Considered a site used to procure wood from Himalayan regions and float it downstream to other Indus towns.

Daimabad (Maharashtra)

Situated on the left bank of the Pravara River, Ahmednagar (now Ahilyanagar) district; discovered by B.P. Bopardikar in 1958. Famous for many bronze goods, some Harappan-influenced — and marks the civilization's southern boundary.

Dadheri (Punjab)

Dadheri (Kotla Dadheri), in Govindgarh, Fatehgarh Sahib district, Punjab, is a Late Harappan site known for painted grey ware culture — associated with the arrival of the Aryans.

09 Seals, Sculpture & Terracotta Art

Draws on the intro plus Q15, Q26, Q85–Q90.

Figures of elephant, rhinoceros, tiger, deer and sheep are depicted on Harappan seals and terracotta art — but the cow was NOT depicted (Q15). Similarly, the horse is not depicted on seals despite dispute over whether Harappans knew the animal (Q88–Q90); the lion has no evidence at all from the civilization (Q89).

Clay/model boats have been found from Mohenjodaro (seals with boat motifs) and Lothal (clay model boats) — Q26.

Most Harappan seals were made of steatite, with some in terracotta, agate, and chert; mostly square, with some cube, circle and cylindrical shapes. The Pipal tree is repeatedly depicted on seals, suggesting sacred/important value (Q87).

The Dancing Girl figurine (found at Mohenjo-daro) is made of bronze — cast using the lost-wax technique, about 10.5 cm high (Q86). Two Indian archaeologists are especially associated with the discovery: Rakhal Das Banerjee and Dayaram Sahni (Q60).

10 Pottery, Metallurgy & Measurement

Draws on the intro plus Q13, Q27–Q28, Q50, Q67, Q69.

Harappan pottery is bright or dark red, uniformly sturdy and well-baked — wheel-made, both plain and painted (Q13). Plain wares are usually red clay with or without a fine red slip; black-painted ware has a fine red-slip coating. Polychrome pottery was rare, mostly small vases with geometric patterns in red, black and green, less often white and yellow. The colour scheme for painting was light black, chocolate or light red over a buff/greenish-buff background.

The Indus Valley Civilization was a Bronze Age civilization — people were NOT familiar with iron (Q27, Q69). Copper was the first metal used by humans (Q67), though the exact global period varies. Harappans were familiar with silver — the earliest evidence of silver use in India comes from Harappan culture (Q12), sourced from Afghanistan and Iran besides Indian mines.

⚠ Trap — bricks, houses and road widths (Q28)

NOT true: "same-size bricks used for all constructions" — both baked and raw bricks were used, with a general dimensional ratio of 4:2:1, but ornamental bricks (Kalibangan) and curved bricks (Chanhudaro) also occur. NOT true: "mostly single-storey houses" — some houses had two storeys (stair evidence found). TRUE: main roads averaged about 10 metres in width.

An ivory scale/ruler was found at Lothal (Q68), alongside the Mohenjodaro ruler — divided into units of 34mm with decimal subdivisions accurate to 0.13mm, using the base unit angula (~17mm), predating the modern metric system by 3,000+ years.

⚠ Trap — Gangeyadeva & gold coins (cross-reference)

Gold coins as a category belong to a much later, early-medieval context (Kalachuri king Gangeyadeva first issued gold coins in early-medieval North India — see the Early-Medieval Dossier); within the IVC's own Bronze Age economy, no coinage of any kind existed — trade ran on barter and standardized weights.

11 Economy, Trade & Metal Sources

Draws on the intro plus Q1, Q4–Q6, Q47–Q49, Q82.

Human Society is considered unique because it depends mainly on Economy — as the economy changes, society changes accordingly (Q1). The trade and commerce of the Indus Valley were in an advanced stage, with both internal and external trade evidenced (Q27).

The important reason to date the Indus Valley civilization before Aryan civilization is pottery evidence (Q5) — red potteries painted with black figures were the Harappan speciality, versus grey-painted pottery used by the Aryans. Indus Valley was Non-Aryan chiefly because it was an urban civilization (Q4), whereas Aryan civilization was rural; other distinguishing points (Q6) include a pictographic script and no knowledge of iron or the defensive bow, versus Vedic culture's extensive iron use.

Cotton was first cultivated in India for clothing (Q82) — the earliest cotton-cultivation evidence in the world comes from Indian excavations; from India it reached Greece, where it was called "Sindon." Indian cotton cultivation began around 3000 BC, versus 2500 BC in Egypt.

The most significant feature of the Indus Valley Civilization is not singular — burnt-brick buildings, the first true arches, and well-planned architecture with advanced trade all count as major features (Q50), so exam framings that ask for "the" single most significant feature often accept "more than one of the above."

12 Religion & Belief System

Draws on the intro plus Q14, Q57–Q58, Q83–Q84.

Many pieces of evidence of Mother Goddess worship have been found. John Marshall identified a seal figure as an early form of Shiva (Rudra) or Pashupati (Lord of Animals) from Mohenjodaro — the three-faced seated male god in yogic posture, surrounded by a Rhino and Buffalo on the right and an Elephant and Tiger on the left. This, plus cylindrical stones, led historians to conclude the Harappans worshipped Lord Shiva and the Lingam symbol.

The beginning of idol worship in India is dated to the Pre-Aryan period (Q14) — evident from Mohenjodaro coin/seal finds depicting deities, worshipped alongside male and female figures.

⚠ Trap — no temples, no priest-kings confirmed (Q83)

NOT true of Indus religion: they possessed great palaces and temples, or employed horse-drawn chariots in warfare — no temple or palace has been found in IVC excavations to date. TRUE: they worshipped both male and female deities — the ONLY correct statement of the three options.

Indus Valley Civilization is considered a secular civilization with no trace of theocratic or organized religion (Q84 statement 1 — correct). No temple or place of worship has been found to date; the only religious sources are mud/stone statues and seals. These indicate worship of female deities, Shiva/Pashupati, and animals like snakes and elephants. Worship of plants/trees or inanimate objects as Linga or Yoni symbols was prevalent (Q84 statement 2 — also correct, re: cotton textile manufacture during this period).

On burial and ritual belief (Q57, marked ambiguous "*" in the source): the undeciphered Indus script leaves room for speculation, but the people probably worshipped Mother Goddess and Lord Shiva, performed Yagya (fire-pit evidence in Rajasthan/Gujarat sites), and burial rituals suggest belief in Atma/spirit — with some evidence of human sacrifice noted at Mohenjodaro.

14 World Context: Contemporary Civilizations & Firsts

Draws on the intro plus Q93–Q96.

Ancient Egyptian Civilization flourished along the banks of the River Nile for almost 3,000 years — the Nile, known as the "lifeline of Egypt," is a major north-flowing river in northeastern Africa and generally regarded as the world's longest river (Q93). The Indus Valley Civilization (India) and Mesopotamian civilization (Iraq) are contemporary civilizations to Egypt's.

Mesoamerican & South American civilizations — North-to-South sequence (Q94)

  • Aztec — northern Mesoamerica (central Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, northern Costa Rica).
  • Maya — southern Mesoamerica.
  • Muisca — eastern Colombia, South America.
  • Inca — western South America, from north of Quito to Santiago in the south.

Correct order, North to South: Aztec → Maya → Muisca → Inca.

Sumerian Civilization people were the first to develop the art of writing in a proper system (Q96) — their Cuneiform script is one of the earliest known writing systems, though their original script was simple and primitive.

The oldest civilization in the world is generally considered to be Mesopotamia (Iraq) (Q95), with Egyptian civilization emerging in the Nile basin after it.
