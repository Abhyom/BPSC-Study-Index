Part A
Post-Mauryan Landscape: Shungas & Kanvas

After the Mauryas, power shifts from Magadha to the north-west; native dynasties rule the east and centre.

1

The Post-Mauryan Landscape

Big Picture

  • The post-Mauryan period brought many historical, political and cultural changes, with the political focus shifting from Magadha to North-Western India.
  • In eastern and central India, Mauryan rule was succeeded by native dynasties: Shungas, Kanvas and Satavahanas.
  • In north-western India, Central Asian ruling dynasties took the stage, with the Kushans becoming prominent.
  • Correct order of the foreign invaders: Greeks (Alexander, 326 BC) → Sakas / Scythians (1st century BC) → Kushanas (1st century AD). Wrong orders (Sakas–Kushanas–Greeks, Sakas–Greeks–Kushanas, Greeks–Kushanas–Sakas) are traps.
  • Earlier, the Persian ruler Darius I (522–486 BC) was the first ruler to invade/capture parts of India (not Cyrus, Cambyses or Shaharsh); per Herodotus he conquered the lands around the Indus, the desert of Rajputana and parts of Punjab.

Chronological Order of Dynasties

  • Maurya (c. 321–185 BC) → Shunga (185–73 BC) → Kanva (73–28 BC) → Kushan (1st–3rd century CE). Shunga ruled after Maurya, and Kanva after Shunga. Wrong sequences (Shunga–Maurya, Maurya–Kushana–Shunga) are traps.
  • Among Chalukya, Pallava, Rashtrakuta and Satavahana, the Satavahanas are the earliest. Satavahana (founded by Simuka, 1st century BC) → Vakataka (founded by Vindhyashakti, 3rd century AD) → Chalukya of Gujarat (founded by Mulraj I, 10th century AD).
  • The Maurya empire was the first pan-India empire: at its height it covered parts of modern Iran and nearly the entire subcontinent barring the southern peninsula; the Kushan empire also spread outside India — so both Maurya and Kushan empires extended beyond India.
2

Shunga Dynasty (185–73 BCE)

Pushyamitra Shunga & His Successors

  • Founded by Pushyamitra Shunga, the commander (senapati) of the last Mauryan ruler Brihadratha, whom he killed in 185 BC. The ancestors of the Shungas came from Ujjain.
  • Ruled mainly northern and central India; capital Pataliputra; known for promoting Brahmanical traditions; the Shungas followed the Mauryas.
  • The Ayodhya inscription records that Pushyamitra performed two Ashvamedha yajnas (not the Sarnath, Besnagar or Hathigumpha inscriptions).
  • Shunga-period scholar: Maharshi Patanjali, author of the Mahabhashya — he is not a member of Kanishka's court.
  • Demetrius II of the Indo-Greeks attacked India in 180 BC and probably clashed with Pushyamitra.
  • Last Shunga ruler: Devabhuti, killed by his own minister (amatya) Vasudeva Kanva.
MCQ Trap / Source ConflictDate conflict: the Shunga end is given as 73 BCE in theory and chronology answer, but one source table writes 75 BC. Use 185–73 BCE.
3

Kanva Dynasty (73–28 BCE)

Kanvas

  • Founded by Vasudeva Kanva, who overthrew the Shungas (killing Devabhuti); Brahmin like the Shungas; ruled for a short period.
  • The Kanvas ruled after the Shungas (not Satavahana, Kushana or Gupta) and were replaced by the Satavahanas in Magadha — Simuka founded the Satavahana dynasty after the assassination of the Kanva king Susharman (Puranas); the earliest Satavahana inscriptions come after they defeated the Kanvas.
Part B
Central Asian Contact

Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Parthians and Kushanas — and what they changed in India.

4

The Indo-Greeks

Early Greek Contact with India

  • Greek contact began with Alexander's invasion of north-western India (327–325 BC).
  • Seleucus Nicator (Alexander's general) ruled from Turkey to the Indus; Chandragupta Maurya defeated him and a marriage alliance followed.
  • Bindusara kept relations with Antiochus of Syria; a Seleucid ambassador, Deimachus, was at his court.
  • Ashokan Rock Edict XIII mentions five Yona (Yavana) kings — deep Greek ties.
  • It is argued that Mauryan administrative institutions drew on Persian and Greek systems.
  • Indian exports: ivory, pearls, indigo, aromatics such as spikenard (fragrant oil from the Gangetic region) and malabathrum (cinnamon leaf).

The Indo-Greek Kingdom & Causes of Invasion

  • Also called Indo-Bactrians or the Yavana Kingdom; covered the north-western subcontinent including parts of Afghanistan, Pakistan and Iran; advanced as far as Ayodhya (Saketa) and Pataliputra.
  • Seleucid decline after 250 BC: Antiochus III came down to the Kabul River and defeated the Indian king Subhagasena, paving the way for Greek entry.
  • The Great Wall of China pushed the Scythian tribes toward Greek and Parthian lands, which forced the Bactrian Greeks towards India.

Indo-Greek Coinage

  • Coinage was the distinguishing feature: first to issue coins definitely attributable to kings (portrait of the reigning king on one side with his name); first to issue gold coins widely in India — they popularised gold in the north-west and the Kushanas then spread them.
  • Greek coins were better shaped and stamped than punch-marked coins; their gold was of the highest quality and followed Roman weight standards.
  • Demetrius II: bilingual square coins — Greek on the obverse, Kharosthi on the reverse.
  • Agathocles (Greco-Bactrian, c. 190–180 BC): held Paropamisadae (satrapy between Bactria and India); known mainly through extensive coinage; first Greco-Bactrian with bilingual legends in Greek and Brahmi; his silver coins depict Sankarshana (Balarama) and Vasudeva (not Havishka, Kanishka or Samudragupta).
  • Strato II (25 BC–10 AD) issued lead coins (not Strato I, Demetrius or Menander).
  • Menander's coins: titled king and soter (saviour), with Athena (with wings / holding shield), gold and silver issues, the upholder of Dhammachakra and Buddhist symbols. Sixteen of his coins were found at Bairat (not Nagari, Rairh or Nagar).

Demetrius II (c. 180 BC)

  • Attacked India in 180 BC; probably clashed with Pushyamitra Shunga; extended Bactrian rule south of the Hindu Kush; issued bilingual square coins. Listed as the first known Indo-Greek king in the timeline table. Classified as Indo-Greek in matching questions (Rudradaman = Scythian, Gondophernes = Parthian, Vima = Kushana).

Menander / Milinda (165/145–130 BC)

  • The most important Indo-Greek ruler: ruled from the Kabul and Indus valleys to western Uttar Pradesh; capital Sakala (Sialkot, Pakistan) — not a Satavahana capital.
  • Coins call him king and soter (saviour), not a great conqueror. He embraced Buddhism under Nagasena; the Pali Milinda Panho (Milindapanho) records their dialogue.
  • Conquests: attacked Pataliputra but did not consolidate; the Hathigumpha inscription says Kharavela of Kalinga (Chedi dynasty) failed to stop him.
  • Not a contemporary of Bimbisara (544–492 BC, actual founder of the Magadha empire, Haryanka dynasty), Gautama Buddha or Prasenjit (ruler of the Koshala Mahajanapada) — these three were contemporaries of one another; Milinda (Menander) is the odd one out.

Antialcidas & Heliodorus

  • Antialcidas (c. 130–120 BC): his emissary Heliodorus was sent to the court of Bhagabhadra, where he set up the Garuda-dhvaja pillar at Vidisha (Besnagar), its capital topped by a Garuda figure, in honour of Krishna (Vasudeva); Heliodorus adopted Vaishnavism (called a Bhagavata).
5

Arrival of the Central Asian Tribes

The Great Migrations

  • The Indo-Greeks were ousted by nomads: Sakas (Scythians), Parthians (Pahlavas) and Kushanas (Yueh-chi / Yuezhi in Chinese) from Central Asia — the result of a chain of migrations.
  • The Yueh-chi, in eastern Central Asia, were pushed west when the Chinese built the Great Wall to protect their villages; they pushed the Sakas towards eastern Iran, where the Parthians had risen after the Seleucid collapse.
  • In 58 BC the independent Parthian kingdom of eastern Iran was established by Vonones.
6

The Shakas (Scythians)

Origin, Rulers & Administration

  • Pushed out of eastern Iran by the Parthian king Mithradates (188–123 BC), they turned to north-western India and settled between the Indus valley and Saurashtra.
  • Five branches with separate centres: Afghanistan, Punjab, Mathura, Western India, Upper Deccan.
  • First Shaka ruler in India: Maues (Moa / Moga), who occupied Gandhara; his successor Azes destroyed the Indo-Greek kingdoms and extended Shaka rule to Mathura.
  • Provincial governors (Kshatrapas / Satraps) ran territories, and many became virtually independent. Gold:silver coin ratio in the Shaka-Kshatrapa period = 1 : 35.
  • Karddamaka Kshatrapas: silver coins were frequent; potin coins were the second category; Chastana issued silver and copper; copper coins were the rare ones.

Rudradaman I (130–150 AD)

  • Important Shaka Kshatrapa; governed Sindh, Gujarat, Konkan, Narmada valley, Malwa, Kathiawar. Classified as a Scythian.
  • The Junagadh (Girnar) rock inscription (Gujarat), c. 150 AD, in Brahmi script: the first long inscription in chaste Sanskrit and the earliest specimen of Kavya style (not Ashoka's or Rajendra I's); afterwards inscriptions began to be written in Sanskrit. It lists his various achievements (not Bhitari, Nasik or Sanchi), credits him with supporting cultural arts and Sanskrit literature, and records that he defeated the Satavahanas in battle (Girnar: the defeat of Vasishthiputra Pulumavi).
  • Sudarshana Lake (Kathiawar, irrigation): originally built under Chandragupta Maurya by his western-province governor Pushyagupta; Rudradaman repaired the embankments and dam without forced labour (not Harsha, Kanishka, Bindusara or Ashoka).

Society & Decline

  • Shakas adopted Hindu names and religious beliefs; their coins carried Hindu gods on one side. Patanjali (Mahabhashya) calls them Anirvasita ('pure') Shudras.
  • Decline: the King of Ujjain expelled the Shakas around 57–58 BC and took the title Vikramaditya (first time), beginning the Vikrama Samvat (57 BC).
7

The Parthians (Pahlavas)

Parthians in India

  • Originally from Iran; succeeded the Shaka rule — Indian texts mention both together as Shaka-Pahlavas; their presence in India was limited compared with the Greeks and Shakas.
  • Gondophernes (1st century AD): displaced the Shakas and conquered Kabul in 43 AD, later lost it to the Kushanas. Classified as Parthian in matching questions.
8

The Kushanas

Origin & Expanse

  • One of the five clans of the Yuechi tribe (also called Yuechis or Tocharians); succeeded the Parthians and Scythians. Originating in north Central Asia near China, they occupied Bactria (north Afghanistan) by displacing the Shakas.
  • Empire from the Oxus (Amu Darya) to the Ganges: Khorasan, Iran, Afghanistan, Pakistan and northern India.

First Kushana Dynasty (from c. 50 AD)

  • Kujula (Khujula) Kadphises / Kadphises I: first Kushan king to conquer Afghanistan; issued coins south of the Hindu Kush; circulated copper coins.
  • Vima Kadphises / Kadphises II: son of Kujula, father of Kanishka; first Kushana to issue gold coins — introduced them for regular use and on a large scale (not Kujula, Kanishka, Huvishka or Hermaeus); a devotee of Shiva; spread the kingdom east of the Indus to Mathura with titles like 'Lord of the whole world' (Sarvalokeshvara) — not Sakari, Mahakshatrapa or Simhachandra.
  • The two kings extended territory to Gandhara, Punjab and as far east as the Ganga–Yamuna doab up to Mathura. First capital Purushapura (Peshawar); Mathura was the second capital in India. Early Kushan gold coins contained more gold than Gupta coins.
MCQ Trap / Source ConflictGold-coin 'first': Indo-Greeks made gold coins popular (and issued them widely in the north-west); the first Kushana to issue them was Vima Kadphises. Answer the exam question by the options: 'first on a large scale / regular use in India' = Vima Kadphises; Gold and copper = Kushanas; Satavahanas never issued gold.

Second Kushana Dynasty — Kanishka

  • Founded by Kanishka, son of Kadphises II, the most famous Kushana king, who took Kushan power to upper India and the lower Indus basin; the empire reached its zenith.
  • His empire: northern Turpan (China) and Kashmir to the Vindhya in the south, northern Afghanistan in the west to eastern UP and Bihar; included Varanasi, Kaushambi, Shravasti and Sanchi (Madhya Pradesh), southwards into Malwa. The centre was Bactria — the Bactrian language appears on his coins and inscriptions.
  • Rabatak inscription (found 1993, near Surkh Kotal, Afghanistan; Greek script, Bactrian language): says he adopted the title Devaputra; he wears a peaked helmet on some coins. It names four cities — Saket, Kaushambi, Pataliputra, Sri Champa — and not Shravasti.
  • Coins and his headless statue near Mathura show a belted tunic, overcoat and boots — evidence of Central Asian origin. The Buddha is depicted on Kanishka's coins (not those of Vima Kadphises, Nahapana or Budhagupta).
  • Hunza rock inscription (Karakoram Highway) names the first two Kadphises and Kusanadevaputra Kanishka; it confirms the empire stretched from Central Asia to eastern India. Buddhist sources say he conquered Magadha, Kashmir and Khotan (Sinkiang).
  • Chinese texts: Kanishka (Yue-tche) asked to marry a Han princess, was refused, and marched on the general Pan Chao (the Chinese general who defeated him; 86 AD in one account with 70,000 men; 73–94 AD in another) but lost to a smaller Chinese force.
  • Decline: in the mid-3rd century the Sassanians of Iran took Afghanistan and the area west of the Indus; satraps then set themselves up as independent rulers.
MCQ Trap / Source ConflictSource conflict: the theory notes list Shravasti as part of Kanishka's empire, but the Rabatak inscription names only Saket, Kaushambi, Pataliputra and Champa — the exam asks which city is not in Rabatak. Also, the notes' text gives the Rabatak find-spot as 'Baghran province'; the explanation gives Surkh Kotal.

Kanishka & Buddhism

  • Hosted the Fourth Buddhist Council at Kundalvana (Kashmir), conducted in Sanskrit; Mahayana doctrines were finalised and the literature, inscribed on copper plates, was sealed in a stupa. Vasumitra headed the council.
  • Supported missions to China; patronised Ashvaghosha, Parsva, Vasumitra and Nagarjuna; built a massive stupa at Peshawar (Purushapura); coins show a mix of Indian, Greek and Zoroastrian deities, reflecting tolerance.
  • Court of Kanishka: Parsva, Vasumitra, Ashvaghosha, Nagarjuna, Charaka (court physician) and Mathara. Not in his court: Patanjali (Shunga period), Kalidasa (Gupta period), Kamban and Vishakhadatta. Ashvaghosha was his poet laureate, a contemporary of Kanishka.
  • Taxila University (not Kanishka-specific) produced Charaka and Jivaka, as well as Kautilya, Chandragupta, Prasenjit and Vasubandhu.

Kushan Coins — Yaudheya Evidence

  • Yaudheya copper coins show a standing six-headed Kartikeya with a peacock on the right, issued during Kushan rule (not Vasudeva, Mitra or Indra). Yaudheyas are attested in the Puranas and Ashtadhyayi.
  • Kushanas produced the largest number of copper coins in northern and north-western India and circulated gold and copper widely; Kushan copper coins in India imitated Roman coins.
9

Eras: Vikrama Samvat & Saka Samvat

Calendars

  • Vikrama Samvat began in 57 BC (also called Krita and Malava); Jain texts put it 470 years after Mahavira's nirvana (527 BC): 527 − 470 = 57 BC. To get it, add 57 to the Gregorian year.
  • Saka Samvat began in 78 AD — to get it, subtract 78 from the Gregorian year; adopted as India's national calendar; most historians say Kanishka began it. Jain tradition: the successor of Vikramaditya was defeated by the Sakas in 135 Vikrama Samvat; to celebrate, the Sakas began a new calendar — 135 − 57 = 78 AD. The difference between the two eras is 135 years (example: 2019 = 2076 Vikrama and 1941 Saka). December 2009 = Saka 1931.
  • Kanishka's coronation date was disputed; international conventions in London (1913 and 1960) agreed on 78 AD in the second.
  • Chaitra is the first month of the national calendar; Chaitra 1 = 22 March (21 March in a leap year) in the Gregorian calendar.
  • The Sarnath Buddha image inscription of Kanishka is dated 81 AD, three years after his coronation.
10

Socio-Cultural Impact of Central Asian Contact

Polity

  • Feudal organisation grew as they imposed rule on native princes. Shakas and Parthians brought the satrap system (like the Achaemenid and Seleucid model).
  • Hereditary dual rule — two kings ruling jointly (even father and son), implying lesser centralisation.
  • Military governorship (Strategos), probably from the Greeks: kept control over indigenous people and blocked north-west invasions.
  • Kushan coin titles: king of kings, Caesar, lord of all lands, sons of God — collecting tribute from many small princes and strengthening divine kingship.

Economy, Trade & Karakoram Highway

  • Gold came from the Altai mountains and possibly from Roman trade.
  • Kushans controlled the Silk Route (China, Central Asia, Afghanistan, Iran, West Asia) — tolls were a major income; they encouraged agriculture and large-scale irrigation (Pakistan, Afghanistan, western Central Asia).
  • Karakoram Highway artefacts show the route of Buddhist monks to China; merchants followed missionaries, making it a trade route for Chinese silk and horses.
  • Kushan trade ran via the Persian Gulf and Red Sea — evidence from the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea and Arikamedu excavations — but nothing describes Kushan naval forces (so a naval base is not a valid reason). The Periplus is an anonymous work of c. mid-1st century CE by a Greek-speaking merchant; translated by Wilfred Schoff (not Ctesias, Pliny, Ptolemy or Strabo).

Warfare & Society

  • Shakas and Kushans brought better cavalry and the large-scale riding horse; they introduced the turban, tunic, trousers, long coats, caps, helmets and boots.
  • Central Asian rulers became Indianized and were absorbed as a warrior (Kshatriya) class; Manu called them second-class Kshatriyas who had deviated from duty.
  • Child marriage began in the Kushana period (1st century AD): the abolition of Upanayana for women and child marriage pushed women to the lowest social stage; 8–10 years was thought the best age for a girl's marriage.

Religion & Mahayana

  • Kushan rulers revered Shiva, Vishnu and Buddha; coins carry Shiva and Vishnu; the Kushan ruler Vasudeva (a synonym of Krishna) openly worshipped Vishnu.
  • Mahayana arose because original Buddhism was 'too puritanical and abstract'; trade and the arrival of Central Asians fostered image worship of the Buddha.
  • The oldest Buddha sculpture is in the Gandhara style; the seated Buddha from Jamal Garhi (Gandhara, Pakistan) dates from the 2nd–3rd century AD.

Art & Architecture

  • Gandhara and Mathura were the two major sculptural schools, fostered by Mahayana (which allowed the Buddha in human form) and royal patronage; Kanishka protected them.
  • Gandhara art = Indo-Greek (Greco-Buddhist, Greco-Roman) style, a blend of Greek, Syrian, Persian and Indian influences, flourishing in the Shaka and Kushan era; all subjects are Buddhist; green schist was its medium.
  • Mathura sculpture was influenced by Gandhara; the headless statue of Kanishka (Mathura shrine, 2nd century CE) and the Gandhara Buddha from Takht-i-Bahi (c. 300 CE, now in Berlin) are examples. Distinct plain and polished redware pottery was common.
  • Bamiyan (Afghanistan) is famed for Buddha statues cut into the hills, destroyed by the Taliban.
  • Pair: Kushans – Gandhara School of Art is correct (Harappan–Painted Grey Ware, Mughals–Ajanta, Marathas–Pahari are not).

Part C
The Satavahanas & Their Contemporaries

The native successors of the Mauryas in the Deccan, plus Kharavela, Ikshvakus and Vakatakas.

11

Origins, Rulers & Capitals

Introduction

  • Rose in the 1st century BC in the Deccan as the native successors of the Mauryas in the Deccan and central India; believed identical with the Andhras of the Puranas (Andhrabhrityah — probably an Andhra tribe). The most powerful dynasty after the Mauryas in the South and the earliest among the Chalukya, Pallava, Rashtrakuta and Satavahana.
  • Ruled parts of Andhra, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Madhya Pradesh. They began in Telangana, moved to the Godavari basin in Maharashtra with Pratishthana (Paithan) as capital, and later moved east to control coastal Andhra.
  • Earliest inscriptions (1st century BC): they defeated the Kanvas and held north Maharashtra and the upper Godavari valley.
  • Simuka (Sindhuka / Shishuka / Shipraka in the Puranas) founded the dynasty after the assassination of the Kanva king Susharman (founded 'near the 2nd century BC' in one answer, 1st century BC in the other).
  • The Matsya Purana gives the longest list — 30 Satavahana rulers (not Vayu or Vishnu Purana).
MCQ Trap / Source ConflictCapital: the real capital was Paithan / Pratishthana; Amaravati is believed to be the initial capital — one question's key gave Amaravati, another gave Pratishthana. Sakala/Sialkot (Menander's capital), Pataliputra (Shunga) and Nagarjunakonda (Ikshvaku) are wrong. Founding date: 1st century BC (Satavahana before Vakataka and Chalukya) — one answer says near the 2nd century BC.

Early Rulers

  • Simuka: founder. His brother Krishna succeeded him; Krishna's son Satakarni I was the first to hold the title Satakarni; the Naneghat inscriptions (eulogy of queen Naganika, wife of Satakarni I) give his governance details — Rajasuya and Ashvamedha sacrifices. Satakarni I is also mentioned in Kharavela's Hathigumpha inscription.
  • Satakarni II is named at the Sanchi gateway; Satavahanas contributed to Sanchi, repaired under Satakarni II. Vasishthiputra Sri-Chimuka is named in the Kanaganahalli (Sannati, Karnataka) Adhalaka Mahachaitya inscription.

Gautamiputra Satakarni (106–130 AD)

  • Defeated the Shaka ruler Nahapana and re-issued Nahapana's coins with his own insignia; the Nashik (Pandavleni) inscription of his mother Gautami Balashri calls him conqueror (destroyer) of Shakas, Pahlavas and Yavanas and records victory over Nahapana.
  • Performed the Vedic Ashvamedha; titles Raja-Raja, Maharaja and Lord of the Vindhya.
  • A patron of Brahmanism: claimed to be a Brahmana, called Ekabrahmana ('unrivalled Brahmana'), re-established the four-fold varna, forbade inter-caste marriage and cross-breeding (protector of the Varna system) — not Pushyamitra, Kharavela, Vasudeva or Samudragupta. Supported scholars and Vedic and Buddhist studies. Nashik inscription–Gautamiputra is a correct pairing.

Later Rulers

  • Vasishthiputra Pulumavi (130–154 AD): son and successor of Gautamiputra; capital at Paithan on the Godavari; Rudradaman I's Junagadh inscription records his defeat.
  • Yajnashri Satakarni (165–194 AD): last significant king; recovered north Konkan and Malwa from the Shakas; ship-motif coins show overseas trade.
  • Hala: composed the Gatha Saptashati (Gaha Sattasai) — 700 Prakrit love poems (also nature and rural life), similar to Sangam poetry.

Satavahana Polity, Society & Economy

Administration

  • Followed the Dharmashastras; the king was the upholder of dharma with divine attributes. Kept some Ashokan structures: Amatyas and Mahamatras; high officials Maharashtrikas.
  • New divisions: Rashtra (province) and Ahara (district).
  • Three grades of feudatories: Raja (right to strike coins), Mahabhoja, Senapati (commander-in-chief appointed provincial governor for tribal areas).
  • Rural areas: Gaulmika, head of a military regiment. Kataka and Skandhavara = military camps/settlements that were administrative centres while the king was there. Pliny: the Andhra army had infantry, cavalry and elephants.
  • Began tax-free land grants to Brahmanas and Buddhist monks (Naneghat inscriptions: tax exemption to land given to monks), creating non-cultivating landowners and a land-based social hierarchy; Brahmans upheld the varna order.

Society

  • Originally a Deccan tribe, but Brahmanized. Matrilineal elements: kings named after mothers (Gautamiputra, Vashishthiputra); some inscriptions issued by king and mother together — yet succession was patriarchal.

Economy & Material Culture

  • Iron tools from ores at Karimnagar and Warangal; gold as bullion (no gold coins; gold workings at Kolar). Coins in lead, potin, copper, bronze (and silver, punch-marked); potin = base alloy of copper, tin and lead with little precious metal.
  • Paddy transplantation and cotton between the Krishna and Godavari. Roman and Satavahana coins in the region show brisk trade; merchants and artisans gave generously to Buddhism — Gandhikas (perfumers, later all shopkeepers) were frequent donors.
  • Pliny: Andhra had 30 walled towns and many villages. Towns grew in Maharashtra by the 1st century BC and later in the eastern Deccan.

Religion, Language & Learning

  • Performed Ashvamedha and Vajapeya; worshipped Krishna and Vasudeva. Mahayana won followers, especially artisans; Nagarjunakonda and Amaravati (Andhra) were Buddhist centres under the Satavahanas and the Ikshvakus; also Nasik and Junar in western Deccan.
  • Official language Prakrit; all inscriptions in Prakrit, written in Brahmi. Sanskrit developed alongside Prakrit and folk languages (kings patronised them) but was used rarely in inscriptions; public arts were encouraged and examples are largely Buddhist.
  • Scholars: Hala (Gatha Saptashati); Sarvasena (Hari Vijaya, Sanskrit; patron of Prakrit); Gunadhya (Brihatkatha in Paisachi, source of the Kathasaritsagara); queen Naganika (Naneghat inscription).

Architecture

  • Rock-cut chaityas and viharas in the north-western Deccan: Karle chaitya; three viharas at Nasik carry Nahapana and Gautamiputra inscriptions (1st–2nd century AD).
  • Amaravati stupa (Andhra) began c. 200 BC, rebuilt late 2nd century AD; its platform has Ayaka (Aryaka) pillars. Nagarjunakonda flourished in the 2nd–3rd centuries under the Ikshvakus, with Buddhist monuments and early Brahmanical brick temples.

Decline

  • Declined c. 3rd century AD; replaced by the Ikshvakus, then the Pallavas in Andhra and the Kadambas in north Karnataka. After Satavahana weakness the Vakatakas rose in Dakshinapatha; the Ikshvakus (rulers called Shriparvatiya in the Puranas, founder Shri Shantamula) were lords under the Satavahanas and became independent after their fall.
  • Vakataka: founder Vindhyashakti; Pravarasena was his son (so Pravarasena as first king is false).

Contemporaries: Kharavela & Inscription Pairings

Kharavela of Kalinga

  • King of Kalinga (Odisha), of the Chedi dynasty (also called Mahameghavahana); a follower and patron of Jainism (not Ashoka, Harsha, Pulakeshin II or Huvishka).
  • Known from the Hathigumpha inscription (undated, partly damaged) at Udayagiri caves near Bhubaneswar, plus minor Udayagiri inscriptions; it records 13 years of reign. Udayagiri has 18 caves and Khandagiri 15; both natural and artificial caves; the Hathigumpha is at Udayagiri, not Khandagiri.
  • Failed to stop Menander's advance (Hathigumpha); the inscription also mentions a possible clash with the Satavahanas.

Inscription–Ruler Pairs

  • Nasik – Gautamiputra ✔; Hathigumpha – Kharavela ✔; Girnar – Rudradaman I ✔; Bhitari – Pulakeshin II ✘ (Bhitari pillar = Skandagupta, at Bhitari, Ghazipur, UP; Pulakeshin II = Aihole, Karnataka).
  • Taxila inscription = Patika.
  • Capitals: Shunga – Pataliputra; Satavahana – Paithan/Pratishthana; Kadamba – Banavasi; Chandela – Mahoba.
  • Places and states: Karnasuvarna (Murshidabad, West Bengal); Pragjyotisha (ancient Assam / Kamarupa); Girnar (Gujarat); Pratishthana / Paithan (Godavari; Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar, Maharashtra).

Trade, Rome & Miscellaneous

Odd One-Offs

  • Justinian (Eastern Roman emperor) is known for Roman law; his Corpus Juris Civilis is still the base of civil law in many states.
  • Ancient trade: numerous river ports and large entrepots — rivers Ganga, Indus, Kaveri; ports Lothal, Muziris and Sopara (trade with Rome and Southeast Asia); warehouses at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro. Both statements true.
