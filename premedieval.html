Early-Medieval Period: Pratihara to Sena

01 Gurjara-Pratihara Dynasty (founded 730 AD)

Earliest-founded dynasty in this dossier. Draws on the intro plus Q36–Q41, Q47–Q49, Q51–Q55, Q67.

Nagabhata I (730–760 AD) is considered the founder of the Gurjara-Pratihara dynasty, but the actual founder was Vatsaraja. Pratihara dynasty belonged to the "Agni" clan of Rajputs; Gurjars were first mentioned in the Aihole inscription of Pulakesin-II and in Banabhatta's Harshacharita. Nagabhatta-I destroyed the army of a Malecha ruler, saving western India from Arab invasion — recorded in the Gwalior inscription.

Mihirbhoja (a.k.a. Bhoja-I, 836–885 AD) was the most prominent/greatest Pratihara ruler. He was a follower of Vaishnavism and attained the titles "Aadi Varaha" and "Prabhas." The capital of the Pratiharas was Kannauj, whose ancient names were Mahodaya Shri and Kanyakubja during Mihirbhoj's regime — also called Mahodaya and "Nagar Mahoday Shri."

⚠ Trap — Mihir Bhoja's parentage & identity

Emperor Ramabhadra and queen Appa Devi were his parents (i.e. he was son of Nagabhatta-II, NOT directly of Ramabhadra by an earlier-generation reading — the two statements both occur in the source). He seized Kannauj around 836 AD. Arab traveller Al-Masudi (of Baghdad) called the Gurjara-Pratihara kingdom "Al-Juzr" and its king "Baura" — a mispronunciation of Adivaraha, though Bhoja had already died by the time of the visit. Separately, Arab traveller Suleman visited India during Mihir Bhoja/Bhoja-I's own reign.

Rajashekhara was a courtier-scholar of Gurjar-Pratihara king Mahendrapala-I and his son Mahipala-I. He compiled Karpuramanjari, Kavyamimamsa, Viddhasalabhanjika, Bal Ramayana and Bhuvankosh — "Prabandhachintamani" is NOT his work, a recurring MCQ trap.

Vakpati, court poet of Yashovarman of Kannauj, authored "Gaudavaho."

Kannauj & the Tripartite Struggle

After Harsha's death, Kannauj became the object of a tripartite struggle among the Pala, Gurjara-Pratihara and Rashtrakuta dynasties during the 8th–9th century AD — the Cholas were NOT part of this struggle. The Pratiharas ultimately emerged victorious.

Not a Pratihara ruler — MCQ trap

Bharatrabhatta-I does not belong to the Gurjara-Pratihara dynasty, unlike Nagabhatta-II, Mahendrapala-I, and (per one listing) Devapala's inclusion in a ruler-list is itself disputed since Devapala was a Pala king.

Q49 (matching) Pratihara → Kannauj; Chola → Tanjore; Paramara → Dhara; Solanki → Anhilwada. Ans: 4,2,3,1
Q54–56 Mahodaya = old name of Kannauj; "Nagar Mahoday Shri" = Kannauj; Jejakabhukti (NOT "Kaushambi") = Bundelkhand.

02 Rashtrakuta Dynasty (founded 753 AD)

Draws on Q33–Q35, Q57 — the Rashtrakutas appear mainly as the third party in the tripartite struggle and as Pratihara adversaries.

Danti Durga laid the foundation of the Rashtrakuta Empire in 753 AD; his capital was Manyakheta. He is said to have performed the "Hiranyagarbha" oblation ritual at Ujjayini.

Amoghavarsha-I was born in 800 AD at Shribhavan near the Narmada river, in a military camp, while his father Govind-III was returning from a successful North India campaign.

Govind-III, successor of Rashtrakuta king Dhruva, attacked North India after consolidating southern power and defeated Gurjara-Pratihara ruler Nagabhatta-II — recorded in the Sanjan and Radhanpur copper-plate inscriptions.

Rashtrakuta king Krishna-III led a confederacy of the Western Gangas, Banas and Vaidumbas against the Cholas at the Battle of Takkolam (see Chola material in the South India dossier for the Chola side of this engagement).

03 Pala Dynasty (founded ~750 AD)

Draws on the intro plus Q3–Q4, Q17–Q32.

Gopala was the founder of the Pala dynasty. Per Tibetan historian Lama Taranath, Gopala was born near Pundravardhana and was elected king of Bengal by the people — the Khalimpur inscription of Dharmapala describes this election as a means to overcome Matsya Nyaya (Law of the Jungle). (Separately, Pallava ruler Nandivarman Pallavamalla was also publicly elected, after the sudden death of Permeshwar Varman.)

Pundravardhana Bhukti was located in the North Bengal region in ancient times; its territory expanded beyond northern Bihar during the era of the Pala, Chandra and Sena dynasties.

Dharmapala — the Builder of Universities

Dharmapala (770–810 AD) was an enthusiastic Buddhist, called "Paramsaugat" in his inscriptions. He constructed the famous Buddhist monasteries at Vikramshila and Somapura (Paharpur). Renowned Buddhist commentator Haribhadra was part of his court; per Taranath he founded 50 religious schools yet showed no religious intolerance.

Vikramshila University was established by Dharmapala at Bhagalpur, Bihar — after Nalanda's decline it became the main centre of Buddhist studies, teaching Vedic texts, rituals, Vedangas, reasoning, medicine, law, astronomy and city-planning. It was later demolished by Bakhtiyar Khalji.

Odantapuri (also called Udnapur), a prominent Bihar education centre, was established by Pala ruler Gopala.

⚠ Trap — Vikramshila location statement-set (69th BPSC 2023)

Of four statements — (1) it was in present-day Banka district, (2) established by Gopala-I, (3) Vajrayana Buddhism flourished there, (4) Astronomy/Logic/Law/Grammar/Philosophy also taught — statements 1 and 2 are INCORRECT: it is actually in Bhagalpur district, established by Dharmapala, not Gopala. Statements 3 and 4 are correct.

Devapala — the Nalanda Grant

The Nalanda copper-plate of Devapala records that Balaputradeva, ruler of Suvarnabhumi (Sailendra dynasty, Java), built a monastery at Nalanda and requested Devapala — also a Buddhist, styled "Param Saugat" — to grant five villages for its upkeep, which he did. This is cited as evidence that early Bihar rulers had contact with Java-Sumatra.

Vijayasena did NOT build Sompura Mahavihar — that was Dharmapala, sometimes confused in MCQ distractors with Kumaragupta-I or Harsha.

Not a centre of learning during the early-medieval period: Taxila — Taxila was an earlier (ancient, pre-medieval) Buddhist learning centre near modern Rawalpindi, where Chanakya, Chandragupta Maurya, Charaka and Jivaka studied; Nalanda (5th c., by Kumaragupta) and Vikramshila/Odantapuri (Pala era) are the correct early-medieval centres.

04 Chandela Dynasty of Jejakabhukti (founded early 9th c.)

Draws on the intro plus Q7–Q8, Q13–Q15, Q50, Q56.

Jejakabhukti was the ancient name of Bundelkhand, named after Jay Shakti (also called Jeja/Jejak), grandson of Nannuk, the founder of the Chandel dynasty. A recurring MCQ trap wrongly pairs "Jaijakbhukti–Kaushambi" — the correct present-day region is Bundelkhand, not Kaushambi.

Dhangadev (also known as Dhanga) was a king of the Chandel dynasty of Jejakabhukti. His successor Vidyadhara built the Kandariya Mahadev temple. Dhanga attained Moksha by abandoning his body in the waters of the Ganga and Yamuna at Sangam, Prayagraj.

⚠ Trap — Q50 pairing set

Nannuka–Chandela ✓ and Jayashakti–Chandela ✓ are correctly matched (the source question lists Jayashakti against "Paramara," which is wrong); Nagabhata II–Gurjara Pratihara ✓ is correct; Bhoja–Rashtrakuta is WRONG (Bhoja was Paramara). Only two of the four listed pairs are actually correct.

05 Paramara Dynasty (founded 9th century AD)

Draws on the intro plus Q16, Q59–Q64.

Paramara dynasty was founded in the 9th century AD by Upendra (also called Krishnaraj). The early power-centre of the Paramaras was Ujjain; the capital later shifted to Dhara. The real founder is separately identified as Siyak-II in the dynasty-capital summary — both figures appear in the source.

⚠ Commission dispute — founder of the Paramara dynasty

One MCQ's official answer key accepted "Kanakpala" as the founder, but the source explicitly states the correct answer is Upendra/Krishnaraj — keep both in mind for exam purposes.

King Bhoj

King Bhoj was a Paramara ruler who ruled from Dhara. After his death, scholars lamented: "Adya Dhara Nira Dhara, Niralamba Saraswati, Pandita Khandita sarve Bhoj Raje Divangate."

Bhoj authored 'Samarangana Sutradhara' (on architecture and artificial/scientific equipment — the answer key was later revised from Bhoja to "Govindaraja" in one contested question, but the source treats Bhoja as primary author). His other works include Saraswati Kanthabharan, Siddhanta Sangraha, Yoga Sutra Vritti, Rajamartand, Yukti-Kalpataru and Charucharya.

The Udaipur Prashasti praises Bhoja — comparing him to King Prithu and crediting him with ruling from Kailash to the Malaya mountains, "achieving, accomplishing and understanding everything that no one else could."

Bhoj also built the Samidheshwar / Tribhuvan Narayan temple at Chittour (Bhoj Jagti), housing a three-faced Shiva idol resembling the main idol of the Elephanta caves, Mumbai.

Bhojshala

Bhojshala temple, in Dhar district, Madhya Pradesh, was established by Paramara King Bhoj in 1035 AD as a Sanskrit school. Its presiding deity is Goddess Saraswati. It now stands within the premises of the Kamal Maula Mosque.

Gangeyadeva was NOT a Parmar king — a recurring MCQ trap, since he actually belonged to the Kalachuri dynasty (see next section). Upendra, Munj and Udayaditya were genuine Paramara kings.

06 Chahamana (Chauhan) Dynasty of Ajmer

Legendary origin per the Hammir Epic; culminates with the fall of Delhi/Ajmer in 1192 AD. Draws on the intro plus Q1–Q2, Q6, Q9–Q11.

Per the Hammir Epic, the Chauhans are described as Suryavanshi — scions of "Chahman," the son of the Sun, and one of the four fire clans. Vasudeva and Naradev are named as the initial kings of the dynasty. Jodhraj wrote Hammir Raso (a recurring trap wrongly credits this to "Abdur Rehman").

Prithviraj III — "Prithviraj Chauhan"

  • Ruler of Ajmer, commonly known as Prithviraj Chauhan.
  • Defeated Muhammad Ghori in the 1st Battle of Tarain — 1191 AD.
  • Suffered defeat in the 2nd Battle of Tarain — 1192 AD.

Vigraharaj IV, the most powerful ruler of the Chauhan dynasty prior to Prithviraj III, conquered nearby regions, triumphed over the Tomaras, and took possession of Eastern Punjab and Delhi, also defeating the Muslim ruler of Punjab.

Author ledger — Chauhan-era chronicles

  • Prithviraj Raso — Chand Bardai.
  • Prithviraja Vijaya — Jayanaka.
  • Hammir Raso — Jodhraj (NOT Abdur Rehman).
  • Visal Dev Raso — Narpati Nalh.
  • Alha-Khand (of Parmal Raso) — Jagnik (see Chandela section).

07 Chalukya (Solanki) Dynasty of Gujarat (founded by Mularaja-I)

Draws on the intro plus Q42, Q49, Q58.

The Chalukyas (Solanki) belonged to the Agni Clan lineage, founded by Mularaja-I. Their capital was Anhilwada.

Jayasimha Siddharaja (1093–1143 AD) was a religiously tolerant Solanki king; per Muslim author Muhammad Aufi, he granted 1 lakh Balom (currency) for the restoration of a demolished mosque in Khambhat.

Jain scholar Hemchandra rose to fame under Jaysimha Siddharaja, but adorned the court of his successor Kumarapala (1143–1172 AD) as advisor.

08 Kalachuri Dynasty (Gangeyadeva, mid-11th c.)

Draws on the intro plus Q64–Q65.

Gangeyadeva was a ruler of the Kalachuri dynasty, capital Tripuri. He adopted the title "Vikramaditya" and was the first to start issuing gold coins in early-medieval North India.

Arab writer and geographer Al-Biruni — Khwarazmian Iranian scholar, author of Tarikh al-Hind — described Gangeyadeva and his capital Tripuri (NOT Al-Bahari, Al-Masudi, or Ibn Battuta).

09 Tomar Dynasty of Delhi (founded Delhi 1052 AD)

Draws on the intro plus Q12.

Anangpal Tomar Rajput (Tomar dynasty), originally feudatories of the Gurjar-Pratiharas, founded the city of Dhillika (modern Delhi) in 1052 AD. The Tomars were later defeated by the Chauhans under Vigraharaj IV, who annexed Delhi (see Chauhan section).

10 Karnata Dynasty of Mithila (founded by Nanyadev, 1097 AD)

Draws on Q71–Q72.

Nanyadev (1097–1147 AD), a great warrior, founded the Karnata dynasty, establishing his capital at Simraungadh and ruling the Mithila region.

Harisimha (1295/1296–1324 AD) was the last ruler of the Karnata dynasty — a great protector of art and literature, who started the Panji system.

11 Gahadavala Dynasty of Kannauj

Draws on the intro plus Q5.

Kumaradevi, queen of Govind Chandra Gahadavala, was Buddhist and constructed the Dharmachakra Jain Vihar at Sarnath.

Krityakalpataru was written by Lakshmidhar, minister of Govind Chandra.

Jayachandra was the last powerful ruler of the Gahadavala dynasty.

12 Sena Dynasty of Bengal (culminates 1178–1206 AD)

Draws on the intro plus Q43–Q44, Q46.

Lakshmanasena (1178–1206 AD) was king of the Sen Dynasty; he reigned 28 years and initiated a new Samvat called "Lakshmana Samvat" — also referred to as the "Luxman Era."

13 Later Coda: Sultanate of Kashmir (15th century AD — later medieval)

This ruler falls well outside the early-medieval window (8th–13th c.) covered above, but appears in the source (Q69–Q70) and is retained here as an appendix rather than dropped.

Zain-ul-Abidin, originally named Shahi Khan, was the brother of Ali Shah and ascended the Kashmir throne in 1420 AD, ruling for nearly 50 years. Owing to his liberal religious policy, patronage of learning, and interest in art and architecture, he was called the "Akbar of Kashmir."

14 Cross-Dynasty Ledger: Jurists, Seaports & Terms

Facts that cut across dynasties or don't anchor to one ruling house. Draws on the intro plus Q45, remaining seaport/matching/terminology questions.

Famous Jurists of Early-Medieval India

Jurist ↔ Work

  • Vigyaneshwara — wrote "Mitakshara."
  • Jimutavahana — wrote "Dayabhaga."
  • Hemadri — the third famous jurist of the period.

Rajashekhara (see Pratihara section) is a court scholar, not one of these three jurists — a recurring MCQ distractor pairs him in the same list.

Motupalli — the Kakatiya Seaport

Motupalli was a very important seaport of the Kakatiya kingdom, presently located in the Bapatla district, Andhra Pradesh. Italian traveller Marco Polo visited this port and wrote of the prosperity and power of the Andhra region. The Motupalli epigraph specifies rates assessed on goods including sandal, camphor, rose-water, ivory, pearls, corals, copper, zinc, lead, silk, pepper and areca nuts — giving insight into the port's exports and imports.

Terminology

Araghatta — in medieval Indian economic history, refers to a Persian-wheel waterwheel used for land irrigation, typically animal-operated (bullocks, buffaloes, camels); mentioned in the Panchatantra and Rajatarangini.

Bastar Inscriptions — Nagavamsa Dynasty

The Kuruspal village (Bastar district, Chhattisgarh) has yielded multiple inscriptions:

  • Kuruspal stone inscription of Someswardeva — much mutilated; records a land grant by Someswardeva of the Nagavamsa dynasty (also referenced as Mahashivgupta Balarjun in the associated MCQ).
  • Kuruspal Tank slab of Dharana Mahadevi — built into a tank's steps; records a land grant near Kalamba village to god Kamesvara (Shiva) by queen Dharna Mahadevi during the reign of Someshwardeva/Someshwar-I.

⚠ Cross-check — dynasty/capital matching set (recurring across exams)

Pratihara → Kannauj · Paramara → Dhara · Solanki → Anhilwada · Chola → Tanjore. Keep these distinct from the South India dossier's Kadamba–Banavasi / Chalukya-of-Badami–Vatapi / Pallava–Kanchipuram set, since "Chalukya" alone is ambiguous between the Deccan (Badami) and Gujarat (Solanki) branches.
