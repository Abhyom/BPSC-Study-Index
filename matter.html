01What Is Matter

  • Everything in the universe made of material is called matter — it has mass, occupies volume, has physical resistance and inertia, and is realised by the sense organs.
  • Early Indian philosophers classified matter into five basic elements — the 'Pancha Tatva': air, earth, fire, sky, water.
  • Characteristics of matter: (i) made of very small particles; (ii) particles have intermolecular space between them; (iii) particles are continuously moving (possess kinetic energy, which increases with temperature); (iv) particles attract each other via intermolecular force; (v) this force's strength varies between types of matter.

02Atoms & Molecules

  • Atom — smallest particle of an element that may/may not exist independently and retains all chemical properties (takes part in chemical reactions).
  • Molecule — group of 2+ atoms chemically bonded; smallest particle of an element/compound capable of independent existence, showing all properties of that substance — but does NOT take part in a chemical reaction.
  • Homomolecules (molecules of an element) — same type of atoms, e.g. O₂, H₂, N₂; number of atoms = atomicity (P₄ = 4, S₈ = 8).
  • Heteromolecules (molecules of compounds) — atoms of different elements in definite proportions.
Heteromolecule combining-mass ratios
CompoundCombining ElementsRatio by Mass

Ammonia (NH₃)Nitrogen, Hydrogen14:3

03Solid, Liquid & Gas

  • The three states of matter arise from variation in intermolecular forces and intermolecular space.
The three classical states — comparison
StateShape/VolumeIntermolecular Force/SpaceKey Facts
SolidDefinite mass, volume, shapeSmall space, strong forcesParticles only oscillate; incompressible, rigid, high density, high m.p.
LiquidNo fixed shape, fixed volumeModerate — enough mobility, particles can't escapeTakes container shape; upper surface always planar; called fluids
GasNo fixed shape or volumeVery weak forces, very large spaceHighly compressible; expands most on heating; also called fluids

Classification of Solids

  • Crystalline Solids — regular particle arrangement, e.g. sodium chloride, diamond, quartz, graphite.
  • Amorphous Solids — irregular/disordered particle arrangement, e.g. glass, rubber, plastics; also called pseudo solids or supercooled liquids.
Everyday Science A rubber band changes shape under force but regains it (and breaks under excess force) — hence classed as solid. A sponge is compressible yet solid, because of air-filled holes. Ice floats on water despite being solid, due to weaker H-bonding creating air-filled space, giving lower density than water.

04Plasma & Bose-Einstein Condensate

  • Plasma — super-energetic, super-excited, ionised gas particles; found in fluorescent tubes, neon sign bulbs; the Sun and stars glow due to plasma (created by very high temperature).
  • Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC) — named after Satyendra Nath Bose (India) and Albert Einstein; formed by cooling extremely low-density gas (~1/100,000th density of normal air) to super-low temperatures.
  • In 2001, Eric A. Cornell, Wolfgang Ketterle, Carl E. Wieman (USA) won the Nobel Prize in Physics for achieving BEC — the latest-discovered/fifth state of matter.

05Diffusion & Gas Pressure

  • Diffusion — intermixing of particles of two different types of matter on their own, e.g. ink spreading in water.
  • On heating, particle speed increases → more intermolecular space/less force → diffusion becomes faster — hence hot food's smell travels farther than cold food's.
  • Rate of diffusion: liquids diffuse faster than solids; gases diffuse fastest of all (high speed, large intermolecular space).
  • Gaseous Pressure — gas particles move randomly at high speed, hitting each other and container walls, exerting pressure.

06Fusion, Melting, Sublimation & Latent Heat

Interconversion terminology — a favourite one-liner MCQ zone
TermMeaning
FusionMelting — solid → liquid
Melting PointTemp. at which solid starts melting at atmospheric pressure; higher m.p. = stronger interparticle attraction
SublimationSolid → vapour directly, without liquid phase; reversible on cooling (sublimates)
VaporisationLiquid → gas rapidly on heating; called evaporation if below boiling point
Boiling PointTemp. at which liquid boils at atmospheric pressure; a bulk phenomenon, varies by place
CondensationGas → liquid, or liquid → solid (solidification)
Latent HeatHeat absorbed/released during a state change at constant temperature
  • Latent heat of fusion = heat needed to change 1 kg solid to liquid at its m.p., atmospheric pressure.
  • Latent heat of vaporisation = heat needed to change 1 kg liquid to gas at its b.p., atmospheric pressure — temperature stays constant during boiling due to this.

07Effect of Temperature & Pressure

  • Heating a solid → kinetic energy increases → particles vibrate faster → heat overcomes attraction forces → particles leave fixed positions → solid melts.
  • Increasing pressure + reducing temperature → gas→liquid→solid; reverse (decrease pressure, increase temperature) reverses this.
Everyday Science Steam at 373K (100°C) has more energy than water at the same temperature (latent heat of vaporisation) — causing more severe burns than boiling water. At high altitudes, low atmospheric pressure means water boils below 100°C, so food takes longer to cook. Inside a pressure cooker, higher pressure raises the boiling point above 100°C, so food cooks faster. Impurities raise boiling point and lower freezing point. Solid CO₂ ('dry ice'/'dry kold') is stored under high pressure and converts directly to gas at 1 atm — bypassing the liquid state.

08Element, Compound & Mixture

  • Robert Boyle first used the term 'element' in 1661; Antoine Laurent Lavoisier (1743–94), French chemist, defined an element as matter that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any physical/chemical process.
  • There are currently 118 elements — 98 naturally occurring, the rest artificially made.
  • Elements divide into metals, non-metals, metalloids. Element symbols derive from Latin/German/Greek names; first letter always capital, second small.
  • Compound — 2+ elements chemically combined in fixed proportion; different properties from constituents; broken only by chemical/electrochemical means (not physical); considered a homogeneous mixture; classed as organic (C as main constituent) or inorganic.
  • Mixture — impure substance, no fixed proportion; separable by simple physical/mechanical means, e.g. air, seawater, minerals, soil.

11Atomic, Molecular & Equivalent Mass

  • Atomic mass — relative mass compared with a carbon-12 atom, expressed in amu ('u', unified mass); 1 amu = 1.66056 × 10⁻²⁴ g = 1/12th the mass of one C-12 atom.
  • Average atomic mass — accounts for natural abundance of different isotopes.
  • Molecular mass — sum of atomic masses of all elements in a molecule, e.g. CH₄ = 1(12.011) + 4(1.008) = 16.043 u.
  • Formula unit mass — sum of atomic masses of all atoms in a formula unit, e.g. NaCl = 23+35.5 = 58.5 u.
  • Equivalent mass = Molecular mass ÷ Valency.

12Physical vs Chemical Changes

TypeDefinitionExamples

13Laws of Chemical Combination

Four foundational laws — proposer, statement, example
LawProposed ByStatement
Law of Conservation of MassAntoine Lavoisier, 1789Matter can neither be created nor destroyed; total mass of reactants = total mass of products
Law of Definite ProportionsJoseph Proust (French chemist)A given compound always contains the same proportion of elements by weight, irrespective of source — also called law of constant proportions
Law of Multiple ProportionsDalton, 1803If 2 elements form >1 compound, masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of the other are in a ratio of small whole numbers — e.g. O combining with fixed H mass in water vs H₂O₂ is 16:32 = 1:2
Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining VolumesGay-Lussac, 1808Gases combine/form in a simple ratio by volume at same T & P — e.g. H₂+O₂→H₂O in a 2:1 ratio

14Mole Concept

  • Mole — SI base unit for amount of chemical species; term from Latin 'moles' ('a heap/pile'), introduced ~1896 by Wilhelm Ostwald; accepted as an SI unit in 1967; symbol 'mol'.
  • 1 mole = amount weighing exactly its formula weight in grams = amount with 6.022 × 10²³ entities (as in 12g of C-12) = Avogadro's number (N₀/N_A).
  • 1 mole of a gas occupies 22.4 L at STP.
  • Number of moles = mass(g)/molecular weight = particles at STP/Avogadro's number = gas volume at STP(L)/22.4.

15MCQ Traps & Assessment Facts

MCQ Trap Father of modern chemistry = Antoine Lavoisier (not Rutherford, Otto Hahn, or Mendeleev).
MCQ Trap Maximum number of states of matter is considered variable (solid/liquid/gas/plasma/BEC and still being researched) — not fixed at three, four, or five.
MCQ Trap Bose-Einstein Condensate is described as the 'fifth state of matter' — not simply a solid, plasma, or generic condensed state.
MCQ Trap A substance can NOT be separated into other kinds of matter by any physical process (that's a chemical process for compounds); but dissolved NaCl CAN be separated from water by evaporation (physical process) — only Statement II is correct in such paired-statement questions.
MCQ Trap A mixture's structure is NOT constant — this is the property that does NOT apply to mixtures (unlike being formed of 2+ substances, separable physically, and components retaining properties).
MCQ Trap Emerald is the gemstone containing beryllium metal (not Topaz, Ruby, Blue Sapphire).
MCQ Trap Dry ice = solid carbon dioxide (not liquid CO₂, liquid nitrogen, or liquid ammonia). Quartz (used as piezo-electric material) contains Silicon (Si).
MCQ Trap A molecule resembles its atom only for a monoatomic molecule (not diatomic/polyatomic/triatomic).
MCQ Trap Statement "Atoms are always neutral in nature" is NOT correct about atoms (ions exist) — the odd one out among otherwise-true atom statements.
MCQ Trap Glass is a supercooled liquid (amorphous solid), not a crystalline solid or liquid crystal; it has no definite melting point and is NOT considered a true compound — both statements are true and Statement II explains Statement I.
MCQ Trap Manganese dioxide is used to remove the green colour from glass (not silicon dioxide, titanium dioxide, sulphur dioxide).
MCQ Trap Water boils at a lower temperature at high altitudes because air pressure is less (not because outside temperature or latent heat is less).
MCQ Trap Lowering atmospheric pressure on a liquid lowers its boiling point (does not increase it or leave it unaffected).
MCQ Trap For an ideal gas: the speed of all gas molecules is NOT the same — this is the false statement among otherwise-true ideal-gas assumptions (zero potential energy, no interactive force, unequal kinetic energies).
MCQ Trap Elements liquid at room temperature/standard pressure: Mercury and Bromine (not Helium or Chlorine, which are gases).
MCQ Trap Purity of milk is determined using a lactometer (not hydrometer, stalagmometer, or thermometer). Cream separates from churned milk due to centrifugal force.
MCQ Trap Iodine is separated from an iodine-potassium chloride mixture by sublimation (iodine sublimes; KCl does not).
MCQ Trap Evaporation of water is NOT a chemical change (it's physical) — unlike curdling of milk, ripening of fruit, and burning of coal, which ARE chemical changes.
MCQ Trap Conversion of blue copper sulphate to black cupric oxide on heating is a CHEMICAL change (Statement I is false) — even though "no change in chemical composition = physical change" (Statement II) is itself true in isolation.
MCQ Trap Liquid pressure at a beaker's base depends on the height of the liquid column — NOT on the base area of the container.
MCQ Trap Chemical changes: souring of milk ONLY among {crystallisation of NaCl, melting of ice, souring of milk} — the other two are physical changes.
MCQ Trap Product of equivalent weight × valency = molecular weight (not vapour density, specific heat, or atomic weight).
Fact Molecular mass of a gas = twice its vapour density (Molar mass = 2 × vapour density).
MCQ Trap Number of atoms in 46g of Na-23 = 2N (46g = 2 moles × Avogadro's number N).

01Solution & Its Components

  • A solution is a homogeneous mixture of 2+ substances whose relative component amounts can change up to a definite limit at constant temperature, e.g. lemonade, soda water.
  • Solvent — the component present in largest quantity; greater dielectric constant = better solvent; water has very high dielectric constant, hence called the 'universal solvent'; used in perfumes, drugs, food processing, beverages, dry cleaning.
  • Solute — component(s) other than solvent, generally in smaller amount ('dissolved substance'), e.g. tincture of iodine = iodine (solute) in alcohol (solvent); soda water = CO₂ (solute) in water (solvent).

02Properties of a Solution

  • Homogeneous mixture of uniform composition.
  • Particles smaller than 1 nm (10⁻⁹ m) — invisible to naked eye.
  • Does NOT scatter light — no Tyndall effect, light path is not visible.
  • Solute and solvent diffuse into each other and cannot be distinguished.
  • Solute particles cannot be separated by filtration (too tiny); do not settle when undisturbed — stable, permanent, transparent.

05Aqueous, Acidic, Basic & Neutral Solutions

  • Aqueous solution — solute dissolved in water, e.g. ethanol in water; Non-aqueous solution — solute dissolved in non-water solvent, e.g. iodine in alcohol.
  • Acidic solutions — more H⁺ ions than OH⁻; Basic solutions — more OH⁻ than H⁺; Neutral solutions — equal H⁺ and OH⁻ concentrations.

07Solubility & Its Factors

  • Solubility = max solute dissolvable in a given solvent amount (generally 100g) at a given temperature/pressure = (w/W) × 100.
  • Nature of solute/solvent — 'like dissolves like': polar solute (NaCl) in polar solvent (water); non-polar solute (cholesterol, bromine) in non-polar solvent (benzene, CCl₄).
  • Temperature effect — solubility usually increases with temperature (endothermic dissolution); but calcium nitrate, calcium oxide, sodium sulphate, calcium hydroxide, calcium citrate show decreasing solubility with rising temperature (exothermic dissolution). Gas solubility in liquid decreases with rising temperature.
  • Pressure effect — no effect on solid-in-liquid solubility; gas-in-liquid solubility increases with pressure.
  • Size of substance — solubility decreases as molecular mass increases.
Everyday Science Soft drink/soda water bottles are sealed under high pressure to increase CO₂ solubility. Deep-sea divers use oxygen diluted with less-soluble helium to minimise decompression sickness ('the bends').

08Colloidal Solution Basics

  • A colloidal solution is a heterogeneous system with two phases: dispersed phase (solute-like) and dispersion medium (solvent-like).
  • Colloidal particle size: 1 nm – 100 nm (1000 Å).
  • Examples: milk, face creams, sponge, rubber, pumice, blood, gems.

  • Gold number — minimum amount of lyophilic colloid (mg) that prevents coagulation of 10 mL gold sol by adding 1 mL of 10% NaCl solution.

By Particle Type

  • Multimolecular colloids — aggregates of many atoms/small molecules, e.g. gold sol, sulphur sol.
  • Macromolecular colloids — natural (starch, cellulose, proteins, enzymes) or man-made (polythene, nylon, polystyrene, synthetic rubber) macromolecules; quite stable, resemble true solutions.
  • Associated colloids (Micelles) — form only above the Kraft temperature (Tk) and above the Critical Micelle Concentration (CMC); behave as normal strong electrolytes at low concentration, e.g. soap solution. Cleansing action of soap/detergent is due to emulsification and micelle formation.

10Properties — Tyndall Effect & Brownian Motion

  • Colloidal solution is a heterogeneous, permanent system — cannot be filtered by ordinary paper; dispersed particles invisible to naked eye but visible under ultramicroscope.
  • Tyndall Effect — light scattering by colloidal particles makes the light path visible; explains blue sky, blue/green sea water, visible comet tails, twinkling stars, blue tinge of smoke, red setting sun.
  • Brownian Movement — continuous zig-zag colloidal particle motion, first observed by British botanist Robert Brown; depends on particle size and solution viscosity (not colloid nature); a stirring effect responsible for sol stability. Suspensions and true solutions do NOT show Brownian movement.
  • Colloidal particles always carry an electric charge — e.g. haemoglobin (blood) is positively charged; starch, gum, gelatin, clay, charcoal sols are negatively charged.
  • Colloidal solution colour depends on the wavelength of light scattered.
  • Coagulation — precipitation of a colloid on adding a small quantity of electrolyte; opposite-charge electrolyte particles neutralise colloidal particles, causing precipitation. Alum/ferric chloride applied on minor cuts stops bleeding by coagulating blood.

11Emulsions

  • Emulsions — liquid-liquid colloidal systems, formed by shaking 2 immiscible/partially miscible liquids; stabilised by emulsifying agents (protein, gum, soap, alcohol).
TypeDescriptionExample
Oil-in-Water (O/W)Oil mixed with water as dispersion mediumMilk, vanishing cream
Water-in-Oil (W/O)Water as dispersed phase, oil as dispersion mediumButter, cream
  • Emulsions show both Brownian movement AND Tyndall effect; can be broken by heating, freezing, centrifuging.

12Everyday Science of Colloids

  • Electrical Precipitation of Smoke — a Cottrell smoke precipitator removes charge from smoke's colloidal solid particles (carbon, arsenic compounds, dust), which then precipitate.
  • Purification of Drinking Water — alum coagulates suspended impurities in water.
  • Colloidal Medicines — Silver sol (eye lotion), colloidal antimony (Kala-azar treatment), colloidal gold (intramuscular injection), Milk of magnesia (stomach disorders) — more effective due to large surface area and easy assimilation.
  • Industries — rubber from coagulated latex; leather tanning (positively-charged animal hide + negatively-charged tannin); paints, inks, plastics, rubber, graphite lubricants, cement are all colloidal.
  • Photography — light-sensitive silver bromide emulsion coated in gelatin on glass/celluloid.
  • Artificial Rain — silver iodide sprinkled on clouds for seeding.
  • Ice-cream Making — gelatin stabilises the colloid, preventing crystallisation.

13Suspensions

  • A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture where solute particles do not dissolve, remaining suspended throughout the medium — e.g. chalk water, polluted river water, muddy water, soil.
  • Particle size: order of 10⁻⁵ cm or more — visible to naked eye.
  • Particles scatter light (visible path) and settle down when left undisturbed — an unstable mixture; separable by filtration. Once settled, it stops scattering light.

14MCQ Traps & Assessment Facts

MCQ Trap A solution is defined as a homogeneous mixture (not a compound, heterogeneous mixture, or suspension). Soil, sea water, air are all heterogeneous mixtures.
MCQ Trap Tin, Ice are NOT mixtures (pure substances); sea water, soil, air, ice-cream, honey ARE mixtures — a repeated "spot the pure substance" trap.
MCQ Trap In a sugar solution, sugar is the dissolved substance (solute), not the solvent, colloid, or suspension.
MCQ Trap Water-soluble salts: Sodium chloride is soluble; Lithium bromide, Calcium carbonate, Magnesium iodide are commonly tested as comparatively less/insoluble decoys — always verify against the specific option set.
MCQ Trap Mercury forms an amalgam by combining with almost any metal (not aluminium, gold, or silver specifically as the defining metal).
MCQ Trap Chromatography is the technique for separating a mixture by passing it through a medium where components move at different rates.
MCQ Trap Smog = smoke + fog combination.
MCQ Trap Mud is a colloidal solution of liquid dispersed in solid — actually it is SOLID dispersed in LIQUID (a Sol); always re-verify dispersed-phase vs dispersion-medium direction rather than assuming from the substance name.
MCQ Trap Solubility of calcium oxide increases on LOWERING temperature → its dissolution process is exothermic (releases heat) — the reverse of the typical solute behaviour.
MCQ Trap Colloidal is NOT a state of matter (unlike gaseous, liquid, solid) — it's a category of mixture/dispersion.
MCQ Trap Soaps clean surfaces based on surface tension principles (not viscosity, floatation, elasticity).
MCQ Trap Silica gel is the best example of a desiccant (not polystyrene, sodium chloride, sodium carbonate).
MCQ Trap Blood is a natural colloid (not urea, cane sugar, sodium chloride — all true solutions).

01Properties of Gases

  • The latest-discovered state of matter is Bose-Einstein condensate.
  • Gases can be compressed easily.
  • Gases have neither definite volume nor definite shape — a gas occupies the entire volume of its container.
  • Measurable gas properties (mass m, volume V, pressure p, temperature T) are interdependent — the relationships between them are called gas laws.

02Boyle's & Charles' Law

Boyle's Law (Pressure-Volume)

  • At constant temperature, pressure of a fixed gas amount varies inversely with volume: p ∝ 1/V, or pV = constant, or p₁V₁ = p₂V₂.
  • At constant temperature, pressure is directly proportional to density of a fixed gas mass.
  • Molecular mass of a gas = twice its vapour density (Molar mass = 2 × vapour density).

Charles' Law (Temperature-Volume)

  • At constant pressure, volume of a fixed gas mass is directly proportional to absolute temperature: V ∝ T, or V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂.
  • Absolute zero — the lowest hypothetical/imaginary temperature at which gases would occupy zero volume.
Everyday Science Bursting of a hydrogen balloon and the puffing of a chappati (roti) are applications of Charles' Law.

03Gay-Lussac's & Avogadro's Law

Gay-Lussac's Law (Pressure-Temperature)

  • At constant volume, pressure of a fixed gas amount varies directly with temperature: p ∝ T, or p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂.

Avogadro's Law (Volume-Amount)

  • Equal volumes of all gases under same T & p contain equal numbers of molecules: V ∝ n.
  • At STP, 1 mole of gas (gram molecular mass) occupies 22.4 L.
  • 1 mole contains 6.022 × 10²³ molecules — Avogadro's constant.

04Combined Gas Law & Dalton's Law

  • Combined Gas Law — for simultaneous variation of T, V, p of a fixed gas amount: pV/T = nR = constant, so p₁V₁/T₁ = p₂V₂/T₂.
  • Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures — atomic theory of matter proposed by Dalton; total pressure of a non-reacting gaseous mixture = sum of partial pressures of each component, at constant T,V: p(total) = p₁+p₂+p₃+…

05Graham's Law of Diffusion

  • At constant T,p: rate of diffusion (r) of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its density: r ∝ 1/√d, so r₁/r₂ = √(d₂/d₁).
  • Since Molar mass (M) = 2 × density (d): r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁).
  • Applications: production of marsh gas (CH₄); separation of gaseous mixtures; determination of vapour densities; separation of isotopes.
  • Diffusion = spontaneous mixing of different gases; rate of diffusion = volume of gas diffused per unit time.
Everyday Science LPG leakage is detected via diffusion of ethyl mercaptan, an additive. Lighter gases diffuse more quickly than heavier gases; CO₂ and O₂ in air diffuse into water for aquatic respiration.

06Ideal Gas & Ideal Gas Equation

  • An ideal/perfect gas — molecules are point masses (mass without volume) that don't attract each other; a hypothetical concept, cannot exist in reality.
  • Permanent gases — hydrogen, oxygen, helium — cannot be liquefied.
  • Perfect gas properties: strictly obeys Boyle's/Charles'/pressure laws under all conditions; pressure coefficient = volume coefficient; cannot be converted to liquid/solid (needs intermolecular attraction, which ideal gases lack).
  • Ideal Gas Equation — combines Boyle's, Charles', and Avogadro's laws: pV = nRT, also called the equation of state.
  • R = universal gas constant = 8.314 J mol⁻¹K⁻¹ or 0.0821 L·atm mol⁻¹K⁻¹.

07Real Gases

  • Real gases follow gas laws (behave ideally) only at high temperature and low pressure.
  • Real gases have definite volume and intermolecular attraction forces — no real gas is truly ideal.

08Kinetic Theory of Gases

  • J Bernoulli first explained gas behaviour in 1738; later Rudolph Clausius and James Clark Maxwell developed the full kinetic theory.

Key Assumptions

  • Gas consists of extremely small, discrete, identical particles (molecules) dispersed throughout the container.
  • Molecule volume is negligible compared to total gas volume.
  • Molecules are in constant random motion at high velocities, moving in straight lines, changing direction on collision.
  • Intermolecular forces are negligible — molecules move freely, independently.
  • All collisions are perfectly elastic — no kinetic energy loss overall (though redistribution can occur).
  • Gravity's effect on molecular motion is negligible compared to collision effects.
  • Gas pressure is caused by molecule hits on container walls — Pressure ∝ number of collisions per unit time per unit area (Newton's second law of motion).
  • Different molecules have different speeds/kinetic energies at a given instant, but average kinetic energy ∝ absolute temperature.
  • Pressure of an ideal gas: pV = (1/3)mNv², where m=mass of 1 molecule, N=total molecules, v=RMS velocity.

  • Relation between the three speeds: α : v : u = 1.414 : 1.595 : 1.732.

10Degree of Freedom

  • Degree of freedom = number of directions a dynamic system can move freely / number of coordinates needed to describe its position; denoted N.
  • N = A − R (A = number of particles, R = number of independent relations).
  • Gas molecules have translational, rotational, and vibrational kinetic energies.

02Bond Energy, Heat of Reaction/Formation/Combustion

  • Bond Energy — energy to break 1 mole of a bond type between two gaseous atoms (kJ mol⁻¹); larger bond dissociation energy = stronger bond; breaking a bond is endothermic, forming a bond is exothermic.
  • Heat of Reaction — heat evolved/absorbed in a reaction, e.g. H₂(g)+Br₂(l)→2HBr(g) +72.8 kJ/mol.
  • Heat of Formation — energy released/absorbed forming 1 mole of a compound from constituent elements.
  • Heat of Combustion — heat evolved burning 1 mole of a substance in excess oxygen.

03Rate of Reaction

  • Rate of reaction = decrease in reactant concentration / time taken = increase in product concentration / time taken.
  • Unit = concentration × time⁻¹, e.g. mol L⁻¹s⁻¹; for gaseous reactions (partial pressures), unit = atm s⁻¹.

05Activation Energy

  • Reactant molecules absorb extra energy to form an unstable activated complex (high potential energy), which then decomposes into products.
  • Activation energy = additional energy needed to form this activated complex; lower activation energy = faster reaction.

06Order & Molecularity of Reaction

  • Order of reaction = sum of concentration-term powers the rate actually depends on (found experimentally); can be 0, 1, 2, 3, or even a fraction. Zero order = rate independent of reactant concentration.
  • Molecularity = number of reacting species (atoms/ions/molecules) that must collide simultaneously in an elementary reaction; cannot be zero or non-integer (unlike order).

  • If a reaction proceeds in several steps, the overall rate is controlled by the slowest step (rate-determining step); molecularity of the slowest step = order of the overall reaction.
  • Pseudo first-order reactions — e.g. inversion of cane sugar, ester hydrolysis — have multiple reactants but rate depends on only one reactant's concentration.

07Catalysis, Promoters & Poisons

  • Catalysis — substances (catalysts) alter reaction rate while remaining chemically/quantitatively unchanged; first observed by Berzelius in 1835.
  • A catalyst provides a new reaction path with different (lower) activation energy.
  • Catalysts catalyse spontaneous reactions but NOT non-spontaneous ones.
  • A catalyst does NOT change the equilibrium constant — it catalyses both forward and backward reactions equally, helping equilibrium be reached faster (not shifting its position).
  • Promoters — enhance catalyst activity, e.g. molybdenum promotes iron catalyst in Haber's process (ammonia manufacture).
  • Poisons — decrease catalyst activity, e.g. carbon poisons silica-alumina catalyst in petroleum cracking.

09Chemical Equilibrium & Law of Mass Action

  • For a reversible reaction A+B⇌C+D: with time, forward reaction rate decreases and backward rate increases, until both become equal and concentrations stay constant — this is chemical equilibrium, which is dynamic (reactions don't stop, both proceed at equal rate).
  • CM Guldberg and Peter Waage proposed the Law of Mass Action in 1864 — rate of a chemical reaction is directly proportional to the active mass of the reacting substance(s), or the product of active masses.
  • For aA+bB⇌cC+dD: Kc = [C]^c[D]^d / [A]^a[B]^b — the equilibrium constant, ratio of backward:forward rate constants.
  • Kc is constant at a fixed temperature and changes only with temperature change.

10Homogeneous/Heterogeneous Equilibria & Le-Chatelier's Principle

  • Homogeneous equilibrium — all reactants/products in the same phase, e.g. CH₃COOC₂H₅(aq)+H₂O(l)⇌CH₃COOH(aq)+C₂H₅OH(aq); N₂(g)+H₂(g)⇌NH₃(g).
  • Heterogeneous equilibrium — more than one phase, e.g. H₂O(l)⇌H₂O(g); CaCO₃(s)⇌CaO(s)+CO₂(g).
  • Le-Chatelier's Principle — a change in any factor determining a system's equilibrium causes the system to shift so as to reduce/counteract the change.
  • Increasing reactant concentration (or removing product) at equilibrium → shifts forward; adding product (or removing reactant) → shifts backward.
  • Rising temperature shifts equilibrium forward for endothermic reactions, backward for exothermic reactions.
  • Increased pressure shifts the reaction in the direction that lowers pressure.

11MCQ Traps & Assessment Facts

MCQ Trap Chemical kinetics deals with ALL of: rate of reaction, speed of reactant→product conversion, and order of reaction — a common "all of the above" answer.
MCQ Trap Corrosion (tarnishing of silver, rusting of iron, green coating on copper/bronze) is an example of a slow reaction.
MCQ Trap Mixing NaCl + AgNO₃ solutions is an instantaneous (fast) reaction — too quick to measure a meaningful reaction rate.
MCQ Trap If activation energy is zero, the rate constant is independent of temperature.
MCQ Trap A catalyst's function: increases the rate of reaction (does not change equilibrium amount or become a final product).
MCQ Trap A positive catalyst increases the rate of reaction (this is its defining role, distinct from just "increasing yield" or "purity").
MCQ Trap When reactant concentration equals product concentration in a reaction, this state is called chemical equilibrium (not simply "reversible reaction" — equilibrium is the specific balanced state).
MCQ Trap In the presence of a catalyst: activation energies of both forward AND backward reactions are lowered by the same amount; rates of forward/reverse reactions increase by the same factor; equilibrium amount is undisturbed — all of these are simultaneously true.
MCQ Trap "Reactions with GREATER activation energy will be faster" is FALSE — reactions with SMALLER activation energy are faster (Statement II is the true one, not Statement I).
MCQ Trap "Catalyst increases forward reaction rate" (true) AND "Catalyst decreases activation energy of the reactant" (true, and this correctly EXPLAINS the first statement) — both correct, II explains I.
MCQ Trap In homogeneous reactions: reactants are in the same physical state; reaction proceeds forward when reactant concentration exceeds product concentration; ALL three given statements about homogeneous reactions can be correct together — verify each part rather than assuming a partial match.
MCQ Trap Statement "reactants react rapidly in presence of sunlight" is TRUE for some reactions (e.g. chloroform oxidation); Statement "Neutralisation is an example of irreversible reaction" needs independent verification — read each numbered statement on its own merit before selecting combined-statement options.
