The Bengal Renaissance & the Brahmo Samaj Family

Raja Ram Mohan Roy → Atmiya Sabha → Brahmo Sabha/Samaj → Tattvabodhini Sabha → the 1866 & 1878 splits
Every fact on Roy, Tagore, Sen and the Brahmo family — GC (157 Qs) + BPSC Wallah theory merged
EntityFacts (GC PYQ facts + Theory facts combined, duplicates merged)
Raja Ram Mohan Roy
(1772–1833)
  • Called Father of Indian Renaissance, Prophet of Indian Nationalism, Father of Modern India, First Modern Man, Yugdoot, Bridge between past and future, Maker of Modern India, Father of Indian Nationalism (no single person is officially "Father of Indian Nationalism" per UPRO/ARO Mains 2013 — trap)
  • 1815: set up Atmiya Sabha in Calcutta — first society he founded — to propagate monotheistic ideals of Hindu religion among rich/influential youth followers
  • 1828 (20 Aug 1828): founded Brahmo Sabha (later renamed Brahmo Samaj), by renting a house from Feringhee Kamal Bose; Tarachand Chakroborty was its first secretary
  • Brahmo Samaj principles: monotheism, opposed idolatry, sacerdotalism, anthropomorphism; preached universal worship of one omnipresent/omniscient God; denied need for priestly class; emphasised reason; upheld Vedas/Upanishads; critiqued Sati; promoted human dignity/social reform (did NOT popularise Vedas-as-infallible — that was Dayanand Saraswati, trap)
  • Mughal Emperor Akbar II gave him the title 'Raja' in 1830, sent him to court of British King William IV as ambassador to negotiate increment of Akbar II's pension
  • Died 27 Sept 1833 at Stapleton near Bristol, England (cause: meningitis); his Samadhi/mausoleum is at Bristol (not Kolkata/Patna/Canada — trap)
  • Advocated English/Western education — opposed the Sanskrit-school system in an 11 Dec 1823 letter to Lord Amherst, favouring maths, natural philosophy, chemistry, anatomy; did not protest Western education (he protested Sati, child marriage, idolatry — trap on "what did he NOT protest")
  • Opposed idol worship, Child marriage, and the practice of Sati
  • Strongly opposed Sati; with Governor-General Lord William Bentinck, got it declared illegal via Regulation XVII of 1829 (Bengal Sati Regulation) — called "murder according to every shastra"
  • Editor/founder context of Samvad Kaumudi — launched 4 Dec 1821 as a weekly; officially edited by Bhavanicharan Bandyopadhyay though Roy's paper (Roy's own name was not in the credits — trap); championed abolition of Sati; also linked to journal Mirat-ul-Akbar
  • Samachar Chandrika (1822), by Bhavani Charan Bandyopadhyay (earlier editor of Samvad Kaumudi), was started in opposition to Roy's religious/social ideas
  • Founded Calcutta Unitarian Committee (with Dwarkanath Tagore and William Adam) — NOT Keshab Chandra Sen (trap — Sen founded Tabernacle of New Dispensation & Indian Reform Association instead)
  • Strongly opposed the Jury Act of 1826, which gave only Christians the right to sit as jurors in trials of Christians
  • Had genuine respect for traditional Eastern/Indian philosophical systems and simultaneously wanted rational/scientific approach + human dignity + social equality for men and women — both are true (IAS Pre 2025)
  • Ideological foundations (theory): Rationalism — promoted reason, advocated widow remarriage, opposed child marriage and polygamy; Religious Universalism (shared with Syed Ahmed Khan, Keshub Chandra Sen) — emphasised unity/equality, criticised caste discrimination
Devendranath Tagore
(Debendranath Tagore)
  • 1839: founded Tattvabodhini Sabha in Calcutta to propagate rational thinking, study ancient texts (Upanishads), and spread Ram Mohan Roy's ideals; merged into Brahmo Samaj in 1859
  • Theory objectives: response to the expansion of Christianity in India, promote Vedantism and indigenous culture, develop the Bengali language; notable followers included Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar and Akshay Kumar Dutt; key publication: Tattvabodhini Patrika
  • Joined Brahmo Samaj in 1843; appointed Keshab Chandra Sen as Acharya of Brahmo Samaj, then stripped him of that title in 1865 over differences of opinion
  • After the 1866 split, his branch became known as Adi Brahmo Samaj (he is its founder — NOT Keshab Chandra Sen, who founded the rival Bhartiya Brahmo Samaj — commonly mis-attributed, trap)
Keshab Chandra Sen
(Keshub Chandra Sen)
  • Joined Brahmo Samaj in 1857; made the movement popular, established branches in UP and Madras
  • 11 Nov 1866: formally founded Bhartiya Brahmo Samaj (also called "Brahmo Samaj of India" / "Neo Brahmo Samaj") after a rift with Devendranath Tagore — historians have sometimes wrongly labelled this "Adi Brahmo Samaj"; the true Adi Brahmo Samaj stayed with Tagore
  • Associated with founding the Tabernacle of New Dispensation and the Indian Reform Association (29 Oct 1870, as its president) — NOT the Calcutta Unitarian Committee
  • 1872: played a key role in passing the Native Marriage Act (Act III of 1872 — also called the Special Marriage Act 1872 / Brahmo Marriage Act 1872 / Civil Marriage Act 1872), fixing minimum marriage age at 14 for girls, 18 for boys
  • 1878: married his under-14-year-old daughter to the king of Coonch Behar, contradicting his own reform stance — led Anand Mohan Bose and Shivnath Shastri to break away and form the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj
  • Reform programme: abolition of child marriage, polygamy, widow remarriage, inter-caste marriage; campaigned for women's education under his leadership
  • Theory: led the "Brahmo Samaj of India" with a broader, organised structure; journal — Indian Mirror; also founded Veda Samaj (1864, Madras, with Sridharalu Naidu) and helped Atmaram Pandurang found Prarthana Samaj (1867, Bombay)
  • Indian Reform Association objectives: build public consensus against child marriages, elevate social status of women, advocate legalisation of Brahmo-tradition marriages
Sadharan Brahmo Samaj
  • Formed by Anand Mohan Bose and Shivnath Shastri after Keshab Chandra Sen's 1878 child-marriage controversy
  • Theory: seceded from Sen's group, emphasised a more inclusive approach; journals — Tattva Kaumudi, The Indian Messenger, The Sanjibani, Navyabharat, Prabasi
Atmiya Sabha
  • Founded 1815, Calcutta — first society founded by Raja Ram Mohan Roy, to propagate monotheistic Hindu ideals among influential youth

Arya Samaj & Dayanand Saraswati

Dayanand Saraswati and Arya Samaj — every fact merged from GC + theory
EntityFacts
Swami Dayanand Saraswati
(born Mulshankar / Mool Shankar; also spelt Mula Shankar Tiwari)
  • Born in old Morvi Princely State, Kathiyawara, Gujarat, into a rich Brahmin family (birth year given as 1824 in one source)
  • 1845–1860 (fifteen years): wandered various places in search of knowledge
  • 1860: came under Swami Virajananda Saraswati at Mathura; started preaching in 1864
  • Nov 1869: engaged in a major Shastrartha with Hindu orthodoxy leaders at Kashi
  • 1867: hoisted the "Pakhand Khandini Pataka" on the bank of the Ganga to enlighten common people against superstition and bad rituals
  • 1875: founded Arya Samaj in Bombay (theory: April 1875)
  • Wrote Satyartha Prakash (also spelt Satyarth Prakash) in 1875 — his masterpiece/magnum opus; also wrote Rigvedadibhashyabhumika, Rigveda Bhashya, Advaitmat Khandan, Panch Mahayajya Vidhi, Ved-Viruddh-mat-Khandanam — sacred book of Arya Samaj (not written by Vivekananda/Tilak/other saints — a repeated trap)
  • 1877: Arya Samaj HQ shifted to Lahore, accelerating the movement's spread
  • Slogan: "Go Back to the Vedas" — call for revival of Vedic learning and religious purity; identified Hinduism with the religion instituted in the Vedas
  • Rejected idol worship, incarnations, tantra-mantra; denied authenticity of the Puranas (blamed them for idolatry); did a logical, scientific, critical analysis of faith
  • Aim: oneness of all religions, society, and the nation
  • Called "The (Martin) Luther of India" / "Martin Luther King of India" for his religious-reform efforts
  • First to use the word 'Swaraj'; first to advocate Hindi as India's official/national language; first to suggest renunciation of foreign goods and adoption of indigenous (Swadeshi) goods
  • According to Annie Besant, first person to say "India is only for the Indians" (framed as his own political message against colonial cultural submergence)
  • Theory: rejected rituals, Brahminical dominance, idol worship, superstitions; promoted social reform, women's rights, social equality; challenged untouchability and caste; used Hindi to reach Northern India; established Dayanand Anglo-Vedic (DAV) schools
Arya Samaj
  • Founded 1875, Bombay, by Dayanand Saraswati; spread rapidly as a reaction against Western influence; HQ shifted to Lahore in 1877
  • Later split into two groups: pro-Western-education vs anti-Western-education (trap) —
    • Opposed Western education: Swami Sraddhanand (Mahatma Munshi Ram Vij) and Lekhraj; established the Gurukul in 1902
    • Supported Western education: Lala Lajpat Rai and Hansraj; established Dayanand Anglo Vedic (DAV) College
  • Supported the Shuddhi Movement (re-converting people back into Hinduism)
  • Gave Hindus self-confidence and self-reliance; undermined belief in superiority of the White Race, while itself asserting the superiority of Hindus (both statements true, but the second does not explain the first — AR trap)
  • Lala Lajpat Rai was a member and did not oppose Arya Samaj's appeal to Vedic authority (a commonly tested false statement)
  • Contributed significantly to development of education and knowledge among followers

The Ramakrishna Movement

Ramakrishna Paramahamsa, Vivekananda, and Ram Krishna Mission — merged facts
EntityFacts
Ramakrishna Paramahamsa
(Gadadhar Chattopadhyay)
  • Emphasised that Hari, Ram, Allah, and Christ are different names for the same God and that there are many ways to reach God/salvation
  • Married to Sharadamani (also known as Sharada Devi) at her age of five, in 1859
  • Guru of Swami Vivekananda (born Narendranath Dutta)
Swami Vivekananda
(Narendranath Dutta)
  • Spread Ramakrishna's message and reconciled it to the needs of contemporary Indian society; emerged as the preacher of Neo-Hinduism — considered its best representative in the second half of the 19th century
  • 1893: delivered his famous speech at the Parliament of the World's Religions, Chicago — gave a short introduction of Hinduism, spoke on "The meaning of the Hindu religion"; represented India there (not Dayanand, Ramakrishna, or Roy — frequently confused option set, trap)
  • Subhash Chandra Bose said: "So far as Bengal is concerned, Vivekananda may be regarded as the spiritual father of the Modern nationalist movement"
  • 1897: founded Ram Krishna Mission (officially registered 1909 under the Societies Registration Act 1860); HQ at Belur, Howrah, West Bengal (also has a centre at Mayavati)
  • Advocated 'Practical Vedanta'; gave his first speech on it on 10 Nov 1896 in London
  • Wrote Jnana Yoga, Karma Yoga, Raja Yoga (and Bhakti Yoga)
  • Quote: "Truth can be stated in a thousand different ways, yet each one can be true."
  • Theory: objectives — rejuvenation of Hinduism through Vedanta and ancient texts; opposed caste restrictions, oppression, superstitions; focused on uplifting women and restructuring education; promoted renunciation and practical spirituality through the Ramakrishna Math and Mission; advocated service to humanity as devotion to God
  • Key concepts: "As many faiths, so many paths" (oneness of religions); "Service of man is the service of God"
Ram Krishna Mission
  • Founded 1897; registered 1909 under Societies Registration Act 1860; HQ at Belur, Howrah (West Bengal)
  • Objective: non-political, non-sectarian spiritual movement engaged in humanitarian, social and cultural service, inspired by ancient Indian culture; ideal is the Theory of Pure Vedanta
  • Accepted traditional Hindu practices (idol worship, rituals, symbols) in their original form — unlike Brahmo/Arya Samaj which rejected idolatry (trap: don't confuse RK Mission's idol-acceptance with Brahmo/Arya's idol-rejection)
  • Core belief: truth of all religions — all religious paths lead to the same one God
  • Considered the last famous religious movement of the 19th century, aiming at harmony of religions, of East and West, of the ancient and the modern, spiritual fulfilment, social equality and peace for all humanity without distinction of caste, creed, race or nationality
  • Math and Mission function as separate but complementary organisations

Maharashtra Reformers — Prarthana Samaj, Satyashodhak Samaj & Allies

Ranade, Phule, Karve, Lokahitwadi, Vishnu Shastri Pandit and their organisations — merged
EntityFacts
Prarthana Samaj
  • Founded 1867, Bombay, by Atmaram Pandurang, inspired by Keshab Chandra Sen (who helped found it)
  • Mahadev Govind Ranade joined in 1869 (theory says 1870), boosting its popularity
  • Aim: oppose the caste system, raise the marriage age for men and women, promote widow remarriage and women's education; disapproval of religious orthodoxy
  • Key mentors: M.G. Ranade, R.G. Bhandarkar; Dhondo Keshav Karve and Vishnu Shastri contributed to the Widow Remarriage Movement and the Widows' Home Association
  • Precursor: Paramahansa Sabha, which promoted liberal ideas and caste/community breakdown
Mahadev Govind Ranade
  • Joined Prarthana Samaj in 1869/1870; called "Precursor of the cultural Renaissance of Western India"
  • 1887: co-founded (with Raghunath Rao) the Indian National Social Conference — aim: abolish polygamy, child marriage and patriarchy/oligarchy (kulinism)
  • Relied on legislation to eradicate social ills like child marriage and the Purdah system; the Conference met for years alongside the annual INC sessions
  • Theory: INC did not want social reforms in its own deliberations, so this separate body was formed; it launched the 'Pledge Movement' against child marriage, advocated inter-caste marriage, opposed polygamy and kulinism
  • Co-founded the Deccan Education Society (1884, Pune) with Tilak, V.G. Chibdonkar, G.G. Agarkar — established Fergusson College, Pune (1885)
  • Gave support to Gokhale's Servants of India Society (1905)
Atmaram Pandurang
  • Founded Prarthana Samaj in 1867, after being inspired by Keshab Chandra Sen
Jyotirao (Jyotiba) Phule
& Savitribai Phule
  • Jyotiba born 1827 to a gardener family; founded Satya Shodhak Samaj (Satyashodhak Samaj) in 1873
  • Wrote the book Gulamgiri, criticising the "opportunistic Vedas" of the Brahmans and calling for protection of lower castes from exploitation
  • Samaj's objective: redeem Shudras/Ati-Shudras from Brahmanical scriptural influence, make them conscious of human rights, liberate them from mental and religious slavery; an anti-caste movement in Maharashtra
  • Theory: leadership drawn from Malis, Telis, Kunbis; rejected Lord Rama as a symbol, promoted Rajah Bali; vision — abolition of caste, equality, human dignity; political engagement via the Poona Municipal Committee (1876)
  • Savitribai Phule headed the women's section of the Samaj, working for lower-caste girls' welfare; also taught alongside Fatima Sheikh and Sagunabai, and ran a night school
  • Together opened the first girls' school in India, at Bhide Wada, Pune, in 1848; ran a widow-remarriage movement and a widow shelter (1854)
  • The women's movement in India is said to have started largely under Jyotiba Phule's inspiration — he believed social evils could only be eliminated after uplifting women and Dalits
Gopal Hari Deshmukh
("Lokahitwadi")
  • Social reformer of Maharashtra (1823–92); popularly known as Lokahitwadi
  • Member of the Governor-General's Council in 1880
  • From age 25, wrote articles for social reform in the weekly Prabhakar under the pen-name Lokahitvadi; wrote 108 articles in the first two years, later compiled as 'Lokahitawadinchi Shatapatre'
  • Appeared in the Delhi court wearing hand-woven Khadi clothes, as a supporter of national self-reliance
Vishnu Shastri Pandit
(Vishnu Parashuram Pandit)
  • Social reformer and advocate of widow remarriage in 19th-century Maharashtra; in the 1850s founded the 'Punarvivahottejak Mandali' (also called the Widow Remarriage Association) to promote widow remarriages
  • Published a Marathi translation of Vidyasagar's book in Indu Prakash in 1868
  • Led the campaign for widow remarriage in Maharashtra
D.K. Karve
(Dhondo Keshav Karve)
  • Championed widow remarriage in Western India; married a widow himself in 1893; became secretary of the Widow Remarriage Association
  • Established a Widows' Home in Pune (1896) for vocational training
  • 1916: founded the Indian Women's University in Bombay (India's first women's university)
  • Founded the Nishkam Karma Math ("Monastery of Disinterested Work") in 1910, Pune — established the women's university in Pune, now part of Mumbai University; emphasised women's education and self-reliance

Theosophical Society & Annie Besant

Theosophical Society, Blavatsky, Olcott, Annie Besant — merged
EntityFacts
Theosophical Society
  • Founded by Madame H.P. Blavatsky (1831–91, Russian/Ukrainian noblewoman), Colonel H.S. Olcott (1832–1907, American army officer) and others, in 1875 in New York (theory adds William Quan Judge as a co-founder)
  • 1882: HQ shifted from New York to Adyar, on the outskirts of Madras (moved there from 1879 onward), which became its International Headquarters
  • 1889: Annie Besant became a member; 1893: she arrived in India and played the most active role for the Society
  • Believed Hinduism to be the most spiritual and esoteric religion; theory adds it was inspired by the Upanishads, Vedanta and ancient philosophies; aimed at religious revival and social reform, advocating revival of Hinduism, Zoroastrianism and Buddhism; recognised transmigration of the soul; preached universal brotherhood; promoted women's advancement; fought untouchability
Annie Besant
  • Came into contact with the Theosophical Society in 1882, became its official member in 1889, arrived in India in 1893; became its chairman/president after Col. Olcott's death in 1907
  • The Society's success in India was mainly attributed to her; she believed Hinduism was the world's most esoteric/spiritual religion and believed in the principle of rebirth
  • Led the Home Rule Movement/League (1916)
  • Theory: founded the Indian Women's Association (1917, Madras) — worked for the upliftment of Indian women via annual conferences (leading to the All India Women's Conference); advocated women's rights and social reforms

Other Reform Organisations

Dev Samaj, Dharma Sabha, Radhaswami Satsang, Servants of India Society, Bahujan Samaj — merged
EntityFacts
Dev Samaj
  • Founded by Shiv Narayan Agnihotri (Bhagwan Devatma) at Lahore in 1887; a former follower of Brahmo Samaj
  • Teachings compiled in the book Dev Shastra
  • Theory: closely aligned with Brahmo Samaj philosophy; advocated righteous living, moral behaviour, spiritual discipline; social code opposed bribery, gambling, alcohol, non-vegetarian food; promoted non-violence and ethical living; strong opposition to child marriage
  • Popularity ended in 1913 after Shiv Narayana appointed his second son as spiritual successor
Dharma Sabha
  • Founded by Radhakant Deb (Radhakanta Deb, 1794–1876) in 1830 at Calcutta (Bengal)
  • Opposed socio-religious reforms; supported orthodox elements; opposed the abolition of Sati
  • Yet Radhakant Deb also played an active role in promoting Western education among girls
  • Theory: countered Brahmo Samaj, opposed radical/liberal reforms, but promoted Western education and celebrated Indian culture's importance and pride
Radhasoami / Radhaswami Satsang
  • Began at Agra in 1861; founding member Shiv Dayal Singh (Soamiji / Swamiji Maharaj / "Tulsi Ram" per theory) — originally a Persian-language translator, later joined his father's moneylending business
  • Followers believed in one god, the greatness of the teacher (guru), Satsang (community of pious individuals), and ordinary social life
Servants of India Society
  • Founded by Gopal Krishna Gokhale in 1905 at Fergusson Hill, Pune district, Maharashtra (with support from M.G. Ranade); co-founded with 3 friends: Natesh Appaji Dravid, Gopal Krishna Deodhar, Anant Vinayak Patwardhan — all lifelong members
  • Gokhale chaired the 21st Session of Congress in 1905 (Banaras/Varanasi); was the political guru of Mahatma Gandhi; K.M. Panikkar called him "the first diplomat of modern India"
  • Objectives: famine relief, welfare of tribals, economic upliftment/employment for women, training "national missionaries" using constitutional means; publication Hitavada (started 1911)
  • After Gokhale's death in 1915, Srinivasa Shastri became president
  • Distinguished members: M.C. Setalvad, B.N. Rao, Alladi Krishnaswamy Iyer, V. Srinivas Shastri, G.K. Devdhar, N.M. Joshi, Pandit Hridayalnath Kunzuru
  • First secular organisation devoted to unprivileged rural and tribal peoples
Bahujan Samaj
  • Founded 1910 by Mukund Rao Patil and Shankar Rao Jadhav, under the influence of Jyotiba Phule
  • Aim: oppose exploitation of lower castes by upper castes, moneylenders and landlords; also opposed the INC; later became a supporter of the British Government
Indian National Social Conference
  • Founded 1887, Bombay, by M.G. Ranade and Raghunath Rao
  • Aim: abolish polygamy, child marriage, patriarchy; served as the social-reform wing of the INC, since Congress itself declined to include social reform in its own deliberations
  • Launched the 'Pledge Movement' against child marriage; advocated inter-caste marriage; opposed polygamy and kulinism

Women's Issues & Reform Legislation

Sati, infanticide, widow remarriage, child marriage, age of consent, women's education — merged
Issue / ActFacts
Abolition of Sati
  • 1829: Bengal Sati Regulation / Regulation XVII of 1829, passed by Governor-General Lord William Bentinck, made Sati illegal, terming it "murder according to every shastra"; achieved through the efforts of Raja Ram Mohan Roy
  • Historical precedents: restrictions on Sati seen earlier under the Portuguese, Dutch and French in the 16th century; Mughal emperor Akbar issued orders against coerced Sati in 1582
Ban on Infanticide
  • A grave practice especially among Bengalis and Rajputs, driven by economic concerns and social pressures
  • Bengal Regulations of 1795 (Regulation XXI) and 1804 (Act III) classified infanticide as murder to discourage the practice
  • Female Infanticide Prevention Act, 1870: required parents to record every birth and verify female children, to curb covert infanticide
Slavery abolished
  • Act V of 1843 made slavery illegal across India, during the tenure of Governor-General Lord Ellenborough; a clause in the Charter Act of 1833 had called for ending slavery; all slaves were freed without compensation to owners
Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856
(Act XV of 1856)
  • Passed 25 July 1856, through the efforts of Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, Principal of Sanskrit College
  • Vidyasagar researched ancient Hindu Shastras/Dharmashastras and demonstrated widow remarriage was permitted by Vedic scripture; petitioned the government with 987 signatories
  • Legalised widow remarriage and recognised children from such unions as legitimate — though its practical impact was limited in Bengal
  • Other reformers who promoted widow remarriage and women's rights: Veeresalingam Pantulu (Madras), Jagannath Shankar Seth, Bhau Daji, Vishnu Shastri Pandit, Karsondas Mulji, B.M. Malabari, Narmad Dave, Justice Govind Mahadeo Ranade, K. Natarajan, Jyotiba Phule, Savitribai Phule
Native Marriage Act, 1872
(Civil Marriage Act / Brahmo Marriage Act)
  • Keshab Chandra Sen played the key role in its passage; fixed minimum marriage age at 14 for girls, 18 for boys (one source: min age of girls placed at 14)
  • Legalised unorthodox inter-caste marriages and banned polygamy
  • Theory: prohibited marriage for girls below 18 and boys below 21 — but did NOT apply to Hindus, Muslims or other recognised faiths, limiting its reach (the two figures — 14/18 in GC vs 18/21 in theory — reflect different provisions/sources; both appear in PYQs, watch for the exact figure asked)
Age of Consent Act, 1891
  • Passed through the efforts of Behramji M. Malabari, a Parsi social reformer born 1853 at Baroda; editor of Indian Spectator and Voice of India; his activism also led to the Criminal Law Amendment Act, 1885
  • Forbade marriage of girls below age 12 (theory)
  • Bal Gangadhar Tilak opposed this Act (trap: the Extremist faction under Tilak did NOT support it)
  • Linked to Rukhmabai Raut's case: India's first female physician and a child-marriage reformer; married at 11 to Dadaji Bhikaji, contested her marriage's validity; lost her appeal in 1887; public outcry led Queen Victoria to intervene and spare her imprisonment; the Rukhmabai Defence Committee (with Malabari and Ramabai Ranade) publicised her case, inspiring this Act
  • Malabari also founded Seva Sadan (1885, Bombay, with Diwan Dayaram Gidumal) — against child marriage, promoting widow remarriage, supporting exploited/abandoned women with education, medical care and welfare services across castes
Sharda Act / Child Marriage Restraint Act, 1929
  • Enacted through the efforts of Indian judge Dr. Har Bilas Sharda; fixed minimum marriage age at 14 for girls, 18 for boys; effective from 1930
  • Amended in 1978 (Child Marriage Restraint Amendment Act), raising the age to 18 for girls, 21 for boys — later substituted by the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006
  • Present-day marriage age: 18 years for girls, 21 years for boys
  • Muthulakshmi Reddi, first woman legislator in Chennai's Legislative Council (theory table), played a crucial role advocating for this Act
Women's Education
  • 19th-century resistance stemmed from the belief that female education was contrary to Hindu scriptures and would cause widowhood
  • Bethune School (1849), Calcutta — founded by J.E.D. Bethune; Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar served as its first Secretary (the institution originally called the Hindu Female School, later renamed Bethune Female School)
  • Vidyasagar was instrumental in establishing girls' schools across Bengal and in challenging the misconception that female education contradicted Hindu scriptures
  • Charles Wood's Despatch (1854), supported by Lord Dalhousie, highlighted the need for educating women as part of broader educational reform
  • Prominent advocates: Jagannath Shankarsheth (founder of School Society & Native School of Bombay, opened English-Marathi school in Girgaum); Bhau Daji Lad (first Indian president of Students' Literary and Scientific Society); Jyotirao & Savitribai Phule (first girls' school, Bhide Wada, Pune, 1848; taught with Fatima Sheikh and Sagunabai; ran a night school); Cornelia Sorabji (first woman to graduate from Bombay University, 1887; advocated women's higher education)

Caste Reform Movements

Satyashodhak Samaj, Nadars, Justice Party, Self-Respect Movement, Ambedkar — merged (see also section 4 for Phule)
EntityFacts
Satya Shodhak Samaj
(see also §4 Phule)
  • Founded 1873 by Jyotiba Phule; organised an anti-caste movement in Maharashtra; liberation of Shudras and Ati-Shudras from exploitation by Arya-Brahmins was its main objective
  • Savitribai Phule headed the women's section; Deenbandhu played an important role in spreading the movement to remote parts of Maharashtra
  • Main aim: upliftment of backward classes and Dalits; wrote 'Sarvjanik Satyadharm Pustak' and 'Gulamgiri' to spread anti-Brahmin ideas
Nadars
  • Demanded the right to enter temples, leading to riots in Tirunelveli in 1899
  • Theory (Nadar Movement, 1910, Tamil Nadu): origin as Shanans, an untouchable caste of agricultural labourers; by the late 19th century became a prosperous mercantile class and adopted the title 'Nadars' to claim Kshatriya status
  • Key organisation: Nadar Mahajan Sangam (established 1910)
Justice Party / Movement
[1916, Madras]
  • Founders: Dr. T.M. Nair, P. Tyagaraja Chetti, C.N. Mudaliar; the "South Indian Liberal Federation (SILF)" formed in 1916 to challenge Brahmin dominance in government service, education and politics
  • Opposed the Congress by calling it a Brahmin-dominated organisation; claimed the same communal representation for non-Brahmins as Muslims received under the Morley-Minto reforms
  • Led to reservations for various communities via government orders; first non-Brahmin political organisation in South India
  • E.V. Ramaswami Naicker (Periyar) became President in 1937, spearheading anti-Brahminism and social reform; criticised Hinduism, the Laws of Manu, and the Puranas as inhumane; removed caste name-boards from hotels, desecrated Hindu idols, cut sacred threads worn by Brahmins; motto: "Some things cannot be mended, only ended."
  • Evolution: renamed Dravida Kazhagam in 1944; became the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (DMK) in 1949 under C.N. Annadurai
Self-Respect Movement
[1925, Tamil Nadu]
  • Founded by E.V. Ramaswami Naicker (Periyar); goal — reject Brahminical customs, challenge caste hierarchy, promote rationalism
  • Criticised Brahmin priests as agents of oppression; advocated alternative wedding ceremonies and women's liberation; mobilised marginalised communities against caste injustice
  • Associated journal: Kudi (1910); impact — reshaped Tamil Nadu's social fabric, formed the Justice Party, pushed egalitarianism and social justice
B.R. Ambedkar
  • Advocated 'Neo-Buddhism' — the Dalit Bauddha Movement, redefining Buddhism and founding the new Buddhist sect 'Navayan'
  • Founded the Bahishkrit Hitkarini Sabha (frequently mismatched to Jyotiba Phule in "not correctly matched" questions — trap)
  • Theory (Depressed Classes/Mahars Movement, 1924, Maharashtra): objective — elevate the Mahar community (untouchables); key demands — access to public places, temple entry, abolition of Mahar Watan, separate legislative representation
  • 1927: Bahiskrit Bharat Samaj Samta Sangh initiated by the Depressed Classes Institution (a Marathi fortnightly); 1942: Caste Federation formed to advocate untouchables' rights
Congress' Harijan Movement / All India Anti-Untouchability League
  • Mahatma Gandhi founded the All India Anti-Untouchability League in 1932 (later renamed Harijan Sevak Sangh)
  • Theory: key leaders — Gandhi, Madan Mohan Malviya, Jamnalal Bajaj; 1917: Congress passed a resolution advocating abolition of untouchability; 1929: Anti-Untouchability Sub-Committee formed within Congress, led by Malviya and Bajaj; journal — Harijan, published by Gandhi
Tilak on untouchability
  • At a conference organised on abolition of untouchability, Bal Gangadhar Tilak (called Lokmanya or Betaj Badshah) said: "If God were to tolerate untouchability, I would not recognize him as God at all."

Muslim Reform Movements

Deoband, Faraizi, Aligarh — merged
EntityFacts
Darul Uloom Deoband
(Deoband Movement)
  • Founding members: Muhammad Qasim Nanautawi, Haji Syed Muhammad Abid Deobandi, Mahtab Ali Deobandi, Zulfiqar Ali Deobandi, Fazlur Rahman Usmani Deobandi, Haji Fazle Haq Deobandi, Shaikh Nihal Deobandi, and others
  • Foundation laid 31 May 1866 at Deoband, Saharanpur district, UP (United Province); objective — overthrow British rule, restore Islamic rule
  • Theory: co-founded by Muhammad Qasim Nanaytavi (1832–80) and Rashid Ahmad Gangohi (1828–1905); other key figures — Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Mahmud-ul-Hasan, Shibli Numani
  • A revivalist movement focused on Quranic teachings, rejecting Western education, advocating a liberal interpretation of Islam; supported the INC; issued a 1888 fatwa against Syed Ahmed Khan's associations (note: Deoband rejected Western education while Aligarh embraced it — commonly contrasted, trap)
  • Mahmud-ul-Hasan fused Islamic principles with nationalism; Jamiat-ul-Ulema upheld Muslim rights; Shibli Numani supported English, European sciences and Hindu-Muslim unity
Dar-ul-Uloom, Nadwatul Ulama
(Lucknow — distinct from Deoband)
  • Established 1898 at Lucknow, by Nadwatul Ulma; the underlying Nadwatul Uloom body was established by Maulana Luftullah and Maulana Shibli Numani at Kanpur in 1893; Shibli Numani taught at this Dar-ul-Uloom (easily confused with Darul Uloom Deoband — trap)
Haji Shariatullah
(Faraizi Movement)
  • Began the Faraizi Movement; a movement against British rule, which had denied Muslims religious freedom and confiscated their rights
  • Theory: 1818–19, East Bengal; later led by his son Dudu Mian; initial focus — reinterpretation of Islam, eradicating un-Islamic practices; after 1840 (post British conquest) it transformed into a revolutionary movement
  • Dudu Mian organised a paramilitary force against Hindu landlords and Indigo planters; built an organisational structure from village to provincial level, with khalifa (authorised deputies) overseeing each region
Syed Ahmed Khan
(Aligarh Movement, 1875)
  • Founded the Muhammadan-Anglo Oriental College, Aligarh (later AMU); founder dates 1817–98
  • Promoted religious reform through rational inquiry, emphasising a scientific/rational outlook; recognised the value of Western education and aimed to socially reform Muslims
  • Founded the Scientific Society in 1864; associated journal: Tahziz-al-Akhlaq (Urdu)
  • Deoband issued a fatwa against his associations in 1888, reflecting the Deoband–Aligarh ideological rivalry on Western education

BPSC Wallah Exclusive — Sikh, Parsi & Additional Muslim Movements

Sikh reform movements, Parsi reform, and Muslim movements not covered in Ghatna Chakra
EntityFacts
Wahabi Movement
[1820s onward — Rohilkhand, Kabul, NWFP, Bengal, Central Provinces]
  • Founder: Syed Ahmad of Rai Bareilly; other leaders — Vilayat Ali, Shah Muhammad Hussain, Farhat Hussain, Inayat Ali (all from Patna)
  • Inspired by Abdul Wahab (Arabia) and Shah Waliullah (1702–63)
  • Objective: revive authentic Islam and Arabian culture as in the Prophet's time; opposed Western influence on Islam; emphasised individual conscience
  • Initially directed against the Sikhs in Punjab, later shifted against the British after Punjab's annexation (1849); played a significant role in the 1857 Revolt
Titu Mir's Movement [1831]
  • Leader: Syed Mir Nisar Ali (Titu Mir), a disciple of Syed Ahmad Barelvi and follower of Wahabism
  • Advocated Sharia principles in Bengal; organised Muslim peasants against Hindu landlords and British indigo planters
  • Confrontation with British police in 1831 resulted in his death
Taayuuni Movement [Dacca, 1839]
  • Leader: Karamat Ali Jaunpuri; founded on the teachings of Shah Waliullah
  • Opposed the Faraizi Movement — a more traditionalist religious stance without Faraizi's revolutionary/social-reform goals
Mohammedan Literary Society of Calcutta [1863]
  • Leader: Abdul Latif
  • Aim: promote modern education and scientific knowledge among Muslims; eradicate practices like polygamy among upper/middle-class Muslims
  • Played a pivotal role in fostering Hindu-Muslim unity
Ahmadiyya Movement [1889]
  • Founder: Mirza Ghulam Ahmad (1839–1908)
  • Promoted human rights, tolerance, separation of mosque and state; opposed jihad; favoured Western education
  • Mirza Ghulam Ahmad claimed to be the Messiah, ending religious wars; criticised for mystical elements akin to the Bahai faith
  • The only Islamic sect holding that the Messiah has already arrived, in the form of Mirza Ghulam Ahmad
Namdhari / Kuka Movement [1841–71, NWFP, Bhain]
  • Founders: Bhai Balak Singh and Baba Ram Singh
  • Religio-political revival within Sikhism, emphasising worship of one God via prayer/meditation per the Rehatnamas; opposed the caste system, infanticide, early marriage; promoted Anand Marriage ceremonies
Singh Sabha Movement [1873]
  • Founders: Thakur Singh Sandhawalia and Giani Gian Singh
  • Aimed to re-establish original-form Sikhism via religious literature, promoting Punjabi, reintegrating Sikh apostates; established Khalsa schools across Punjab; countered Christian missionaries, Brahmo Samaj, Arya Samaj, Muslim maulvis
Akali Movement [1920–25]
  • Offshoot of the Singh Sabha Movement; aimed to liberate Sikh gurudwaras from corrupt, hereditary Udasi mahants
  • 1922: Sikh Gurdwaras Act passed (amended 1925), granting gurudwara control to the Sikh masses via the Shiromani Gurudwara Prabandhak Committee (SGPC)
  • Non-violent, non-cooperation movement launched 1921 — regional but not communal
Nirankari Movement [1851]
  • Founder: Baba Dayal Das
  • Opposed idol-worship and veneration of tombs; rejected the militant Khalsa brotherhood; accepted living Gurus as spiritual leaders; simplified/standardised birth, health and marriage rituals
Rehnumai Mazdayasan Sabha [1851]
(also referenced in GC — see below)
  • Founders: Dadabhai Naoroji, Nauroji Furdonji, S.S. Bengalee
  • Aimed to reform orthodox Parsi practices — modernising social customs, improving women's education, reforming marriage; advocated restoring Zoroastrianism's purity, uniform inheritance/marriage laws
  • GC: same body founded 1851 by Naoroji Furdonji and others, described as a Parsi religious reform association

BPSC Wallah Exclusive — Additional Caste Movements

Caste-reform movements with no matching Ghatna Chakra question/option
EntityFacts
Sree Narayana Guru Dharma Paripalana (SNDP) Movement
[1903, Kerala]
  • Founder: Sree Narayana Guru (1854–1928); the Ezhavas (toddy-tappers) faced untouchability, denial of education and temple entry in Kerala
  • Catalyst: 1888 symbolic act at Aruvippuram, installing a Sivalinga from the Neyyar River to challenge caste practices
  • 1889: Aruvippuram Kshetra Yogam; 1903: SNDP Yogam registered under the Indian Companies Act, Guru as permanent chairman, Kumaran Asan as general secretary
  • Slogan: "One religion, one caste, one god for mankind"; criticised Congress and Gandhi for not addressing caste discrimination
  • Inscribed on the Aruvippuram temple wall: "No dividing walls of caste or race, live in brotherhood."
  • Promoted social mobility for Ezhavas in education, government jobs, temple entry, political representation; led to structural changes in Kerala's power dynamics
Nair Movement [1891, Kerala]
  • Key leaders: C.V. Raman Pillai, K. Rama Krishna Pillai, M. Padmanabha Pillai
  • Emerged against Brahmin dominance in Kerala; C.V. Raman Pillai established the Memorial to challenge Brahminical authority; M. Padmanabha Pillai founded the Nair Service Society (NSS) to uplift the Nair community
Vokkaliga Sangha [1905, Mysore]
  • Initiated as an anti-Brahmin movement in Mysore; T. Byanna was its first president
  • Patrons: Maharaja of Mysore Krishnaraja Wodeyar IV and Diwan V.P. Madhava Rao (patron and vice-patron respectively)
Vaikom Satyagraha [1924, Northern Travancore]
  • Leader: K.P. Kesava; objective — challenge denial of temple and public-road access to untouchables
  • Supported by Jathas (processions) from Punjab and Madurai; Mahatma Gandhi visited Kerala to show solidarity
Temple Entry Movement [1924–1938]
  • Guruvayur Satyagraha (1931): leaders K. Kelappan and poet Subramaniyam Tirumambu ("the singing sword of Kerala"); 16 volunteers marched to Guruvayur; notable satyagrahis — P. Krishna Pillai, A.K. Gopalan
  • Travancore Proclamation (1936): Maharaja of Travancore allowed all Hindu castes access to government-controlled temples
  • Temple Entry in Madras (1938): led by C. Rajagopalachari; Madras administration expanded the movement's influence across South India
Gopal Baba Walangkar Founded Anarya Dosh Parihar Samaj; published 'Vital Vidhwansak' to protest against Brahmanism.
Iyothee Thass (Tamil Nadu) Promoted Siddha medicine and Buddhist conversion; founded the Dalit Mahajan Sabha.
Kaivartas' Movement [1897 onward, Midnapore, Bengal] Establishment of the Jati Nirdharan Sabha (1897) and the Mahishya Samiti (1901) marked initial steps toward the Kaivartas' evolving into the cohesive Mahishya identity.
