Partition of Bengal (1905)

The spark that lit the whole decade
Partition of Bengal — full picture
Aspect Detail
Announced 20 July 1905 by Viceroy Lord Curzon (resolution itself signed 19 July 1905 — 63rd BPSC's answer key uses this earlier date).
Implemented 16 October 1905 (63rd BPSC).
Annulled 12 December 1911, at the Delhi Durbar, by King George V.
Two new provinces Western Bengal (with Bihar & Orissa), capital Calcutta; Eastern Bengal (with Assam), capital Dacca.
Official justification Bengal's population of 78 million made administration unwieldy; development of Assam also cited.
Lieutenant-Governor of Bengal at the time Sir Andrew Henderson Leith Fraser (1903–1908), who played a key role in planning the partition.
Real motive, per Curzon himself "Calcutta is the centre from which the Congress Party is manipulated throughout the whole of Bengal and indeed the whole of India... The whole of their activity is directed to creating an agency so powerful that they may one day be able to force a weak government to give them what they desire."

Real Motives

  • Weaken Indian nationalism — Bengal was the nerve-centre of the national movement; splitting it was meant to cripple that unity.
  • Linguistic and religious division — reduced Bengalis to a minority overall, created a Hindu-majority west and a Muslim-majority east.
  • Divide and rule — a deliberate strategy to foster communal division between Hindus and Muslims; making Dacca the capital of the Muslim-majority province was specifically meant to secure Muslim political support (Curzon's tactic).

Effects

  • Massive anti-partition agitation across Bengal.
  • Birth of the Swadeshi Movement — economic boycott of British goods.
  • A more aggressive, Extremist stance took root within the national movement.
  • Sowed the seeds of communal politics that led directly to the formation of the Muslim League (1906).
  • Surendranath Banerjee's reaction: "The Partition announcement fell like a bomb shell. We felt that we had been insulted, humiliated and tricked" (67th BPSC Re-exam).
Context — reconciling the "which date" confusion Three dates circle this event and PYQs test all three separately: 19 July 1905 (resolution signed), 20 July 1905 (public announcement — the date most PCS answer keys use), and 16 October 1905 (the partition actually took legal effect, observed as a day of mourning in Bengal — Rabindranath Tagore suggested this day be marked as Rakhi Diwas, tying Hindus and Muslims together symbolically with wristbands). Keep all three straight: announce → resolution → effect, spanning exactly three months.
2

The Swadeshi and Boycott Movement

Struggle methods used for the first time
  • Swadeshi and Boycott were adopted as methods of struggle for the first time in India's freedom movement during the agitation against the Partition of Bengal (66th, 68th, 63rd BPSC; I.A.S. 2016 confirms the same, ruling out the Home Rule Movement, Non-Cooperation, or the Simon Commission visit as the "first" instance).
  • Formal launch: 7 August 1905, at a mass meeting in Calcutta Town Hall, where the Boycott Resolution was passed — the actual formal proclamation of the Swadeshi Movement.
  • Krishna Kumar Mitra, editor of the weekly Sanjivani, was the first to suggest a boycott of British goods, in a special edition dated 13 July 1905 — urging people to mourn, boycott, and sever links with British officers/offices. His call was publicly endorsed at a gathering in Bagerhat (Khulna district) on 16 July 1905.
  • 16 October 1905 (the day partition took effect): Indians mourned by fasting, bathing in the Ganges, singing Bande Mataram, and tying rakhi unity bands to each other.
  • Immediate cause of the movement's launch: the Partition of Bengal itself (I.A.S. 2010) — not Tilak's imprisonment, Lajpat Rai/Ajit Singh's deportation, or the Chapekar brothers' sentencing, all distractor options in that PYQ.
  • 7 August is now also observed as National Handloom Day (first observed 2015) — deliberately chosen to commemorate the 1905 Swadeshi launch date (I.A.S. 2023 confirms this direct causal link between the two statements).

New Forms of Struggle

  • Boycott of foreign goods: burning foreign textiles, mass abstention from imported products.
  • Public gatherings/processions to mobilise and unify.
  • "Corps of Volunteers"/Samitis: local bodies like Swadesh Bandhab Samiti and Swadeshi Sangam spread ideas through cultural events and social work.
  • Festivals as propaganda: Tilak's Ganapati and Shivaji festivals became Swadeshi platforms.
  • Atma-Shakti (self-reliance): promoted self-reliance, national pride, and social reforms like fighting caste discrimination and dowry.
  • Swadeshi education: Bengal National College and National Schools; the National Council of Education (established 15 August 1906) focused on nationalist education — formulated originally by Satish Chandra Mukherjee in 1889.
  • Swadeshi enterprises: textile mills, the Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company, reducing dependency on British goods.

Cultural Impact

  • Rabindranath Tagore composed Amar Sonar Bangla during the movement — it later became Bangladesh's national anthem. Tagore, however, was also a critic of the movement's aggressive, exclusive nationalism and over-reliance on boycott — he emphasised better East-West understanding and later denounced parts of it in his essay "The Cult of the Charkha" (1925), and called British rule "a political symptom of our social disease."
  • Abanindranath Tagore painted the iconic "Bharat Mata" image (1905) — a four-armed goddess in a saffron sari — to visually personify India as a nation; he and brother Gaganendranath Tagore founded the Indian Society of Oriental Art (1907, initiated with E.B. Havell) to revive Indian art traditions. Nandalal Bose also significantly shaped Indian art through this Society.
  • Jagdish Chandra Bose showcased India's scientific potential during this era.
  • Desher Katha by S.G. Deuskar: summarised the economic-drain arguments of Ranade and Naoroji, warning against Britain's "hypnotic conquest of the mind," and inspired Swadeshi plays and songs.
  • British journalist H.W. Nevinson spent four months touring India reporting for the Manchester Guardian, Glasgow Herald, and Daily Chronicle; compiled his reports into the book The New Spirit in India.
  • Vande Mataram became the movement's theme song and the National Movement's overnight rallying anthem.

Extent & Limits of Mass Participation

  • Students led picketing against foreign goods, facing punitive action: disaffiliation, job disqualification, revoked scholarships, fines, expulsion, arrest, even physical punishment.
  • Women actively participated in protests, processions, and picketing — for the first time in any Indian political movement.
  • Farmers and Muslims (in general) showed limited participation — a class that was "mainly unaffected" (40th BPSC). Maulana Azad was an active exception among Muslim leaders, but many upper/middle-class Muslims stayed away, put off by Hindu-centric symbolism; Liyakat Hussain (contrary to one distractor option) did not lead Barisal's Muslim peasants — Ashwini Kumar Dutta did (I.A.S. 2002).
  • Nawab Salimullah actively backed the East Bengal partition, seeing it as favourable for Muslims.
  • The British encouraged the newly-formed All India Muslim League (1906) to deepen this communal division.
  • Strikes: Burn Company Clerks' Strike (1905, Howrah), East Indian Railway Strike (1906), Jute Mills Strikes (1906–1908), Rawalpindi Strike (led by Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh). Foreign mill strikes were led by Subramaniya Siva and Chidambaram Pillai.

Why the Movement Dissipated

  • Government repression, internal Moderate–Extremist divisions, and limited geographic reach beyond Bengal.
  • Muslim support was hindered by communal divisions and the rival All India Muslim League (1906).
  • Lack of central organisation, directionless energy, and communal riots.

Congress Sessions — Benaras, Calcutta & Surat (1905–07)

Where the Swadeshi debate split the party
Three pivotal Congress sessions
Session President Key developments
Benaras, 1905 Gopal Krishna Gokhale Moderate–Extremist differences first emerged over whether to extend the Swadeshi Movement nationwide; a deliberately mild resolution condemning the partition was passed to avert an immediate split. Gokhale also founded the Servants of India Society the same year.
Calcutta, 1906 Dadabhai Naoroji Moderates proposed Naoroji (respected by both camps) to avoid an outright split; Extremists had wanted Tilak or Lajpat Rai. "Swarajya" (self-government) was mentioned for the first time as a Congress goal — but left undefined; four resolutions passed: Swaraj, Boycott, Swadeshi, National Education. Naoroji unfurled the "Flag of Swaraj for India" at this session (68th BPSC).
Surat, 1907 Rash Behari Ghosh Extremists wanted Tilak or Lajpat Rai as president and pushed to guarantee retention of all four 1906 resolutions; Moderates insisted on excluding Tilak. The firm stances on both sides made the split inevitable — the session ended in physical disorder.

The Surat Split (1907) — cause and consequence

  • Root cause: the Extremists' lack of faith in the Moderates' capacity to negotiate effectively with the British Government (I.A.S. 2016) — not Lord Minto introducing communalism, the founding of the Muslim League, or Aurobindo Ghosh's failure to be elected president, all distractor options.
  • The Swadeshi Movement itself is identified as the movement that caused the Moderate–Extremist split in Congress (I.A.S. 2015) — not Quit India, Non-Cooperation, or Civil Disobedience, which all came later.
  • After the split, Congress was controlled by Moderates, refocused on constitutional, self-government methods.
  • The two factions were popularly nicknamed "Garam Dal" (Extremists, led by Lala Lajpat Rai) and "Naram Dal" (Moderates, led by Rash Behari Ghosh, who went on to preside).
  • Dadabhai Naoroji presided over Congress three times total: 1886, 1893, and 1906 — a common trap is claiming "four times," which is incorrect (U.P.P.C.S. 2014).

Government Repression, 1907–1911

  • Seditious Meetings Act (1907)
  • Criminal Law Amendment Act (1908)
  • Incitement to Offences Act (1908)
  • Explosive Substances Act (1908)
  • Indian Press Act (1910)
Context — Dadabhai Naoroji, in his own words and others'
  • C.Y. Chintamani: "Many intelligent and selfless leaders had embellished for the people of India, but in his era, no one was like Dadabhai Naoroji."
  • Gopal Krishna Gokhale: "If there is the divine in man, it is in Dadabhai Naoroji."
  • Known as the "Grand Old Man of India"; first Indian elected to the British House of Commons, 1892, from Central Finsbury, on a Liberal Party ticket (not Conservative — a repeated distractor).
  • First Indian appointed Professor of Mathematics and Natural Philosophy at Elphinstone College, Bombay; started the Gujarati newspaper Rast Goftar in 1851.
  • From 1904 onward, Naoroji was the leader who continuously emphasised the grant of "self-rule" to India — first raising "Swaraj" at the International Socialist Congress (1904), then formally demanding it in his 1906 Calcutta presidential address (U.P.P.C.S. Mains 2016).

Annulment of Partition & the Delhi Durbar (1911)

  • 1911: Lord Hardinge (as Viceroy) organised a grand Delhi Durbar, attended by King George V and Queen Mary.
  • Announcements at the Durbar (12 December 1911): Bengal Partition revoked; and British India's capital shifted from Calcutta to Delhi.
  • Officially justified as "administrative necessity, for better governance of a unified Bengal" — really a concession to sustained Swadeshi/Boycott pressure.
  • 22 March 1912: the new province of Bihar and Orissa was formed (came into existence 1 April 1912); Assam restored to its pre-1874 position; the Calcutta High Court retained jurisdiction over the new province initially. Bihar became a fully separate province only in 1936.
  • Actual physical/administrative transfer of the capital was completed in 1912, under Lord Hardinge II — so while the announcement is dated 1911, many PYQs (when both years appear as options) prefer 1912 as the completion year.

Delhi Conspiracy Case (1912)

  • An assassination attempt on Viceroy Lord Hardinge during his ceremonial procession through Chandni Chowk, Delhi, 23 December 1912, marking his state entry to the new capital.
  • Organised by Rash Behari Bose and Sachindra Nath Sanyal (extremist revolutionaries), with the bomb thrown into the Viceroy's howdah — Hardinge was injured but survived; his attendant died.
  • Fourteen persons were accused; Master Amir Chand, Bhai Balmukund, Avadh Bihari, and Basant Kumar Biswas were sentenced to death; Balraj and Harvansh Sahay got life imprisonment; Dinanath turned approver; Bhagwati Charan Vohra was not involved in this specific case (a repeated matching trap, since he appears in later 1920s cases instead).
  • The organisation responsible is identified as the Anushilan Samiti network (U.P.P.C.S. 2023); Rash Behari Bose, who led the plot, escaped to Japan and evaded trial.
  • Sachindra Nath Sanyal wrote his memoir Bandi Jeevan ("Life of a Prisoner") while jailed for his role.

Muslim League & Morley-Minto Reforms

Communal politics gets institutionalised.

5

Formation of the All India Muslim League (1906)

  • Simla Deputation: 1 October 1906, a delegation of 35 prominent Muslim leaders led by Aga Khan III met Viceroy Lord Minto at Shimla, demanding separate electorates for Muslims at all levels of governance.
  • All India Mohammadan Educational Conference, Dhaka, December 1906: Nawab Salimullah Khan (President of the Welcome Committee) proposed establishing the All India Muslim League here — making him the founder in most answer keys, even though Aga Khan III and Mohsin-ul-Mulk are also closely credited.
  • A 60-member temporary committee was formed with Mohsin-ul-Mulk and Waqar-ul-Mulk as joint secretaries; Aga Khan III became its first President; headquartered at Lucknow.

Stated Objectives (1906)

  • Promote loyalty among Indian Muslims toward the British government, clearing up any misconceptions about government intentions.
  • Protect and advance Muslim political rights and interests, representing their needs to government.
  • Prevent hostility from rising between Muslims and other communities.

Morley-Minto Reforms — Indian Councils Act, 1909

  • Named after John Morley (Secretary of State for India) and Lord Minto (Viceroy, who replaced Curzon in 1905).
  • Formally the Indian Councils Act, 1909.

Key Provisions

  • Expanded Legislative Councils, though British officials retained the majority, keeping ultimate control.
  • Separate electorates for Muslims — the reform's most consequential (and biggest-blunder) provision — allowing Muslims to elect their own representatives (66th BPSC), formally institutionalising communal division for the first time.
  • Increased elected membership in both Imperial and Provincial councils, though some seats remained nominated.
  • Muslims received representation exceeding their population share, via lower income qualifications for Muslim voters than Hindu voters.
  • Indirect elections — members chosen through local bodies.
  • Legislatures could pass resolutions and vote on budget items, but not on the budget as a whole.
  • Satyendra Sinha became the first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council.
Context — why Morley-Minto is called the seed of Partition
  • Mahatma Gandhi: "The Morley-Minto Reforms have been our undoing. Had it not been for the separate electorates then established, then we should have settled our differences by now."
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad went further, calling Lord Minto the "Father of Pakistan" — since the 1909 Act's communal-electorate principle, once established, was carried forward and expanded by the Government of India Act, 1919, ultimately culminating in Partition (63rd BPSC).
  • Correct answer to "what event sowed the seeds of eventual Partition": reservation of seats and separate electorates for Muslims in legislative bodies (U.P.P.C.S./U.P.U.D.A. 2001/2004) — not merely the League's founding or the 1905 partition itself, both of which were precursors rather than the structural cause.
Part III
Revolutionary Nationalism — Phase I (1907–1917)

When petition politics gave way to the bomb and the pistol.

7

Revolutionary Nationalism — Background & Phases

  • First Phase: followed the decline of the Swadeshi Movement, running until 1917.
  • Second Phase: arose after the Non-Cooperation Movement (see Ch.17 for its direct continuation into the 1920s).
  • Driving factors: decline of moderate leadership under Curzon-era repression, and weak post-Swadeshi leadership, disillusioned young nationalists into adopting revolutionary tactics.

Methods Employed

  • Acts of heroism: assassinations of unpopular officials and internal informers.
  • Swadeshi dacoities: robberies (often targeting moneylenders) to fund revolutionary activity.
  • Military conspiracies: during WWI, plans for coordinated action with Britain's wartime enemies (chiefly Germany).
Context — Aurobindo Ghosh's ideological role vs. organisational founders Several matching PYQs list "Anushilan Samiti — Aurobindo Ghosh" as correct, while others give its organisational founder as Promotha (P.) Mitra. Both are correct at different levels: Aurobindo Ghosh was the movement's guiding ideological figure — he wrote the influential pamphlet Bhawani Mandir (1905) calling for a revolutionary organising centre, and propounded the theory of Passive Resistance in his paper Bande Mataram — while P. Mitra founded the actual Calcutta organisational structure (with a first branch under Satish Chandra Basu, March 1902, and a second under Mitra himself with Jatindra Nath Mukherjee and Barindra Kumar Ghosh — Aurobindo's own brother — also in 1902). A separate Dhaka branch was founded by Pulin Bihari Das in October 1906. So "founder" answers legitimately vary by which branch and which level (ideological vs organisational) a question is testing.

Punjab Extremism

  • Lala Lajpat Rai initiated the newspaper Punjabee and the organisation Anjuman-i-Mohisban-i-Watan in Lahore.
  • Revolutionaries active in this thread included Lala Hardayal, Bhai Parmanand, and Syed Haider Raza.

Revolutionary Activities Outside India

India House, London (1905)

  • Shyamji Krishna Verma established the Indian Home Rule Society (IHRS) in February 1905, London — founded the nationalist student hostel "India House" (1905–10) to foster radical Indian nationalism, and started the monthly journal The Indian Sociologist (January 1905).
  • Key figures at India House: V.D. Savarkar and Lala Hardayal, who also contributed to The Indian Sociologist. It inspired revolutionaries as far as Paris and Geneva.
  • Shyamji later relocated from London to Paris, then Geneva, as British government pressure mounted.

Assassination of Curzon-Wyllie (1909)

  • Madanlal Dhingra shot dead Sir Curzon-Wyllie (political aide-de-camp to the Secretary of State for India) and Cowasji Lalkaka, 1 July 1909, at a meeting of the National Indian Association (an organisation of Indian loyalists — see the Congress dossier for its earlier, non-political founding by Mary Carpenter, 1870).
  • Dhingra made no attempt to escape, and during the trial personally requested a death sentence; hanged in London, 17 August 1909 (63rd BPSC).

Madam Bhikaji Cama — "Mother of the Indian Revolution"

  • Born 24 September 1861 to a wealthy Parsi family (father Sorabji Patel); married advocate/social worker Rustomji Cama.
  • Served as personal secretary to Dadabhai Naoroji in his nationalist work before independently joining the revolutionary movement, operating from Paris and Geneva; brought out the journal Bande Mataram.
  • At the International Socialist Conference, Stuttgart, Germany — 21/22 August 1907 — she became the first person to unfurl India's tricolour flag on foreign soil; the flag itself was jointly designed by V.D. Savarkar, Shyamji Krishna Verma, and Bhikaji Cama.
  • Returned to Mumbai in 1935; died there in August 1936.

The Ghadar Party & the Komagata Maru Incident

  • Founded 21 April 1913 (some sources say formed at a meeting in Oregon), headquartered at San Francisco, USA; a revolutionary association of expatriate Indians in the USA and Canada, aiming to overthrow British rule through armed revolt, not petitions.
  • Sohan Singh Bhakna founded the underlying "Hindustan Association of the Pacific Coast" (1913), which published the weekly newspaper The Ghadar (60th–62nd BPSC) — the paper's fame gave the whole movement its name. Bhakna was the party's first Chairman/President; Lala Hardayal was its ideological torchbearer.
  • Other key members: Ramchandra, Bhawan/Bhagwan Singh, Kartar Singh Sarabha, Barkatullah, Bhai Parmanand, Pandit Kashiram, Abdul Rahman, Raja Mahendra Pratap, Madam Bhikaji Cama.
  • Women in the Ghadar movement: Gulab Kaur ("Ghadri Gulab Kaur"), Bhikaji Cama, Agnes Smedley, Kartar Kaur, Rani Bhagwan Kaur, Sushila Mohan, Bhagwati Giri, Janki Devi — organised support, spread ideas, ran safe houses, raised funds, and built international awareness.
  • The Ghadar's first issue appeared November 1913, serialising Savarkar's The First War of Indian Independence — 1857 and citing Tilak and Aurobindo. "Ghadar di Goonj" (a poetry collection) urged cross-religious unity and armed rebellion.
  • Lala Hardayal, separately, in 1911 got the opportunity to become Professor of Indian Philosophy at the University of California/Stanford (71st BPSC).

Komagata Maru Incident (1914) & the Ghadar Rebellion (1915)

  • The Komagata Maru was a Japanese steamliner chartered by wealthy Punjabi businessman Gurdit Singh to challenge discriminatory Canadian immigration laws — departed 1914 carrying 376 Indians (mostly Sikh and Punjabi Muslim) toward Vancouver.
  • Canadian authorities refused entry; after a standoff, the ship returned to Budge Budge port, Calcutta; a clash with British police there killed 20 passengers (one drowned), and 202 were jailed.
  • A Shore Committee — led by Hussain Rahim, Balwant Singh, and Sohan Lal Pathak — had been formed to protect Indian travellers' rights during the standoff.
  • The incident, combined with the outbreak of WWI, directly triggered the Ghadar Rebellion (1915): activists planned revolts in Ferozepur, Lahore, and Rawalpindi, but the plot was betrayed; 45 leaders were executed, and Pan-Islamist figures like the Ali brothers, Maulana Azad, and Hasrat Mohani were interned under the Defence of India Rules, 1915. Rashbehari Bose fled to Japan; Sachin Sanyal was transported for life.
  • The failed rebellion is remembered as Ailan-e-Jung ("Proclamation of War"); it ultimately failed for lack of mass support within Punjab itself.

Part IV
WWI, Home Rule, and the Lucknow Reunion

Nationalism finds a wartime opening — and Congress patches itself back together.

12

First World War & Nationalist Response

  • Allies: Britain, France, Russia, USA, Italy, Japan. Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Turkey.
  • Moderates supported British war efforts as a matter of duty; Extremists (led by figures like Tilak) hoped self-governance would be the eventual reward for wartime loyalty; Revolutionaries saw the war itself as an opportunity to strike immediately at weakened British power.
  • British repression — suppressing anti-colonial publications, interning leaders like the Ali brothers and Maulana Azad — intensified anti-imperialist sentiment among the so-called "Young Party."
  • Financial strain: white troop numbers in India fell to around 15,000, while the war also opened possibilities of support from Germany and Turkey.
  • December 1914, Madras Congress session: openly supported British war efforts — marking the first time a Governor attended a Congress session.
  • 1916: the joint Congress–Muslim League session at Lucknow saw Jinnah and Ambika Charan Majumdar presiding over unified discussions (see Ch.14 for full detail).
13

The Home Rule League Movement

Two parallel leagues, one wartime demand
  • Goal: self-government (Home Rule) for India within the British Empire, modelled on Irish Home Rule demands.
  • Annie Besant first publicly outlined the idea in her weekly The Commonweal, 2 January 1914.
  • Other supporters beyond Tilak and Besant: G.S. Khaparde, Joseph Baptista, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Motilal Nehru, Jawaharlal Nehru, Bhulabhai Desai, Chittaranjan Das, K.M. Munshi, B. Chakravarti, Saifuddin Kitchlew, Madan Mohan Malaviya, Tej Bahadur Sapru, Lala Lajpat Rai, and Sir S. Subramania Iyer (who renounced his knighthood in protest).
  • Drew moderate Congress members and some from Gokhale's Servants of India Society — but Anglo-Indians, many Muslims, and non-Brahmins in the South largely stayed away, fearing Hindu-majority rule.

Context — why the two Leagues never merged Annie Besant herself explained: "Some of Tilak's supporters dislike me and some of mine dislike him. We however, had no quarrel with each other." The two Leagues were treated as complementary, dividing India geographically rather than competing — hence multiple PYQs correctly note "both continued to exist despite the differences" as the accurate statement, and any option claiming they "merged into one" is a trap.

Government Response & the Movement's Decline (by 1919)

  • British repression, especially in Madras: banned students from political activity.
  • June 1917: Annie Besant and associates arrested — triggered nationwide protests. Edwin Montagu himself remarked wryly: "Shiva... cut his wife into fifty-two pieces only to discover that he had fifty-two wives. This is what happens to the Government of India when it interns Mrs Besant."
  • Weak organisation, sectarian unrest (1917–18), Montagu's 1917 reform-promise statement and Besant's release pacifying moderates, Extremist calls for passive resistance discouraging moderate involvement, the divisive Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms (1918), and a leadership vacuum (Tilak's absence, Besant's indecision) all combined to end the movement by 1919.
  • 1918: Annie Besant visited Bihar (Patna) twice (18 April and 25 July) specifically to strengthen Home Rule League activities there (71st BPSC).
  • 1920: the All India Home Rule League elected Mahatma Gandhi as President, who renamed it "Swarajya Sabha" — folding it formally into the Congress fold (I.A.S. 2018).

Related Background — Annie Besant & Theosophy

  • The Theosophical Society was established in 1875 by Colonel Henry Steel Olcott, Madame Helena Petrovna Blavatsky, and William Quan Judge — Annie Besant was not its founder, only a later member (joined 1889) and eventually its President, 1907–1933 (I.A.S. 2013 confirms this exact distinction).
  • Besant was also a proponent of the British Fabian movement (I.A.S. 2005).
  • She became the first woman President of the Indian National Congress at the Calcutta session, 1917 (see the Congress dossier for the full session-by-session record).

The Lucknow Session (1916) & the Lucknow Pact

Why Lucknow, 1916, mattered doubly

  • Presided by Ambika Charan Majumdar — this session reunited Moderates and Extremists after a 10-year (nine-year, per one PYQ) split: the Extremists excluded since 1907 (Surat) were formally readmitted.
  • Factors enabling reunion: diminished old controversies, recognition of political stagnation, active reconciliation efforts by Annie Besant and Tilak, and Tilak's own more conciliatory stance after his June 1914 release from prison. The deaths of moderate stalwarts Gopal Krishna Gokhale and Pherozeshah Mehta also removed some of the strongest institutional opposition to readmitting Extremists.
  • Both the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League held their annual sessions at Lucknow in the same year (1916) — a rare and deliberate coincidence enabling joint negotiation.
  • In this very session, Bal Gangadhar Tilak declared: "Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it."
  • Also in this session, Raj Kumar Shukla first apprised Mahatma Gandhi of the plight of Champaran peasants — planting the seed for Gandhi's first satyagraha in India, in 1917 (U.P.R.O./A.R.O. 2013).

The Lucknow Pact — main clauses

  • Self-government declared as the shared goal.
  • One-third Muslim representation guaranteed in the central government — the first time Congress formally accepted separate electorates for Muslims (1916, a notable and consequential Congress concession — R.A.S./R.T.S. 1999).
  • Separate electorates continued (building on Morley-Minto).
  • A weightage system in legislative representation (minorities over-represented relative to population, in specific provinces).
  • Central Legislative Council strength: 150 members.
  • Four-fifths of provincial legislators to be elected.
  • Adult franchise for direct elections (an aspirational demand, not yet implemented).
  • Veto power for each community on bills specifically affecting it.
  • Separation of Executive and Judiciary — an older Moderate demand, folded into this joint platform.

Contributing factors to the Pact: Muslim dissatisfaction over Britain's refusal to aid Turkey, the 1911 Bengal Partition annulment, and denial of Aligarh University affiliation; the League's own 1912 Calcutta session shift toward supporting self-government; and WWI-era repression fuelling shared anti-imperial sentiment. Sarojini Naidu called Jinnah, the pact's chief architect (alongside Tilak), the "Ambassador of Hindu-Muslim Unity."

Context — the fragile 1916–1922 unity window The period 1916–1922 is specifically identified in PYQs as the era of Congress–Muslim League unanimity (U.P.P.C.S. 1992) — but the Lucknow Pact was only a temporary arrangement; the League retained its separate identity throughout and kept advocating Muslim-specific interests independently. The relationship began fraying as early as 1920, when Jinnah resigned from Congress over disagreements with Gandhi's approach (specifically the shift toward mass agitation and the linking of the Khilafat and Non-Cooperation movements) — meaning the "unity window" quietly cracked two years before its formally cited 1922 end-point.

Montagu's Declaration (1917) & the Government of India Act, 1919

The direct legislative sequel to this whole decade

Montagu's Declaration, August 1917

  • Marked a significant British shift — acknowledging India's desire for self-governance and introducing the concept of the "progressive realisation of responsible government", where rulers would eventually answer to elected Indian representatives rather than solely to Britain.

Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms)

  • Architects: Edwin Montagu (Secretary of State for India) and Lord Chelmsford (Viceroy); Prime Minister of England when the Act passed: Lloyd George (60th–62nd BPSC).
  • Clearly defined the jurisdictional split between central and provincial governments for the first time.
  • Dyarchy introduced at the provincial level — the "Father of Dyarchy" is credited as Lionel Curtis (60th–62nd BPSC). Subjects split into "reserved" (law & order, finance — under the Governor and his bureaucratic executive council) and "transferred" (education, health — under ministers from the elected legislature, answerable to it). No executive-councillor accountability to the legislature; the Governor could take over transferred subjects if constitutional machinery failed; Secretary of State/Governor-General could interfere only in reserved subjects.
  • Provincial legislatures expanded to 70% elected membership; communal/class electorates consolidated; voting rights granted to women for the first time. Legislature could initiate legislation but needed the Governor's assent; Governor retained veto power and could restore rejected budgets.
  • No responsible government at the central level — the Governor-General remained chief executive; central/provincial administrative lists maintained separately; the Viceroy's Executive Council included 3 Indian members; the Governor-General retained control over reserved provincial subjects and could restore budget cuts, certify bills, and issue ordinances.
  • Bicameral central legislature introduced: Central Legislative Assembly (145 members, 3-year tenure) and Council of State (60 members, 5-year tenure, male-only). Legislators had limited budget control — 75% of the budget remained non-votable; some Indian representation was allowed on important committees, including finance.
  • Important administrative change: the Secretary of State for India's salary was moved to be paid from the British exchequer going forward, rather than from Indian revenues.
  • A High Commissioner for India was appointed, based in London for a six-year term, tasked with overseeing Indian trade in Europe (63rd BPSC).

Congress Reaction — verdicts on the 1919 Act

  • Congress, led by Hasan Imam, deemed the reforms "disappointing" and "unsatisfactory," demanding effective self-government instead.
  • Tilak: "Unworthy and disappointing — a sunless dawn."
  • Annie Besant: "Unworthy of England to offer and India to accept."

Moderates vs Extremists — the People Behind the Ideology

  • The first two decades of the INC (1885–1905) are described as its Moderate era. Prominent Moderates: Dadabhai Naoroji, Surendranath Banerjee, Mahadev Govind Ranade, Pherozeshah Mehta, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Anand Mohan Bose, Dinshaw Wacha, W.C. Bonnerjee, Rash Behari Ghosh — confident in British fairness, never aiming at aggressive nationalism for independence.
  • Most Moderate leaders hailed from urban areas (43rd BPSC; U.P.P.C.S. Mains 2016) — dominated by advocates, doctors, engineers, journalists, and literary figures, with little genuine mass connection.
  • Pherozeshah Mehta: "The voice of Congress is not of the masses, but it is the responsibility of citizens in contact with Congress to understand these emotions and propagate them to the Nation."
  • Gopal Krishna Gokhale: believed in the purity of means and ends; political mentor ("Guru") of Mahatma Gandhi; first entered politics in 1889. In 1897, Gokhale and Dinshaw Wacha presented proof of Indian expenditure before the Welby Commission. Elected to the Bombay Legislative Council (1899) and the Imperial Council (1901).
  • Welby Comission estb. 1895:royal commission set up by the British Government in 1895 to investigate wasteful spending and financial administration in British India. Its official name was the Royal Commission on the Administration of Expenditure of India.Dadabhai Naoroji served as a member of the commission, becoming the first Indian to sit on a Royal Commission. He used this platform to present his famous "Drain of Wealth" theory, arguing that British economic policies were impoverishing India) of Gopal Krishna Gokhale and Dinshaw Wacha; deposed before the commission in 1897, providing powerful critiques of high military spending and unfair tax burdens.
  • Extremist leaders — Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh — emerged in the early 1900s, targeting "Swaraj" outright, and explicitly rejecting the Moderates' "politics of prayer, petition, and protest."

Tilak — later life details

  • Jailed for 6 years in Mandalay, Burma (1908), for sedition over an anti-British article in his newspaper Kesari; wrote Gita Rahasya while imprisoned there.
  • Started the Shivaji Mahotsav and Ganpati festival to cultivate mass nationalist feeling — turning traditional festivals into political platforms; he also founded the "Lathi Club" along with Hindu Akhadas.
  • Died 1 August 1920; his funeral bier was lifted by Mahatma Gandhi, Maulana Shaukat Ali, and Saifuddin Kitchlew; Maulana Hasrat Mohani read the eulogy.
  • Not a communalist, despite using religious festivals as political tools — a common Assertion-Reason trap (U.P.P.C.S. 2001) concludes he was a genuine nationalist who used religion strategically, not for communal ends.
Context — international events that shaped the pre-Gandhi movement A landmark I.A.S. 1994 question confirms that all four of the following international events influenced India's national movement before Gandhi's rise: the Italian-Abyssinian War (1898), the Boxer Movement in China (1899–1901), the Irish revolutionary movement, and — with the greatest impact — Japan's victory over Russia in the Russo-Japanese War (1905), which shattered the myth of white/European racial invincibility and directly energised Indian revolutionary confidence in this exact period.

Revolutionary Activities in the 1920s — Phase II

Included for continuity, since these cases are the direct successors of the 1907–17 organisations above

Why the movement resumed

  • The Russian Revolution (1917) inspired Indian revolutionaries with Marxist/socialist ideology.
  • New communist groups emerged, emphasising the proletariat's role.
  • Publications like Atmasakti, Sarathi, Bijoli, plus banned works like Bandi Jiwan (Sachin Sanyal — see Ch.4) and Pather Dabi (Sharatchandra Chatterjee), glorified revolutionary sacrifice and spread socialist ideas.

Hindustan Republican Association / HRA (1924, Kanpur) Founded by Ram Prasad Bismil, Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee, Sachindra Nath Sanyal (President) at a conference of young revolutionaries, October 1924 — aimed to overthrow colonial government and establish a Federal Republic of the United States of India. Bhagat Singh (born 26 Sept 1907) was just over 21 years old at the time (64th BPSC).
Lahore Conspiracy Case (1929–1930) British prosecution of HSRA revolutionaries for Saunders' killing; Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, Sukhdev sentenced to death, hanged and secretly cremated on the banks of the Sutlej at Hussainiwala, Ferozepur district, 23 March 1931. Batukeshwar Dutt was not hanged in this case — sent instead to the Cellular Jail, Andaman, for "Kala Pani." Jatin Das, arrested under the supplementary Lahore Conspiracy charge (14 June 1929), died on a 63-day hunger strike for jail reforms (13 Sept 1929) — the first Indian revolutionary to die that way.
Chittagong Armoury Raid (18 April 1930, Bengal) Organised by Surya Sen ("Master Da") via his Indian Republican Army (IRA), with Ganesh Ghosh, Lokenath Baul, Nirmal Sen, Ambika Chakraborty, Naresh Roy, Kalpana Dutt, Anand Gupta and others (60th–62nd BPSC). Clash with government forces (22 April 1930) killed 80 soldiers and 12 revolutionaries. Surya Sen arrested 16 Feb 1933, hanged 12 January 1934. Kalpana Dutt was arrested and given life imprisonment.
Meerut Conspiracy Case (1929, Meerut) Labour leaders and three Englishmen accused of conspiring to organise a railway strike — deepened British anxiety over communist influence in India.
Kanpur Conspiracy / Kanpur Bolshevik Conspiracy Case (1924, Kanpur) Main accused: Muzaffar Ahmed, Shaukat Usmani, Nalini Gupta, S.A. Dange, Gopal Basak (and others — 13 accused in total, including M.N. Roy, tried in absentia) — an early crackdown on rising communist ideology; M.A. Ansari was not among the accused (a repeated matching trap).
Ghadar Conspiracy (1915, worldwide/India) Led by Har Dayal, Raghubir Singh, and others — see Ch.11 for full detail; conspirators mobilised the Indian diaspora internationally.
Berar Conspiracy Case (1916, Berar) R.S. Nimbkar and others incited armed rebellion.
Bombay Conspiracy Case (1917, Bombay) Key figures: V.N. Chatterjee, Joseph Baptista — sedition and incitement to warfare charges, highlighting educated Indians' growing role in the freedom struggle.
Peshawar Conspiracy Cases (1922–1927, Peshawar) Pashtun leaders charged with anti-British activity — harsh sentences, including transportation for life, galvanised North-West Frontier support for Non-Cooperation.
Murder of Michael O'Dwyer (13 March 1940, Caxton Hall, London) Udham Singh (67th BPSC Re-exam) assassinated the former Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab, Michael O'Dwyer, responsible for endorsing/upholding the actions during the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (1919) — Singh had survived that massacre himself. He was hanged in London.

Other Revolutionary Bodies (Related, Cross-Referenced)

  • Naujawan Bharat Sabha: founded by Bhagat Singh, March 1926, with Chhabil Das, Yaspal, Ramchandra, Bhagvati Charan Vohra — a left-wing body organising worker/peasant youth around Marxist ideas. Ambika Chakravarti (of the Chittagong Jugantar Party) joined only later, not as an early/founding member.
  • Hindustan Independence League: founded by Rash Behari Bose in Japan (Tokyo conference, March 1942), later formalised at a Bangkok Convention (June 1942) attended by Subhas Chandra Bose — falls outside this dossier's core period but flagged for continuity with earlier revolutionary networks abroad (Ch.10–11).
  • Forward Bloc: founded by Subhas Chandra Bose, 3 May 1939, after resigning the Congress presidency.
  • Rashtriya Swayam Sevak Sangh (RSS): founded 27 September 1925 by Keshav Baliram Hedgewar, headquartered at Nagpur — a social/cultural organisation, not itself a revolutionary terrorist body, but often grouped in the same PYQ era-context.
Context — how Bengal's female revolutionaries fit the picture
  • December 1931: two schoolgirls, Shanti Ghosh and Suniti Chowdhury, killed a district magistrate.
  • February 1932: Bina Das fired point-blank at Governor Sir Stanley Jackson during her own convocation ceremony.
  • Both episodes show that women's participation in Bengal's revolutionary underground, which began quietly in the 1907–17 period discussed above (e.g. through the Anushilan/Yugantar network's broader social base), became directly militant by the early 1930s — a natural extension of the trend, not an isolated development.

Famous Revolutionary Sayings

  • Bhagat Singh: "Criticism and independent thinking are the two indispensable qualities of a revolutionary."
  • "Inquilab Zindabad": slogan originally written by Maulavi Hasrat Mohani; popularised by Bhagat Singh, who made it famous among freedom fighters.
  • "Sarfaroshi ki tamanna ab hamare dil me hai...": written by Bismil Azimabadi (born Saiyad Shah Muhammad Hasan) — not Ram Prasad Bismil himself, despite the shared pen-name "Bismil," a very common mix-up; published 1922 in the magazine Sabah.
