0. General Properties of Metals

Elements with a tendency to lose electrons and form cations are called metals. Except the right-hand corner, all s, d and f-block elements are metals. In pre-historic times only three metals were known — Copper, Silver, Gold; today ~90 metals are known.

Physical Properties

  • All metals except mercury are solid at room temperature.
  • Metallic lustre due to free electrons; metals can be polished.
  • Generally hard, except sodium, potassium, calcium (soft).
  • Malleable (hammered into sheets) — gold and silver most malleable.
  • Ductile — gold most ductile; from 1 mg gold a 200 m long wire can be drawn.
  • Good conductors of heat — silver and copper best; lead and mercury comparatively poor.
  • Good conductors of electricity; sonorous.
  • High densities, except sodium and potassium.
  • High melting points, except gallium and cesium (low).

Chemical Properties

  • Electropositive — lose electrons to form cations; react readily with non-metals.
  • Metal + Oxygen → Metal oxide (e.g. Cu→CuO black; Al→Al₂O₃).
  • Metal oxides are generally basic; Al₂O₃ and ZnO are amphoteric (both acidic + basic). Oxides soluble in water are called alkalis.
  • Reaction with water: highly reactive (Na, K, Ca, Mg) → metal hydroxide + H₂; moderately reactive (Al, Fe, Zn) → react with steam → metal oxide + H₂; least reactive (Pb, Ag, Cu, Au) → no reaction at all.
  • Reaction with dilute acids → salt + hydrogen; dilute HNO₃ does NOT evolve H₂ (strong oxidiser, itself reduced to nitrogen oxides) — exception: Mg and Mn give H₂ even with dilute HNO₃.
  • More reactive metals displace less reactive metals from salt solutions, e.g. Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu↓.

Quick-fire traps:

  • Most abundant metal in earth's crust = Aluminium; most abundant element (non-metal) = Oxygen, then Silicon.
  • Most abundant element in the human body = Oxygen.
  • Silicon is sometimes classed as a metalloid (properties of both metal & non-metal).
  • Less reactive metals (Au, Pt) occur in free/native state; more reactive metals occur combined; Cu and Ag occur in BOTH free and combined states.
  • Iron pyrite is a mineral of iron but NOT its ore — extraction from it is uneconomical.

1. Sodium (Na) and Its Compounds

Atomic No. / Mass

11 / 23

Group / Period / Block

Group-1, 3rd period, s-block

Electronic Config.

1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹

Crustal Abundance

2.27% by weight

  • Light, soft, silvery-white metal — can be cut with a knife.
  • Highly reactive; kept stored in kerosene oil to prevent contact with oxygen (reaction is strongly exothermic).
  • Reacts with air → sodium oxide (Na₂O); reacts with water → NaOH + H₂.
  • Soluble in benzene. With ammonia → sodamide; with alcohol → sodium alkoxide + H₂; with acids → salt + H₂.
  • Extracted by electrolysis of molten NaOH (Castner's process) or molten NaCl (Down's process).
  • Uses: reducing agent, alloying metal, anti-scaling agent; coolant in nuclear reactors (liquefied form — used in Fast Breeder Test Reactor, FBTR, not standard reactors which use water/heavy water); making TEL (tetraethyl lead) via sodium-lead alloy.

  • NaCl + ice at lower pressure/moderate temp gives a freezing mixture.
  • Human body requires ~500 mg sodium/day; regulates blood pressure and body pH.
  • Pure NaCl is NOT hygroscopic — apparent hygroscopic nature is due to impurities of MgCl₂ and CaCl₂ (this is why salt gets moist in the rainy season).
  • During dehydration/vomiting/loose motion, body salt levels fall abruptly.
  • On exposure to air, NaOH absorbs CO₂ and converts to Na₂CO₃ — this efflorescence causes it to crumble to white powder.
  • Baking soda + tartaric acid = baking powder; CO₂ released makes bread/cake rise, soft and spongy.
  • Sodium chloride was an important symbol of India's freedom struggle (salt satyagraha).
  • Sodium never occurs free in nature — it is highly reactive and always found combined.
  • Sodium vapour street lamps glow yellow because sodium atoms emit excess absorbed energy in the yellow region of the spectrum; the light is monochromatic and does not scatter/split through water droplets/fog — hence preferred for street lighting.
  • Water-softening agent: Na₃PO₄. Sodium calcium silicate is called soft glass.
  • Detergent powder's dominant chemical = sodium alkyl sulphate.

Atomic No. / Mass

12 / 24

Group / Period / Block

Group-2, 3rd period, s-block

Structure

Shiny grey solid, hexagonal close packing (hcp)

Config.

1s²2s²2p⁶3s²

  • Word "magnesium" from Greek "magnesia". Does not occur free (high reactivity); occurs as chlorides, carbonates, sulphates.
  • Extracted from magnesite by electrolytic reduction, or from carnallite by electrolysis of anhydrous MgCl₂.

  • Uses: flashlight ribbon, firecrackers, photography, fireworks (brilliant white light); alloy Electron = Mg(95%)+Zn(4.5%)+Cu(0.5%), used in aircraft frames and motor vehicles.

3. Calcium (Ca) and Its Compounds

Atomic No. / Mass

20 / 40

Discovered by

Humphry Davy, 1808

Abundance

3rd most abundant element in earth's crust

Group / Period / Block

Group-2, 4th period, s-block; config 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s²

  • Present in soil, plants, bones, eggshells. Extracted by electrolysis of fused 85% CaCl₂ + 15% CaF₂ (CaF₂ lowers melting point). CaCl₂ obtained as a by-product of the Solvay process.

Atomic No. / Mass

13 / 27

Group / Period / Block

Group-13, 3rd period, p-block; config 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p¹

Abundance

3rd most abundant element, MOST abundant metal in earth's crust

Chief Ore

Bauxite

  • Does not occur in free state. Chief ore is bauxite, concentrated by Baeyer's process (if Fe₂O₃ is main impurity) or Hall's process; white bauxite (SiO₂ impurity) concentrated by Serpek's process.

  • Aluminium surface is often "anodized" — deposition of a protective layer of aluminium oxide.

6. Iron (Fe) and Its Compounds

Atomic No. / Mass

26 / 56

Group / Period / Block

Group-8, 4th period, d-block

Abundance

4th most abundant element in earth's crust

Chief Ore

Haematite (Fe₂O₃)

  • Discovered before 5000 BC; most useful metal. Present abundantly in green vegetables and in the haemoglobin of blood.
  • Extracted from haematite (Fe₂O₃) in the blast furnace by carbon-reduction process.
  • Ferromagnetic, lustrous, greyish-tinge; melting/boiling points 1533°C / 2450°C.
  • Does not react with dry air/water; passing steam over red-hot iron produces H₂ gas.
  • With cold, dilute HNO₃ → ferrous nitrate + ammonium nitrate. With hot conc. H₂SO₄ → ferrous/ferric sulphate + SO₂. With cold conc. HNO₃ → ferric nitrate + NO₂. Forms halides/sulphides with halogens/sulphur.
  • Passivity of Iron: highly concentrated HNO₃ forms a layer of Fe₃O₄ on iron's surface, making it passive/unreactive; reactivated by heating in a flow of H₂ gas.
  • Iron deficiency causes anaemia; excess intake leads to siderosis (tribal Africans historically at risk from brewing beer in iron utensils).
  • Iron is present in cytochrome.

Rust & corrosion traps:

  • Rust requires iron, oxygen and moisture together — not just oxygen.
  • On rusting, the weight of iron increases (oxygen is added, forming hydrated iron(III) oxide).
  • Rust is iron oxide — not tannin (a common distractor).
  • Galvanized iron (zinc-coated) does not rust — zinc oxidises preferentially (sacrificial protection); this is why oil should not be stored in galvanized pots (zinc reacts with fatty acids/moisture to form toxic compounds and hasten rancidity).
  • Iron does not form an amalgam with mercury — the notable exception among metals; hence mercury is transported in iron containers.
  • Bailadila range (Dantewada, Chhattisgarh) is a major source of high-grade haematite, first geologically mapped by P.N. Bose (1898-1900).

7. Copper (Cu) and Its Compounds

Atomic No. / Mass

29 / 64

Group / Period / Block

Group-11, 4th period, d-block; config [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹

Named After

Cyprus, Roman era ~9000 BC

Chief Ores

Copper pyrite (chalcopyrite), copper glance (chalcocite)

  • Transition element; called "coinage/currency metal" along with silver and gold. Second most useful metal.
  • Exists in native as well as combined form; extracted mainly from copper pyrite (CuFeS₂) and copper glance (Cu₂S) by auto-reduction process.
  • Reddish-orange, malleable, ductile, good conductor; melting/boiling points 1083°C / 2310°C; specific gravity 8.95.
  • Does not react with dry air, but with moist air forms basic copper carbonate (green coating/patina).
  • Does NOT react with dilute, cold HCl and H₂SO₄.
  • Hot conc. H₂SO₄ → SO₂ gas; dilute HNO₃ → NO₂ gas; 50% HNO₃ → NO gas; conc. HNO₃ → NO₂ gas. N₂ gas forms when HNO₃ vapours are passed over hot copper.
  • Essential element of living organisms — main constituent of respiratory enzyme complex cytochrome C oxidase.
  • Uses: electroplating, electrotyping, wires, electrical appliances, calorimeters, domestic utensils, coins, alloys.
  • Copper is NOT extracted by thermal (smelting) reduction — it is extracted by the auto-reduction process, being low in the reactivity series.
  • No reaction occurs when steam is passed over copper (unlike Al, Fe, Zn which do react with steam).

8. Silver (Ag) and Its Compounds

Atomic No. / Mass

47 / 108

Group / Period / Block

Group-11, 5th period, d-block; config [Kr]4d¹⁰5s¹

Chief Ores

Argentite (Ag₂S), horn silver (AgCl), ruby silver (3Ag₂S·Sb₂S₃)

  • Soft, white, lustrous transition metal; found in free (native) and combined states.
  • Extracted mainly from argentite by MacArthur cyanide process; commercially obtained as a by-product during gold extraction.
  • Melting/boiling points 960°C / 1955°C; relative density 10.47; best conductor of both heat and electricity among all metals.
  • Inert to dry heat and H₂S-free air; but turns black in presence of H₂S (forms black silver sulphide, Ag₂S: 2Ag + H₂S → Ag₂S + H₂). This is why silver articles tarnish black in polluted air, and why it's risky to eat egg (contains sulphur) with a silver spoon.
  • Inert to acids and bases, but dissolves in NaCN (with O₂) to give soluble sodium argentocyanide complex.
  • Inert to dilute H₂SO₄ but evolves SO₂ with concentrated; gives NO / NO₂ with dilute / concentrated HNO₃ respectively.
  • Uses: coins, utensils, ornaments; filling tooth cavities (alloyed); silver plating.

9. Gold (Au) and Its Compounds

Atomic No. / Mass

79 / 197

Group / Period / Block

Group-11, 6th period, d-block; config [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹⁰6s¹

Chief Ores

Calaverite (AuTe₂), Sylvanite (AgAuTe₂)

  • Soft, bright yellow, lustrous metal; occurs free (native)/alluvial deposits/ores; also extracted from auriferous sand by amalgamation (alloying with Hg).
  • Melting/boiling points 1064°C / 2610°C; specific gravity 19.3 — among the densest common metals (Gold > Mercury > Steel in decreasing density).
  • Inert to most reagents, but dissolves in aqua regia (HCl : HNO₃ = 3:1) — hence gold's title "king of metals".
  • Inert to air, but reacts with K/Na cyanide in excess of air to give aurocyanide ion.
  • Very soft — alloyed with copper for ornaments (increases hardness).
  • Uses: ornaments, coins, electroplating, sugar and pharmaceutical industries.
  • Most ductile metal among common metals; also most malleable along with silver.
  • Accessed in the native (free) state in nature — a classic noble-metal example (along with platinum). Uranium and lead are NOT noble metals.
  • Gold is chemically non-toxic in nature.

10. Zinc (Zn) and Its Compounds

Atomic No. / Mass

30 / 65

Discovered

Before 1000 BC

Group / Period / Block

Group-12, 4th period, d-block

Chief Ore

Zinc blende (ZnS)

  • Occurs combined as zinc blende (ZnS), zincite (ZnO), calamine (ZnCO₃); extracted mainly from zinc blende by carbon reduction process.
  • Bluish-white, hard, brittle, non-malleable and non-ductile. Melting/boiling points 419°C / 920°C; specific gravity 7.1; good conductor of heat/electricity.

  • Uses: galvanising iron, alloy production, household items, smoke screen in battlefield, dihydrogen gas production, extraction of gold.
  • Food cans are coated with tin, not zinc, since zinc is more reactive and could contaminate food; tin, being less reactive, is safer.

Chief Ore

Cinnabar (HgS)

  • Found as cinnabar ore; obtained by carbon-reduction process.
  • Silvery white metal, exists in liquid form at room temperature (the only common metal to do so).
  • At 4.12 K, its electrical resistance vanishes and it acts as a superconductor.
  • Mixture of Hg with fats/sugar on strong mobilisation gives a brown powder — called "deadening of mercury".
  • On exposure to ozone, loses its meniscus and sticks to glass (superficial oxidation).
  • Forms alloys with all metals except iron and platinum — such alloys are called amalgams; hence mercury is transported in iron containers.
  • Does not react with water, steam, alkalis, or dilute HCl/H₂SO₄.
  • Cold, dilute HNO₃ → mercurous nitrate + NO; conc. HNO₃ → mercuric nitrate + NO + NO₂. Dissolves in aqua regia. Forms HgCl₂ with chlorine/sulphur.
  • Uses: thermometers, barometers, amalgams, cosmetics (mascara), mercury-vapour/fluorescent lamps, tube lights (Hg vapour + Ar), contact liquid in electrical connections, extraction of Ag/Au, vermillion making, LCD screens, batteries.
  • Mercury is the poorest conductor of heat among metals compared to other common metals; mercury and lead give greater electrical resistance compared to other metals when passing electric current.
  • An immediate rise or fall in barometer reading indicates clear weather or an approaching cyclone respectively.
  • Mercury is preferred in thermometers because it is reflective (easy to read), doesn't wet the glass, is a heat-conducting metal, expands evenly/linearly with high circulation power, and has a large liquid-range of temperature.

Uranium (U)

  • Plutonium: the Hiroshima bomb contained U-235; the Nagasaki bomb contained Pu-239. Research nuclear reactors use plutonium.

Extraction of Metals — Ores, Minerals & Metallurgy

🟨 Concept   🟦 Fact/Number   🟩 Person/Place   🟥 MCQ Trap   🟪 Rule

1. Metallurgy: Basic Terms

  • Extraction of metals = attainment of pure metal from its compound.
  • Metallurgy = the entire scientific/technological process for extracting and isolating metal from ore.
  • Minerals = naturally occurring chemical substances in the earth's crust, obtainable by mining (e.g. bauxite, kaolinite) — classed as inorganic solids.
  • Ores = minerals from which metals can be extracted conveniently and profitably. All ores are minerals, but all minerals are NOT ores — e.g. iron pyrite is a mineral of iron but not its ore (extraction too costly).
  • Gangue / Matrix = earthen impurities (soil, sand, etc.) present in the ore.
Hydrometallurgy

Extraction of metal by dissolving in aqueous solution.

Pyrometallurgy

Extraction by heating with a suitable reducing agent.

Electrometallurgy

Extraction of metal by using electricity.

Reasoning traps:

  • Ore-classification criterion: a mineral is called an ore if a metal can be profitably extracted from it (not just "extracted", and not about cost of the metal itself).
  • Some minerals contain a large percentage of metal, others only a small percentage — both statements are valid/possible.

2. Concentration of Ores

Removal of gangue/unwanted material from ore is called ore concentration, dressing, or benefaction. Besides hand-picking, key techniques:

Hydraulic Washing

Gravity separation — an upward stream of running water washes powdered ore; lighter gangue is washed away, heavier ore remains. Used for iron ores (Fe₂O₃, Fe₃O₄).

Magnetic Separation

Based on differing magnetic properties of ore and gangue. Used for tungsten from cassiterite (SnO₂), Fe₂O₃, Cr₂O₃, etc. Also increases concentration of haematite ore.

Froth Floatation

For removing gangue from sulphide ores. Collectors (pine oils) and froth stabilizers (cresols) added; oiled ore particles rise with froth, which is skimmed off.

Leaching

Used when ore is soluble in a suitable solvent. Bauxite leached with conc. NaOH; silver and gold leached with dilute sodium/potassium cyanide.

3. Extraction from Concentrated Ore — Step 1: Conversion to Oxide

Calcination

Heating ore with or without air; volatile matter escapes, leaving metal oxide. Done for carbonate, hydroxide, oxide ores.

Roasting

Done only for sulphide ores; ore heated in regular air supply below the metal's melting point; impurities (S, As) oxidise & escape as volatile gases, leaving metal oxide.
CuFeS₂ + 3O₂ → CuO + FeO + 2SO₂

4. Step 2: Reduction of Oxide to Metal

Smelting

Reduction by carbon or CO; used for metals in the middle of the reactivity series (Pb, Fe, Sn, Zn). Ore heated above its melting point with coke; flux is added.
PbO + CO → Pb (molten) + CO₂

Flux & Slag

Flux converts infusible impurities into a fusible substance called slag (Flux + Acidic/Basic Impurities → Slag), which floats lighter above the molten metal. Acidic flux (e.g. SiO₂) removes basic impurities; basic flux (e.g. CaO, FeO) removes acidic impurities.
e.g. In iron smelting, limestone (CaCO₃ → CaO at 1000°C) reacts with SiO₂ impurity to form CaSiO₃ slag.

Aluminothermic Process

Oxides of chromium and manganese reduced by aluminium.
Cr₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Cr

Auto-Reduction

Used for metals low in the reactivity series — oxides of Cu, Hg, Pb reduced to metal by heating alone.

Electrolytic Method

Used for metals high in the reactivity series — Na, Al, Ca; their oxides are reduced to metals electrolytically. Sodium obtained by electrolysis of NaCl.

  • Alkali and alkaline earth metals are obtained by electrolysis of their chlorides, oxides or hydroxides.
  • In the Hall-Héroult process of aluminium electrolysis, purified Al₂O₃ is mixed with Na₃AlF₆ (cryolite) or CaF₂ to lower the melting point and increase conductivity.
  • Thermite mixture = Fe₂O₃ + Al (3:1) — used for welding railway lines.
  • The extraction of iron from its ore is termed reduction; smelting is the metallurgical process in which a metal is obtained in a fused state.

2. Karat / Carat

Karat (Gold Purity)

24-karat gold = pure (≥99.9%) gold. 18-karat = 75% gold + 25% other metals. 1 g of 12-karat gold contains 0.5 g (50%) pure gold. Each karat = 1/24th of the whole. Copper/silver mixed with pure gold for hardness (pure gold too soft for jewellery).

Carat (Gemstone Weight)

Standard unit of weight for gemstones including diamonds. 1 carat = exactly 200 mg = 0.2 g. "Carat" (UK/Australia/India) is also sometimes used for gold purity where the US uses "karat".

3. Alloy Facts & MCQ Traps

  • Copper is not an alloy — it's a pure metal; steel, brass, and bronze ARE alloys.
  • German silver contains NO silver — it's Cu+Ni+Zn only, a persistent naming trap.
  • An amalgam is specifically a mercury-metal mixture; iron is the one common metal that does NOT form an amalgam with mercury.
  • Solder is generally Tin + Lead (~32% Sn : 68% Pb) — low melting point, good for heat-sensitive components.
  • Match: German silver → Nickel (as component, along with Cu & Zn); Solder → Tin; Bleaching powder → Chlorine; Hypo → Sodium (thiosulphate).
  • Statement check: "Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc" — TRUE; "Magnetite is the main ore of Aluminium" — FALSE (it's iron's ore); "Amalgams mainly involve mercury" — TRUE; "KNO₃ is used in photography" — FALSE (that's AgBr; KNO₃ is used in gunpowder).
  • Stainless steel becomes non-magnetic specifically due to nickel addition; extra carbon is added for hardness.
  • To increase steel's hardness, the quantity of carbon is increased (Carbon distorts the iron crystal lattice).
  • Chromium is the key metal added to iron to produce stainless steel and to make it rust-free / heat-resistant / abrasion-resistant.
  • Only Copper is common to Brass, Bronze AND German Silver among their constituent metals.
  • Copper alloys (classic pairing question) = Brass and Bronze — NOT Invar (a nickel-steel) and NOT Nichrome (a nickel-chromium alloy).
