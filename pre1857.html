Historical Trade Links & the Sequence of Arrival

Why Europe came, and in what order
  • Centuries of trade already existed between India and Europe — evidenced in the Sangam age, when Roman gold and goods reached India.
  • Two broad trade-route families: the Silk Route (via the Amu Darya River, Caspian Sea, Black Sea) and sea routes via the Arabian Sea and Mediterranean.
  • Italian merchants used their geographical position over the Mediterranean to dominate this trade before the sea route was found.
  • The search for a direct sea route was itself driven by the desire to bypass these Italian/Arab middlemen.

PYQ traps on "first/last"
  • Among all European powers, the Portuguese were the first to establish maritime/sea trading centres in India.
  • The French were the last Europeans to come to pre-independence India as traders (1667/1668), even though the Danes departed earlier (1845) than the French (1954) — arrival order and departure order are different questions.
  • The first joint-stock company to trade with India (among the given PYQ options) was the Dutch East India Company (Dutch parliament resolution, 1602); the English Company (1600) predates it but wasn't in the option set.

PYQ fine print

The Portuguese in India

First in, last out

Papal Sanction & the Sea Route

  • 1454: Pope Nicholas V granted Prince Henry of Portugal the right to explore the East, including India.
  • Treaty of Tordesillas (1494): Portugal and Spain divided the non-Christian world by an imaginary Atlantic line; Portugal got everything east of it, including India.
  • Bartholomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1487.
  • Vasco da Gama reached Calicut on 20 May 1498, guided (per historical evidence, not the Arab-navigator theory) by a Gujarati sailor/pilot — sometimes named Abdul Majid. Welcomed by the Zamorin (hereditary title of the rulers of Calicut).
  • Da Gama's cargo returned with 60× profit over expedition cost, in the black pepper trade — enough to lure other merchants to India.
  • He stayed roughly 3 months on his first visit and revisited India in 1501 and 1524 (some sources say 1502 & 1524); he died in Cochin, 1524, during his third visit.
  • Portugal introduced papaya, pineapple, and guava to India in the 16th–17th centuries.
Trap — Da Gama's helper Two competing claims exist: an Arab navigator Ahmad Ibn Majid (older theory) vs. the Gujarati sailor (per authentic historical evidence) who actually guided da Gama to Calicut. Exam answer key favours the Gujarati sailor.

Administration & Religious Policy

  • Controlled Goa, Mumbai, Daman & Diu, southern trading posts, and military posts at San Thome (Chennai) and Nagapattinam.
  • 16th century: Hooghly (West Bengal) became a crucial base — used by the Portuguese for piracy in the Bay of Bengal (asked in I.A.S. 1995 and 65th BPSC).
  • Krishnadevaraya permitted the Portuguese to build a fort at Bhatkal.
  • Religious policy: promoted Christianity, persecuted Muslims; initially tolerant of Hindus, later forced conversions; sent Jesuits to Akbar's court (1580) — failed to convert him.
  • 1535: Ghiyasuddin Mahmud Shah, ruler of Bengal, promised the Portuguese forts at Chittagong and Satgaon in return for help against Sher Shah Suri's invasion.
  • Portuguese Bengal settlements: Hooghly (1579, with Akbar's permission) and Bandel (1599, with the "Bandel Church"/Basilica of Holy Rosary). By the 16th century Satgaon declined and was replaced by Hooghly.

Relations with the Mughals & Decline

  • Good relations under Akbar and Jahangir; deteriorated after English entry (1608).
  • Portuguese piracy angered the Mughals; under Shah Jahan, Portuguese lost favour — Hooghly captured after a Mughal siege in 1632, led by Bengal Subedar Qasim Khan, who imprisoned thousands of Portuguese residents.
  • 1961: Indian annexation of Goa; last Portuguese Governor-General of Goa was Manuel António Vassalo e Silva (1958–61). At India's independence (1947), the Governor-General of Goa was José Ferreira Bossa.

The Dutch in India

  • Cornelis de Houtman reached Sumatra/Bantam in 1596.
  • Dutch East India Company (VOC) established 1602 by Dutch States General resolution, with powers to trade, acquire land, and wage war; capital 6,500,000 Guilders; eastern HQ at Batavia (Jakarta).
  • First factory: Masulipatnam, 1605; first Bengal factory at Pipali (1627), later shifted to Balasore (~1676) after Hooghly floods (1600) wrecked earlier establishments; proper Bengal trade base established at Chinsurah, 1653/1656–57 (fort there named Gustavus Fort).
  • Main trade goods: indigo, textiles, silk, saltpetre, opium, black pepper.
  • William Fort built at Kochi, 1663 after defeating the Portuguese; Kochi included in British settlements under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1814.
Trap — "Fort William" at Kochi A later Dutch fort at Chettiar (Thrissur district), built 1714 with the Cochin king's permission and recaptured after Zamorin's capture, was also renamed "Fort William." An I.A.S. 2005 question on this had no fully correct option — don't confuse this Dutch "Fort William" with Calcutta's British Fort William.

Decline

  • Amboyna Massacre (1623): ten English merchants beheaded on Amboyna (Indonesia) on orders of the Dutch governor — intensified Anglo-Dutch rivalry.
  • 1667 compromise: British exited Indonesia; Dutch shifted focus there.
  • Defeated by the British in the Battle of Hooghly (1759) — also called the Battle of Chinsurah/Biderra, fought 25 Nov 1759 during the Seven Years' War — marked the start of Dutch decline in India.

The Danes in India

  • Danish East India Company established 1616.
  • First factory: Tranquebar (near Tanjore), 1620.
  • Serampore (near Calcutta) became their most significant settlement — a major centre for the Danes (distinct from Dutch Chinsurah and Portuguese Bandel/Hooghly).
  • Focused on missionary activities rather than commerce or politics — hence the earliest, quietest exit (sold to British, 1845).

The English in India

From Levant monopoly to Bengal Diwani

Founding & Early Ventures

  • Levant Company (1592) was granted the monopoly to trade with the Ottoman Empire and its eastern reaches (incl. India) in silk, spices, etc. — technically the first British company chartered for eastern trade, predating the East India Company.
  • 31 December 1600: Queen Elizabeth I issued a royal charter to the "Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies" — exclusive 15-year monopoly on English trade east of the Cape of Good Hope.
  • Emperor of India when the Company was formed (1600): Akbar (1556–1605). Trap: when the Company's ships actually arrived and began trading, the reigning emperor was Jahangir (1605–1627).
  • 1580: Francis Drake's voyage around the world; 1588: English victory over the Spanish Armada — both fostered British maritime enterprise.

PYQ traps — English early ventures
  • Western Presidency was initially at Surat, later shifted to Bombay.
  • India's first Municipal Corporation was set up at Madras on 29 September 1688 ("Madras Nagar Nigam") — not Calcutta/Bombay.
  • English officer who defeated the Portuguese at Swally: Thomas Best (not Hawkins, Roe, or Child).
  • John Child, British official, cordoned Mughal ports at Bombay (1688) and tried to capture Hajj pilgrims → Aurangzeb ordered his siege/expulsion; Child died (1690) before the order was executed.
  • The first factory of the East India Company (per one exam's option set) is dated to Surat.

Key Areas of British Control (Farmans & Grants)

  • Golden Farman (1632): issued by the Sultan of Golconda — free trade in Golconda ports for 500 pagodas/year.
  • Farrukhsiyar's Farmans (1715/1717): secured by John Surman from Mughal emperor Farrukhsiyar — trading privileges in Bengal, Gujarat, Hyderabad; exemption from extra customs duties in Bengal (Rs 3,000/yr); authority to issue dastaks (passes) for goods; duty exemption in Hyderabad; duty exemption in Surat for Rs 10,000; Bombay-minted coins recognised across the Mughal Empire.
  • Staple exports from Bengal, mid-18th century: cotton, silk, saltpetre, opium.

Company Developments & Merger

  • 1615: authority to issue commissions to captains. 1686: authority to appoint Admirals, mint coins.
  • 1698–1702: financial trouble — unable to provide £2 million to the British government; a rival "parallel company" emerged (Jan 1701–Apr 1702), sending Sir William Norris as ambassador to Aurangzeb.
  • 1702–1709: both companies operated together; 1709: old company surrendered its charter to Queen Anne → the United Company of Merchants of England Trading to the East Indies — dominant from 1708–1873.

The French in India

  • French East India Company ("Compagnie Française pour le Commerce des Indes Orientales") established 1664 by Colbert (Finance Minister), during the reign of Louis XIV — received a 50-year monopoly on Indian/Pacific Ocean trade; state was the largest shareholder.
  • Fresh expedition under François Caron (1667) set up the first French factory in India at Surat, 1668.
  • Marcara (a Persian who accompanied Caron) founded a French factory at Masulipatnam, 1669, with permission from the Sultan of Golconda.
  • Chandernagore (near Calcutta) granted to the French 1673 by Shaista Khan, Mughal subahdar of Bengal.
  • Pondicherry acquired 1674 from Sher Khan Lodi of Bijapur — became the French nerve-centre under François Martin. Note: The Portuguese were actually the very first European power to briefly occupy Pondicherry, then the Dutch second (from 1693, they even captured it), before French dominance from 1673/1674; British occupied it in 1793 but returned it to France in 1814 (Treaty of Paris); the English never permanently held Pondicherry.
  • Other French centres: Mahe, Karaikal, Balasore.
  • Dutch captured Pondicherry (1693) — returned to France under the Treaty of Ryswick.
  • Company reorganised (1720–1742) under Governors Lenoir and Dumas.

The Carnatic Wars (1740–1763): Anglo-French Struggle

Three Carnatic Wars at a glance
WarYearsKey eventsTreaty / Result
First Carnatic War 1740/1746–48 Extension of the Austrian War of Succession (Europe, 1740). Immediate cause: Commodore Barnett/Bernett seized French ships. French under Dupleix captured Madras (Siege of Madras, 1746). Nawab of Carnatic Anwar-ud-din sent ~10,000 troops under Mahfooz Khan to besiege the French, but the French (Captain Paradise/La Bourdonnais) decisively defeated the Nawab's army at the Battle of St. Thome (Adyar river). British recaptured Madras (1748) under Admiral Boscawen and Robert Clive. Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (18 Oct 1748) — ended the Austrian war in Europe and, with it, the First Carnatic War; Madras restored to the British.
Second Carnatic War 1749–1754 Declining Mughal authority + succession wars in Carnatic/Hyderabad. Battle of Ambur (Aug 1749): combined forces of Muzaffar Jung, Chanda Sahib & the French defeated and killed Anwar-ud-din; Muzaffar Jung became Subedar of Deccan; Dupleix appointed Governor of Mughal territories south of the Krishna; Nizam surrendered districts in Northern Circars to the French; a French army under Bussy stationed at Hyderabad. Later British counterattacks under Robert Clive reversed French gains. Treaty of Pondicherry (1754) — affirmed Muhammad Ali Khan Wallajah as Nawab; French withdrawal.
Third Carnatic War 1757/1758–1763 Extension of the global Seven Years' War. Siege of Madras (1758): captured by French under Count de Lally. British recaptured Madras (1759) under Sir Eyre Coote. Battle of Wandiwash (Jan 1760): decisive British victory (Coote) over French (Lally) — broke French power in the Deccan permanently. Treaty of Paris (1763) — French ceded key territories (incl. Pondicherry after a long siege) but were allowed Indian settlements for commercial-only purposes.
Chronology & matching traps
  • Order of 18th-century battles: Battle of Ambur (1749) → Battle of Plassey (1757) → Battle of Wandiwash (1760) → Battle of Buxar (1764).
  • First Carnatic War = inconclusive on the ground but formally ended by Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle; Second Carnatic War is also often called "inconclusive"; Third Carnatic War ended decisively via Treaty of Paris.
  • Dupleix is credited as the first European to systematically involve European troops in the internal quarrels of Indian princes to acquire territory — the tactic that later guided Clive and the English.
  • French Commander defeated at Wandiwash: Count de Lally (not Dupleix, who had already been recalled to France by then).

Factors Behind English Success over the French

  • Company governance: English company had elected directors; the French company was state-owned.
  • Naval dominance: the Royal Navy was the largest/most advanced, defeating both Portuguese and French navies. According to Voltaire, French naval strength was so reduced during the Austrian Succession war that it had barely any battleships left for the Seven Years' War.
  • Industrial Revolution technology boosted English productivity.
  • Military discipline and strong leadership.
  • Political stability in Britain vs. turmoil/revolution in France.
  • Acceptance of rule: British placed less emphasis on spreading Christianity than the Portuguese/French.
  • Debt-market utilisation: the Bank of England let Britain finance wars effectively; France went bankrupt.
  • Getting huge wealth & manpower from the Conquest of Bengal further tilted the balance decisively in Britain's favour.

India on the Eve of British Conquest — Mughal Decline

The Mughal Empire began declining in the 18th century, especially during Aurangzeb's reign (1658–1707); his overextension and forced conversions strained resources and alienated allies. By Muhammad Shah's time (1719–48), regional states like Hyderabad, Bengal, Awadh, Punjab emerged as leaders sought autonomy.

External Shocks

  • Nadir Shah's invasion (1738–39): defeated the Mughal army at Battle of Karnal, captured Muhammad Shah, looted Delhi (Peacock Throne, Kohinoor diamond).
  • Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasions: captured Delhi (1757), appointed an Afghan caretaker; defeated the Marathas at the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) — a major setback for both Mughals and Marathas.

Causes of Mughal Decline (summary points)

  • Aurangzeb's overextension & alienation of allies; weak, incapable successors.
  • Nobility's shift from military duty to luxury; court factionalism.
  • Flawed succession system → repeated power struggles.
  • Rise of the Maratha Peshwas challenged central authority; military loyalty drifted to regional commanders.
  • Economic hardship from raids, mismanagement, jagirdari competition among nobles.
  • Divisive policies sparked uprisings; foreign invasions (Nadir Shah, Abdali) damaged prestige; growing European presence exposed India's stagnation in trade and warfare.

Rise of Regional States

Successor states, independent kingdoms, and new states
Categorisation of Regional States
CategoryMeaningExamples
Successor StatesFormer Mughal provinces whose governors became near-independent, hereditary rulers without contesting Mughal supremacy.Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad
Independent KingdomsArose mainly due to weakening Mughal grip over provinces.Mysore, Rajput territories
New StatesEstablished by factions that actively rebelled against Mughal rule.Maratha, Sikh, Jat territories

Hyderabad

Founder: Kilich Khan (Nizam-ul-Mulk), former Mughal Wazir. Won the Battle of Shaker-Kheda (1724) against the Mughal Viceroy, assumed control, became Asaf Jah. Despite Baji Rao I's defeat of him in 1737, he retained autonomy. Under him Hyderabad became one of the wealthiest states in the British Raj. After his death (1748), internal conflict + French intervention favoured Muzaffar Jung; stability restored under Nizam Ali Khan (1762). The Nizam allied with the British and did not rebel against British control (64th BPSC) — granted the Northern Circars for protection against Haidar Ali.

Awadh

Founder: Saadat Khan (Burhan-ul-Mulk), established autonomy in 1722, rising from Faujdar of Biyana. Safdar Jung succeeded (1739); defeat at the Battle of Buxar (1764) marked Awadh's decline. Notable Hindu officials at the Nawab's court: Raja Tikait Rai (Artha Mantri/Diwan of Nawab Aasafudaullah), Raja Jhaulal; Raja Naval Rai appointed "Bakshi" & Deputy Governor of Bengal by Nawab Safdar Jung.

Bengal

Founder: Murshid Quli Khan, former Diwan under Aurangzeb — the last Governor/Subedar of Bengal independently appointed by a Mughal emperor (Farrukh Siyar, 1717); became independent Nawab after 1717, shifted capital to Murshidabad (from Dhaka/Maksudabad); introduced Mal Jasmani revenue reform & the Izara system in land settlement. Succeeded by son-in-law Shujauddin (also Mughal-ratified, hence sometimes called "last Subedar" too), then Alivardi Khan (1740), who stopped tribute to the Mughal Emperor by 1756 and famously compared Europeans to honeybees: "if left undisturbed they give honey; disturbed, they bite till death." Siraj-ud-Daula inherited a politically unstable Bengal in 1756; his reign ended after the Battle of Plassey (1757).

Mysore & Kerala

Mysore: under the Wodeyar dynasty (formed after the Battle of Talikota, 1565); Haidar Ali became de-facto ruler by 1761 with French-modernised military; captured Dod Ballapur, Sera, Bednur, Hoskote (1761–63); countered Maratha invasions after Madhavrao's death (1772). Later, Tipu Sultan fiercely resisted British encroachment. Kerala: Martanda Varma expanded Travancore and reformed the military.

The Sikhs

  • Guru Tegh Bahadur executed by Aurangzeb for resisting forced conversions, defending Kashmiri Pandits.
  • Guru Gobind Singh established the Khalsa (1699), transforming Sikhs into a militant force.
  • Banda Bahadur led Sikh revolts post-Gobind Singh, ended the Zamindari system, captured & executed 1710. After his defeat (1716), Sikhs split into Bandai (liberal) and Tat Khalsa (orthodox) factions, unified by Bhai Mani Singh in 1721.
  • 1748: Kapur Singh Faizullapuria formed the Dal Khalsa, dividing Sikhs into Taruna Dal (youth) and Budha Dal (veterans).
  • Ranjit Singh founded the Sikh Empire — see Chapter 16.

The Marathas

  • Originally soldiers for Deccan Sultanates; imposed Chauth (25%) and Sardeshmukhi (10%) taxes on Mughal lands.
  • Founder: Shivaji; son Sambhaji continued resistance vs. Aurangzeb; Shahu secured Maratha influence after release from Mughal captivity; Balaji Vishwanath (as Peshwa) consolidated territories; Baji Rao I expanded reach up to Agra, and formed the confederacy delegating power to the Gaekwads of Baroda, Bhonsles of Nagpur, Holkars of Indore, Sindhias of Gwalior, Peshwas of Poona.
  • Decline began with the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) and the death of Peshwa Madhavrao I (1772), despite occasional cooperation vs. the British (1775–1782).

Rohilkhand, Farrukhabad, Rajputs & Jats

  • Rohilkhand: The Rohillas, led by Daud and son Ali Mohammad Khan, established Rohilkhand from Bareilly post-Nadir Shah's invasion.
  • Farrukhabad: Founded by Mohammad Khan Bangash, an Afghan chieftain, near Delhi.
  • Rajputs: seized the Mughal-decline opportunity to reclaim territories early 18th century; notable leaders — Ajit Singh (secured Ajmer & Gujarat), Jay Singh II (Agra governor, expanded influence in Surat) — but internal conflicts left them vulnerable to Maratha incursions.
  • Jats: primarily agricultural settlers who rebelled against Aurangzeb's policies, causing significant uprisings.
One-off ruler note
Begum Samru
1750–1836; built a famous church at Sardhana near Meerut; husband Walter Reinhardt Sombre (European mercenary) was granted the Sardhana Jagir by Shah Alam II for defeating Zabita Khan; she ruled Sardhana after his death.

Art, Education, Trade & Navy — Society on the Eve of Conquest

Art, Architecture & Literature

  • Bara Imambara: built by Asaf-ud-Daula in Lucknow (1784).
  • Jaipur: founded by Sawai Jai Singh (II), who established five astronomical observatories (Jaipur, Delhi, Mathura, Ujjain, Varanasi) and created the Zij-i Muhammad Shahi timetables; also had Euclid's "Elements of Geometry" translated into Sanskrit.
  • Padmanabhapuram Palace: notable architectural gem in Kerala.
  • Urdu poetry flourished in the 18th century — Mir, Sauda, Nazir, Mirza Ghalib.
  • Malayalam literature: supported by Travancore rulers — poet Kunchan Nambiar.
  • Sittar poetry (Tamil): critiques of temple rule and caste by Tayumanavar.
  • Punjabi epic: Warris Shah authored Heer Ranjha.
  • Sindhi literature: Shah Abdul Latif wrote Risalo.
  • Widow remarriage was discouraged; efforts by Raja Sawai Jai Singh and Prashuram Bhau to promote it failed.

Education

  • Traditional education focused on literature, law, religion, logic — missing science & technology.
  • Elementary: Maktabs (Muslim) and Pathshalas (Hindu) — maths, reading, writing.
  • Higher education: Chatuspathis and Tols in Bihar/Bengal (Sanskrit, Persian); Madrasahs taught Arabic and Quranic studies.

Trade, Industry, Navy & Shipbuilding

  • India was largely self-reliant, exporting more than it imported, balancing trade with gold and silver.
  • Imports: pearls, silk, wool, tea, spices, drugs, gold. Exports: cotton textiles, silk, opium, spices, precious stones.
  • Textile hubs: Dacca, Surat, Patna, Kashmir (woollen products).
  • Shipbuilding prominent in Maharashtra, Andhra, Bengal, Kerala (Calicut, Quilon); Shivaji's Navy countered the Portuguese; European ships were often built in India for trade.
  • Assessment of land revenue by soil/crop quality actually dates to Sher Shah Suri & Akbar, well before the British; mobile cannons were introduced in the Mughal era; tobacco/red chillies were introduced by the Portuguese — none of these three were "introduced by the English" (a common false-attribution trap).

British Conquest of Bengal

Bengal Before the Conquest

  • Bengal was a wealthy Mughal province, exporting saltpetre, rice, indigo, textiles to Europe.
  • Since the 1630s, the English East India Company sourced ~60% of its Asian imports from Bengal.
  • Calcutta's population grew from 15,000 (1706) to ~1,00,000 (1750).
  • Alivardi Khan defeated the Marathas, allowed the British to fortify Fort William, but grew wary of European influence.
  • Siraj-ud-Daula faced internal dissent from commanders (Shaukat Jang, Mir Jafar); court figures Jagat Seth and Omichand opposed him.

Battle of Plassey (1757)

  • Prelude: British misuse of trade privileges damaged the Nawab's finances; the alleged Black Hole Tragedy (20 June 1756) — per survivor J.Z. Holwell, 146 English prisoners held in an 18×14'10" cell, 123 died of suffocation, 23 survived; contemporary Muslim historian Ghulam Hussain (author of Siyar-ul-Mutakherin) does not mention this incident — a genuine historiographical dispute.
  • Siraj captured Calcutta ("Alinagar") in June 1756, alarmed by British fortification of Fort William.
  • Robert Clive forged alliances with Siraj's internal rivals (esp. Mir Jafar).
  • Battle fought 23 June 1757 on the banks of the Bhagirathi river, Nadia district (present-day Palasi), West Bengal. Despite being outnumbered, the British won decisively — a part of the Nawab's army under three "traitors" Mir Jafar, Yar Latif Khan, Rai Durlabh did not fight.
  • Siraj was executed; Mir Jafar installed as new Nawab in exchange for support — ceded 24 Parganas to the British and gifted £2,34,000 personally to Clive.
  • Regarded as the first decisive military success of the English East India Company in India, and the founding battle of British political dominion over Bengal, Bihar, Orissa — though actual British supremacy is credited to Buxar, not Plassey (Plassey won by treachery; Buxar by military skill, per Sir James Stephen).
  • Robert Clive — called the "Heaven-Born General" by William Pitt; Governor of Bengal twice (1758–60, 1765–67); "father/founder of the English Empire in India."

From Mir Jafar to Mir Qasim

  • Mir Jafar grew frustrated with British interference → involved in the Battle of Biderra (1759), which ended in British victory.
  • Treaty of 1760: Mir Jafar's son-in-law Mir Qasim installed as ally, deepening British control.
  • Mir Qasim (1760–63), regarded as the ablest successor of Alivardi Khan (proven as Faujdar of Poornia & Rangpur): shifted capital from Murshidabad to Munger to escape British/Calcutta interference; modernised his army on European lines; set up matchlock-gun manufacturing at Munger; reformed state finances, fined corrupt officers, introduced new taxes.

Battle of Buxar (1764) & the Treaty of Allahabad (1765)

  • Mir Qasim resisted British dominance (misused dastaks), allied with Shah Alam II (Mughal emperor) and Shuja-ud-Daula (Nawab of Awadh).
  • 22/23 October 1764: British forces under Major Hector Munro decisively defeated this combined force. Governor of Bengal during this time: Henry Vansittart; Nawab of Bengal during the actual battle: Mir Jafar (reinstated).
  • Regarded by many historians (e.g. Sir James Stephen) as more decisive than Plassey for establishing British supremacy in India — "Plassey made the English controllers of Bengal; Buxar made them contenders for supremacy over the whole country," opening the road to Delhi.
  • Treaty of Allahabad (August 1765) — two-part settlement:
    • Shuja-ud-Daula agreed to: surrender Allahabad & Kara to Shah Alam II; pay Rs 50 lakh war indemnity; give Balwant Singh (Zamindar of Banaras) full possession of his estate.
    • Shah Alam II agreed to: reside at Allahabad under Company protection; issue a Farman (12 August 1765) granting the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar & Orissa to the Company in lieu of Rs 26 lakh/year; a provision of Rs 53 lakh to the Company for Nizamat functions (defence, police, justice).
  • Deputy Diwans appointed by Clive: Mohammad Raza Khan (Bengal) and Raja Shitab Roy (Bihar) — Diwani/Nizamat business conducted by Indians but authorised by the British.
  • Successors of Shah Alam II — Akbar II (1806–37) and Bahadur Shah II (1837–57) — were mere Company pensioners.
  • After acquiring Bengal's Diwani (1765), the British reached Sylhet (1765); post-Burma War, David Scott tried linking Assam to Sylhet via Jaintia/Garo hill roads — faced resistance from the Khasi tribe under Tirot Sing — the first hill-tribe contact after the 1765 Diwani grant.

Dual Government (1765–1772)

After Buxar, the Company held revenue rights (Diwani) and law enforcement (Nizamat), while the Nawab kept only ceremonial authority. This Dual Government failed to maintain order or manage Bengal's economy and was abolished by Warren Hastings in 1772 (66th BPSC). Historian K.M. Panikkar said that from 1757–1773 the Company made Bengal a "State of Robbers."

Conquest of Mysore

Wodeyar dynasty formed after the Battle of Talikota (1565); Haidar Ali became de facto ruler by 1761, modernising the military with French support. He set up a modern armoury at Dindigul in 1755 (sent there by the Mysore king to control rebellious Palayams; became de-facto ruler of Dindigul and later of Mysore). After Peshwa Madhavrao's death (1772), Haidar successfully countered Maratha invasions.

WarYearsKey Battles / EventsResult / Treaty

British Governor-General: Warren Hastings. British (Sir Eyre Coote) alienated Haidar's allies; Haidar won initially at Porto Novo (Nov/July 1781) but was ultimately checked; Haidar Ali died of cancer, 7 Dec 1782; son Tipu Sultan continued the fight. Treaty of Srirangapatna (March 1792) — Cornwallis for the British, with Nizam & Maratha representatives also party. Tipu ceded nearly half his territory (divided among British, Nizam, Marathas — British got Baramahal, Dindigul, Malabar); paid Rs 330 lakh war indemnity; his two sons held hostage until payment.
Fourth1799 Tipu sought to reclaim power after the Wodeyar ruler's death, proclaiming himself Sultan; British (Lord Wellesley) alarmed by Tipu's ties with France. War began 17 April 1799; culminated in the Siege of Seringapatam (4 May 1799). British commanders: Arthur Wellesley (brother of the Governor-General), Stuart, General Harris. Tipu Sultan killed in battle defending Seringapatam; family imprisoned at Vellore. Lord Wellesley conferred the title Marquess by the Irish House of Lords. New Mysore state handed to the old Wodeyar (Hindu) dynasty under minor ruler Krishnaraja III, who accepted the Subsidiary Alliance.
Post-Tipu Mysore & matching traps
  • 1831: William Bentinck alleged misgovernance and took direct control of Mysore. 1881: Lord Ripon restored the kingdom to its (Wodeyar) ruler.
  • Correct matching: First Anglo-Mysore War → British lost; Second → Haidar Ali defeated the British (initially); Third → Tipu ceded territory but did not lose his throne; Fourth → Tipu defeated and killed.
  • British general who defeated Haidar Ali at Porto Novo: Sir Eyre Coote (not Munro, Popham, or Goddard).

Tipu Sultan's Administration & Legacy

  • Made Srirangapatna his capital; became a member of the Jacobin Club (French Revolutionary body) and planted the "tree of liberty" as a Mysore–France friendship symbol.
  • Sent ambassadors/established embassies (on modern lines) with Iran, Istanbul (Ottoman), Afghanistan, France — seeking help against the British; considered the first Indian ruler to do so on modern lines.
  • Governance: Western methods, dual leadership in provinces, a panchayat system.
  • Economic: reformed land revenue, controlled trade, set up commercial monopolies.
  • Military: disciplined army, a Board of Admiralty, planned (but never matched British) navy.
  • Provided funds for construction of the idol of Goddess Sharda at the Shringeri temple.
  • Innovator in rocket technology and sericulture; promoted religious tolerance.

The Anglo-Maratha Struggle for Supremacy

Three Anglo-Maratha Wars

Nana Phadnavis then violated Purandar by granting the French a port. Treaty of Salbai (May 1782) — approved by Hastings & Phadnavis; 20 years' peace; British retained Salsette, returned post-Purandhar territories, upheld trade privileges, and the Peshwa was barred from aiding other European powers; guaranteed by Mahadji Scindia.
Second1803–05 Arose after Peshwa Madhavrao Narayan's suicide (1795) → Bajirao II's ascension amid discord. Nana Phadnavis (chief minister) died 1800, opening the door to British intervention. Conflict ignited when the Peshwa attacked the British Residency at Poona. Tulsi Bai took charge of Holkar's forces after Jaswantrao Holkar's death but struggled with poor advisors. Confederacy & matching
  • Confederacy families delegated authority by Baji Rao I: Gaekwads (Baroda), Bhonsles (Nagpur), Holkars (Indore), Sindhias (Gwalior), Peshwas (Poona).
  • The Marathas are singled out (U.P.P.C.S. 1993) as the power that opposed the British most vigorously in India, being suppressed only after three full wars.
  • British defeat at Wadgaon and subsequent rejection of that treaty by Hastings is a favourite "who-rejected-what" trap.

Conquest of Sindh

  • 1783: Mir Fath Ali Khan of the Talpura Baloch tribe took control of Sindh, expelling the Kallora dynasty; region later divided among the Char Yar Amirs.
  • 1799: Lord Wellesley aimed to neutralise French influence in Sindh, but Fath Ali Khan expelled the British agent (1800).
  • 1807: Treaty of "Eternal Friendship" — peaceful relations, free passage for British traders/travellers.
  • 1832: Treaty of 1832 — allowed British trade while respecting the Amirs' control over tariffs.
  • 1836: Lord Auckland prioritised Sindh to block Russian expansion and strengthen British Afghan influence.
  • 1838 treaty (Auckland): permitted English intervention in Amir–Sikh disputes and stationed a British resident free to travel with an English escort — Sindh became a British protectorate, 1838.
  • 1838: Tripartite Treaty — British, Ranjit Singh, and Shah Shuja — compromised Sindh's sovereignty in British favour.
  • 1839: Subsidiary Alliance imposed on the Amirs — British military presence, control over foreign relations.
  • After the First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–42), Amirs' resistance triggered British intervention.
  • 1843: Charles Napier annexed Sindh (67th BPSC Re-exam) under Governor-General Ellenborough — Amirs ousted, Sindh integrated into the British Empire.

British Conquest of Punjab

Ranjit Singh's Rise

  • Born 13 November 1780 at Gujranwala, to Maha Singh, chief of the Sukerchakia Misl; father died when Ranjit Singh was 12; a regency council (mother, mother-in-law, Diwan Lakhpat Rai) ran affairs 1792–97; he assumed direct power in 1797.
  • 1798: Zaman Shah invaded Punjab; while retreating, his cannons fell into the Jhelum River; Ranjit Singh returned them, and in gratitude Zaman Shah gave him the title Raja and made him Subedar of Lahore.
  • 1799: took control of/made Lahore his capital. 1802: captured Amritsar from the Bhangi Misl, uniting the political capital (Lahore) and religious capital (Amritsar).
  • Later annexed Multan (1818), Kashmir (1819), Peshawar & the North-West Frontier/Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa (1834); his kingdom also came to include Srinagar.
  • Set up the Adalat-i-Ala (highest appellate court) at Lahore; also set up modern cannon-manufacturing foundries at Lahore/Amritsar.
  • Famous saying: "God wants me to see all religions as one, so He took the light of my other eye."
  • Shah Shuja (grandson of Ahmad Shah Abdali) gifted the Kohinoor diamond to Ranjit Singh (Shah Shuja had lost his throne to Shah Mahmud/Fateh Khan/Dost Muhammad and sought Ranjit Singh's help).

Succession Chaos After Ranjit Singh (d. 1839)

  • Successor: Kharak Singh (an opium addict); two rival factions emerged — the Dogra Brothers (Dhyan Singh, Gulab Singh, Suchet Singh) vs. the Sandhawalia Sardars (Atar Singh, Lahna Singh, nephew Ajeet Singh) — driving growing chaos.
  • Maharaja Duleep Singh — last ruler of the Sikh Empire, reigned 1843–49; converted to Christianity, travelled to Russia, later (1886) re-accepted Sikhism; died in Paris, 22/23 October 1893; funeral/cremation in England (not Nasik).

PYQ traps — Punjab annexation
  • Under the Treaty of Lahore (1846): the Maharaja ceded all territory beyond the Sutlej forever, and gave up rights over all forts between the Sutlej and Beas rivers — but the clause about the English army never passing through Sikh territory again is false (only 2 of 3 given statements are correct in that PYQ).
  • H.M. Elliot and Robert Montgomery were not members of the "Board of Three" that governed Punjab post-annexation — only Henry Lawrence, John Lawrence, and Charles Mansel were.

British India's Relations with Neighbouring Countries

Bhutan, Nepal, Burma, Tibet, Afghanistan

Bhutan

  • 1826: after British annexation of Assam, Bhutanese raids into Bengal/Assam caused tension.
  • 1863–64: Lord Elgin's mission treated harshly by Bhutan → cession of passes to Bhutan; 1865: British annexed these passes in retaliation, granted Bhutan an annual subsidy.
  • Duar Conflict (Anglo-Bhutanese War) ended in Bhutan's defeat → Treaty of Sinchula (1865): Bhutan ceded Assam & Bengal Duars plus Dewangiri (80,000 sq km) for a 50,000-rupee annual payment.

Nepal

  • 1801: British annexation of Gorakhpur created overlapping borders with Nepal.
  • 1813–23: Nepal's capture of Butwal & Sheoraj during Lord Hastings' tenure sparked the Anglo-Nepalese War (1814–1816); key battles at Nalapani, Jaithak, Malaon; Gorkha commander Amar Singh Thapa died at Malaon → Nepal's defeat.
  • Treaty of Sagauli (1816): Nepal ceded Garhwal, Kumaon, Terai; accepted a British Resident; gave up claims over Sikkim; granted British control over Nepal's foreign relations and commercial ties.

Burma

  • British expansion driven by Burma's resources, markets, and countering French Southeast-Asia ambitions.
  • First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–26): caused by Burma's occupation of Arakan & Manipur, threats to Assam. Treaty of Yandabo (1826): Burma paid 1 crore rupees compensation, ceded Arakan & Tenasserim, gave up claims over Assam/Cachar/Jaintia, recognised Manipur's independence, accepted a British Resident at Ava.
  • Second Anglo-Burmese War (1852–53): British occupied Pegu, secured Lower Burma.
  • Third Anglo-Burmese War (1885): after King Thibaw's antagonism & foreign alliances, the British annexed Upper Burma — full control.
  • Burma separated from British India in 1937; achieved independence 4 January 1948 under nationalist leader U Aung San.

Tibet

  • Tibet (nominal Chinese sovereignty, governed by Buddhist lamas): British attempts at relations had failed; Russian influence was growing.
  • British Expedition to Tibet (1903–04): alarmed by Russian influence, Lord Curzon sent Colonel Younghusband with a Gurkha contingent to force negotiations.
  • Treaty of Lhasa (1904): Tibet to pay an indemnity of 75 lakh rupees over 75 years (later reduced to 25 lakh); cede the Chumbi Valley as security and respect the Sikkim frontier; open trade markets at Yatung, Gyantse, Gartok; prevent foreign infrastructure. British forces withdrew from Chumbi Valley in 1908.

Afghanistan

  • Forward Policy (1836): Lord Auckland's policy to protect British India's borders from Russian invasion → First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–42): British installed Shah Shuja as Amir but faced resistance, costly retreat (1842), restoration of Dost Mohammed.
  • Policy of Masterly Inactivity (1864–69): John Lawrence avoided intervening in Afghan succession disputes.
  • Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–80): Amir Sher Ali's refusal to accept a British envoy led to invasion → Treaty of Gandamak (1879): Afghanistan's foreign relations made subject to British control.
  • Durand Line (1893): established the boundary between British India and Afghanistan — caused unrest.
  • Third Anglo-Afghan War (1919): Afghanistan declared war, but the Treaty of 1921 granted it full control over foreign affairs — end of British influence there.
  • Context: 19th-century Russian influence in Persia and threats to British interests led Britain to try to keep Afghanistan a neutral buffer state between British India and Russia.
