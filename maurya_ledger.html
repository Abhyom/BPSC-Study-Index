01Sources of Mauryan History

Literary Sources

  • Kautilya's Arthashastra — treatise on statecraft; the primary indigenous source on Mauryan administration.
  • Megasthenes' Indica — a Greek ambassador's eyewitness account of Chandragupta Maurya's court and contemporary India.
  • Vishakhadatta's Mudrarakshasa — a Sanskrit political drama on the fall of the Nandas and rise of Chandragupta; Dhundiraja wrote a commentary on it.
  • Patanjali's Mahabhashya — a grammatical work, also a source for the Shunga transition.
  • Buddhist texts: Divyavadana, Dipavamsa, Mahavamsa (Sinhalese chronicles), Samanta Pasadika of Buddhaghosha.
  • Jain texts and the Puranas (including Vayu Purana) supplement the dynastic chronology.

Archaeological Sources

  • Ashokan inscriptions — rock, pillar, and cave edicts — the most reliable primary evidence.
  • Palace and city remains at Kumrahar and Bulandibagh (near Patna) — remnants of ancient Pataliputra.
  • The Junagadh Rock Inscription of Rudradaman (150 A.D.) — a later Shaka-era record that independently confirms Mauryan-era construction in Gujarat.

02Chandragupta Maurya (321–297 B.C.)

  • Chandragupta Maurya was the first emperor to politically unify India, bringing fragmented kingdoms into a single vast empire.
  • Founded the Mauryan dynasty — considered India's oldest dynasty, ruling from 321 B.C. to 185 B.C.
  • Overthrew the Nanda dynasty (ruled by Dhana Nanda) with the guidance of Chanakya (Kautilya).
  • Expanded his empire to the edge of present-day Iran in the north-west, and as far as north Karnataka in the south.
  • His unified empire stretched from Bengal and Assam (east) to modern-day Afghanistan (west), Kashmir (north) to the Deccan plateau (south).
  • Early conquests stretched his boundary from the Beas to the Indus river.
  • Chandragupta Maurya — not Bindusara — gets historical credit for the conquest of the Deccan; Jain and Tamil inscriptions across southern India confirm this.
  • Defeated the invading army of Seleucus Nicator — the Macedonian governor of the eastern part of Alexander's empire (governor of Sindh and Afghanistan) — in 305 B.C.
  • The Junagadh Inscription of Rudradaman (150 A.D.) records that Pushyagupta, Chandragupta's provincial governor of Anarta and Saurashtra (Gujarat), built the Sudarshan Lake for irrigation — proving western India was part of the Mauryan Empire.
  • His 80-pillared palace at Pataliputra had wooden floors; excavated at Kumrahar by D.B. Spooner. City-wall remains were found at Bulandibagh.
  • Pataliputra was called 'Polibrotha' (Palibothra) in Greek texts.

Greek Identification of Chandragupta

  • William Jones was the first scholar to recognise 'Sandrokottos' as Chandragupta Maurya.
  • Plutarch (and Arrian) described him as 'Androcottus'/'Androkottus'.
  • Justin, a Greek writer, described the meeting between Sandrokottos and Alexander the Great.

Chandragupta in Literature

  • Mudrarakshasa by Vishakhadatta — a political drama on the court intrigues at the time of Chandragupta; describes the presence of 'Durabhisandhi' in his court.
  • It depicts Chandragupta as 'a scion of the Nanda house' — son of Mahapadmananda as well as Maurya-putra — and calls him "Vrishal" and "Kulheen".
  • Vishakhadatta's other works: Devichandraguptam and Abhisarika-vanchitaka.
MCQ Trap

The Sanchi Stupa was built by Ashoka, not Chandragupta — it has no direct association with him. Chandragupta's genuine associations are: Susima (his grandson via Bindusara), the Junagadh Inscription of Rudradaman, and Vishnugupta (his mentor Kautilya).

03Chanakya / Kautilya & the Arthashastra

  • Kautilya — also known as Vishnugupta and Chanakya — served Chandragupta as prime minister, advisor, and chief priest.
  • He was the pioneering scholar of political science and authored the Arthashastra — one of the oldest books on the political condition of India.
  • Vishnugupta was his childhood name (his father was Sage Chanak). The Arthashastra identifies its author as Kautilya in every verse except one, where he is called Vishnugupta.
  • Per researcher Trautmann, "Chanakya"/"Kautilya" is presumably the name of the author's gotra (clan).
Concept — What the Arthashastra Actually Is

The Arthashastra is a treatise on the principles and practice of statecraft — not merely an economics manual, foreign-policy tract, or guide to wealth accumulation. It is compared to Machiavelli's "The Prince" (Il Principe).

Courts, Slavery & Marriage — Arthashastra Provisions

  • Two types of courts: Dharmasthiya (analogous to modern civil courts) and Kantakshodhana (analogous to modern criminal courts).
  • Per the Dasa-Kalpa chapter: a person could become a slave as a result of judicial punishment. If a female slave bore her master's son, she was legally freed — but that son did NOT inherit the legal status of the master's own son.
  • Allows the remarriage of a wife deserted by her husband. A wife could seek divorce if her husband stayed abroad too long or was physically handicapped; a husband could leave a wife for being barren or adulterous.
  • Mauryan rural administration, in ascending order of size: Samgrahana (10 villages) → Karvatika (200 villages) → Dronamukha (400 villages) → Sthaniya (800 villages).
  • Sarthwah — the head of a convoy of merchants — is a term described in the Arthashastra.
MCQ Trap

Megasthenes' Indica claims India had no slavery. This directly contradicts the Arthashastra's own Dasa-Kalpa chapter, which clearly describes slavery through judicial punishment. Don't let one source's silence override the other's explicit statement — know which source is being asked about.

04Bindusara

  • Greek writers addressed him as 'Amitrochates' — Sanskrit form Amitraghat ("killer of enemies").
  • Jain texts call him 'Simhasena'; his mother's name in Jain texts was Durdhara.
  • Per the Divyavadana, a revolt broke out in Taxila during his reign, for which he sent his son Ashoka to suppress it.
  • Per Strabo: Antiochus I Soter sent Deimachus as ambassador to Bindusara's court, replacing Megasthenes.
  • Per Pliny: Egypt's king Ptolemy II Philadelphus sent Dionysius as ambassador to the Mauryan court.
  • Bindusara requested three items from the Syrian king: sweet wines, dry figs, and a philosopher. Antiochus I Soter sent everything except the philosopher.
  • Per Buddhist evidence, Ashoka was appointed viceroy of Avanti during Bindusara's reign.
  • Per the Samanta Pasadika of Buddhaghosha: Ashoka won the Avanti Mahajanapada and merged it into the empire, during Bindusara's rule.
MCQ Trap

A conquest of the Deccan is sometimes wrongly attributed to Bindusara — his Deccan victory is historically doubtful; Chandragupta Maurya is the ruler credited with it.

05Ashoka — Accession & Extent of Empire

  • Ashoka's coronation on the throne of Magadha took place in 269 B.C.
  • In his inscriptions he is titled 'Devanampiya' / 'Devanampiyadasi' ("Beloved of the Gods") and 'Priyadarshi' / 'Piyadasi'. The Puranas also call him "Ashok Vardhan".
  • Except Assam and the Deep South, the entire subcontinent came under Ashoka's empire.
  • Sri Lanka was never part of Ashoka's territory. Major Rock Edict II names Chola, Pandya, Satiyaputta, Keralaputta and Tamraparni (Sri Lanka) as kingdoms outside Mauryan rule — called "edge/frontier states."
  • Satavahana is the one South Indian kingdom not mentioned in any Ashokan inscription.
  • Ashoka and his grandson Dasaratha were followers of Buddhism — Dasaratha also adopted the title "Devanampiya."
  • Ashoka built India's first hospital and herbal garden; he founded public gardens, medicinal-herb gardens, dug wells, and planted shade trees along roads.
Concept — A Hindu-Buddhist Balance

Despite embracing Buddhism, Ashoka retained the epithet "Devanampriya" ("beloved of the gods/handsome") — evidence that he did not abandon respect for the older Brahmanical tradition even as a Buddhist convert.

06The Kalinga War

  • The Kalinga War was fought eight years after Ashoka's coronation, i.e. in 261 B.C.
  • Rock Edict XIII gives the clearest account of the war and Ashoka's victory.
  • Ashoka expressed deep remorse for the bloodshed caused by his conquest of Kalinga.
Concept — Why Kalinga Was Invaded

The reasons were both political and economic. Kalinga was a bridge between north and south India; its people dominated the Indian Ocean trade and controlled overland routes from the Gangetic valley to the Deccan. An independent Kalinga threatened Magadhan overseas and land trade — making its conquest strategically necessary.

07Ashoka's Dhamma

Concept — What Dhamma Is

Dhamma was Ashoka's ethical code, based on the triple foundation of longanimity (patience), liberty, and compassion — declared for the welfare of all humanity, not tied to any single religious community. Its core themes were peace, non-violence, respect for elders, and religious toleration. Dhamma is not simply another name for Buddhism.

  • The definition of Dhamma draws from Buddhist texts: the Rahulovada-Sutta, the Lakkhana Sutta (Digha Nikaya), and the Dhammapada.
  • In the 14th year of his reign, Ashoka appointed a new cadre of officers called Dhamma-Mahamatras to promote Dhamma.

Key Edicts on Dhamma

EdictWhat It Says
Rock Edict IBans slaughter of living beings for sacrifice in the royal kitchen — killing reduced from hundreds of thousands daily to just three animals (two peacocks and a deer), and eventually to none.
Rock Edict XIExplains Dhamma policy — duty toward elders, abstaining from killing animals, generosity toward friends.
Rock Edict XIIDevoted completely to religious toleration — declares that criticising another's sect while praising one's own actually injures one's own sect.

MCQ Trap

"Which edict is devoted completely to religious tolerance?" → Rock Edict XII, not XIII (which covers the Kalinga war) and not XI (which is broader Dhamma policy).

08Third Buddhist Council & Dhamma-Yatra

  • Per the Sinhalese chronicles Dipavamsa and Mahavamsa, the Third Buddhist Council was held at Pataliputra during Ashoka's reign; chaired by Moggaliputta Tissa.
  • After embracing Buddhism, Ashoka gave up hunting and undertook pilgrimages known as Dhamma-yatra.

  • At Lumbini, the Rummindei Pillar was erected to mark the birthplace of Buddha; Ashoka exempted the Bali tax there and reduced land revenue from 1/6th to 1/8th.
  • The Bhabru (Bairat) Inscription proves Ashoka's Buddhism — it is the only inscription where he calls himself 'Piyadasi Raja' (King) of Magadha.

09Ashokan Inscriptions

  • Ashokan inscriptions fall into three classes: Rock inscriptions, Column (Pillar) inscriptions, and Cave inscriptions.
  • 14 Major Rock Edicts are found at: Shahbazgarhi, Mansehra, Kalsi, Girnar, Dhauli, Jaugada, Yerragudi, and Sopara.
  • Most inscriptions are in the Prakrit language and Brahmi script. Only Shahbazgarhi and Mansehra are in the Kharosthi script (still Prakrit language).
  • A ruined Aramaic script inscription was found at Taxila; another at Laghman. The Shar-i-Kuna (Kandahar) Inscription is bilingual — Greek and Aramaic.
  • Kharosthi was written right-to-left and was mainly the script of north-west India.

Decipherment

  • James Prinsep was the first scholar to decipher Ashoka's inscriptions, via letters inscribed on stone tablets (1835 A.D.).
  • Prinsep initially mistook the title 'Devanampiya Piyadassi' for a Sri Lankan king, since Sinhalese chronicles used the same sobriquet for Ashoka.
  • The Maski Inscription (Karnataka), discovered in 1915, finally confirmed the personal name 'Ashoka'.
  • Ashoka's personal name appears specifically in the Gujarra, Maski, Nettur, and Udagolam minor rock edicts.
  • Pre-Ashokan Brahmi script has been found at Anuradhapura (Sri Lanka), with further early evidence at Piprahwa, Sohgaura, and Mahasthan.

  • Ashokan stone pillars are highly polished, monolithic, tapering — but they are standalone sculptures, NOT part of larger architectural structures.
  • All Mauryan-era columns are carved from Chunar sandstone.
  • The Sarnath Pillar's four back-to-back lions — the symbol of power — is now India's national emblem.

MCQ Trap

Rock Edict II mentions the South Indian kingdoms (Chola, Pandya, Satiyaputta, Keralaputta, Tamraparni), not Edict III, IX, or the First Pillar Edict. Edicts II and XIII together are the ones tested on Sangam kingdoms.

12City Administration — Pataliputra

  • Described in Megasthenes' Indica. Pataliputra was governed by a 30-member municipal council, divided into six committees of five members each.
  • The 6th committee collected sales tax — every citizen paid one-tenth of earnings; evaders faced the death penalty.
  • The 3rd committee maintained records of birth and death registration — a function that continues in municipal administration to this day.
  • Town officials were called 'Astynomoi'; overall town administration was headed by the Nagrak / Purmukhya.

  • Megasthenes states there was no slavery in India, no inter-caste marriage, and no occupation outside one's fixed caste (varna).
  • Per Megasthenes, philosophers were of two kinds: Brahmins (liberation through knowledge of Brahman) and Shramanas (liberation through ascetic practice).

Family Law

  • Manusmriti (38th section): a widower could remarry, but a widow whose husband died could not.
  • Manusmriti labels foreign invaders who did not follow priestly customs — Shakas, Kushans, Hunas, Pratiharas — as "Fallen Kshatriyas."
  • The system of governing villages through autonomous elected panchayats was evolved by the Dravids (Cholas) — not the Mauryans.

  • Barabar Hill Caves (Jahanabad district, Bihar): four caves in total; Ashoka's inscriptions found on three of the four; dedicated to the Ajivika sect (founded by Makkhali Gosala); belong to the Mauryan period (321–185 B.C.).

  • Taxila was the most famous centre of learning during the Mauryan period.

16Decline of the Mauryan Empire

  • The last Mauryan emperor was Brihadratha.
  • He was assassinated by his own commander-in-chief, Pushyamitra Shunga, around 185/184 B.C., who founded the Shunga Dynasty.
  • Pushyamitra Shunga, a devout Brahmin, performed two Ashwamedha Yajnas, supervised by Patanjali (author of the Mahabhashya).
  • The last Shunga king, Devabhuti, was killed in a conspiracy by his Brahmin minister Vasudeva (Kanva), who then founded the Kanva Dynasty.
  • Per the Vayu Purana, the last Kanva ruler Susharma was deposed by his Andhra (Satavahana) servant Simuka/Sindhuka.

Concept — The Sudarshan Lake Timeline

Built by Chandragupta Maurya's governor Pushyagupta → canals added by Ashoka's governor Tushaspa → repaired by Rudradaman's governor Suvishakha (2nd century A.D.) → later repaired again by Skandagupta. The Junagadh Inscription of Rudradaman uniquely records the works of both Chandragupta Maurya and Ashoka on this one lake.

18Important Chronology

  • 326 B.C. Alexander invades India; Dhana Nanda rules Magadha at the time.
  • 321 B.C. Chandragupta Maurya establishes the Mauryan Empire, overthrowing the Nandas.
  • 305 B.C. Chandragupta defeats Seleucus Nicator.
  • 269 B.C. Ashoka's coronation at Magadha.
  • 261 B.C. The Kalinga War (8 years after coronation).
  • 259 B.C. Pilgrimage to Bodhgaya (10 years after coronation).
  • 255 B.C. Kanakamuni stupa enlargement (14 years after coronation); Dhamma-Mahamatras appointed.
  • 249 B.C. Pilgrimage to Lumbini and Nigalisagar (20 years after coronation).
  • 185/184 B.C. Brihadratha assassinated by Pushyamitra Shunga; end of the Mauryan Empire (321–185 B.C.).
  • 1797 A.D. Colin Mackenzie traces the Amravati Stupa.
  • 1835–38 A.D. Brahmi/Kharosthi decipherment efforts by James Prinsep, Grotefend, and Lassen.
  • 1915 A.D. Maski Inscription confirms Ashoka's personal name.
