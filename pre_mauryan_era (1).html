Part A
Rise of the Mahajanapadas & Magadha

From tribe to territorial state, the sixteen Mahajanapadas, and Magadha's climb under Haryanka, Shishunaga and Nanda.

1

Sixth Century BC: From Jana to Mahajanapada

Jana → Janapada → Mahajanapada

  • The Later Vedic Period (900–600 BC) saw a transition from a tribal polity based on lineage (Jana) to a territorial state (Janapada).
  • Janapada = the land where a Jana (people, clan or tribe) sets foot or settles.
  • Janapadas fought one another for resources and political dominance; some extended their territory and brought several janas under their jurisdiction — these grew into Mahajanapadas (large states), which were either monarchical or republican in polity.
  • Before Gautama Buddha's birth ancient India was divided into 16 major states — the Shodasha (Sixteen) Mahajanapadas.
  • The rise of large states with towns as administrative centres created allegiance to the Janapada rather than to one's jana or tribe.
  • Agricultural surplus, growth of crafts and trade and rising population produced towns in the Gangetic plains — the second urbanisation (the first was the Harappan Civilization). The second urbanisation period = 6th to 3rd century BC; its great kingdom was Magadha.

Why Large States Rose

  • Favourable location: the Ganga plains were fertile and close to the iron production centres.
  • Iron technology improved agriculture → surplus collection → sustained military and administrative needs → stable settlement.

Republics of the Buddha Period

  • Ten republics (ganas) are known from the Buddha's time: Shakya of Kapilavastu, Bhagga of Sumsumaragiri, Buli of Alakappa, Kalama of Kesaputta, Koliya of Ramagama, Malla of Kusinara, Malla of Pava, Moriya of Pipphalivana, Lichchhavi of Vaishali and Videha of Mithila.
  • Yaudheya was not among the early (Buddha-period) republics.
  • Lumbini, the birthplace of Gautama Buddha, lay in the tribal republic of the Shakyas.
  • The Lichchhavis of Vaishali established the world's first republic; Vaishali took its name from King Vishal of the Mahabharata era, is a great Buddhist pilgrimage site and the birthplace of Mahavira.
  • Vajji was the largest and most powerful republic of the Buddha period; its capital Vaishali was the capital of the Vajji confederacy, ruled by the Lichchhavis.
  • Panchala was originally a monarchy but seems to have become a republic in the 6th–5th century BC.

Religious Ferment of the 6th Century BC

  • The 6th century BC was a period of great religious upheaval in all parts of the world, and the Vedic religion had become very complex — both statements are true.
  • Many religious sects arose across the middle Ganga valley; hermits of different faiths preached their ideas and rejected each other's philosophies.
  • Similar movements elsewhere: Confucius (China), Zarathustra (Iran) and Pythagoras (Greece) challenged traditional assumptions.
  • Vedic religion grew complicated: stress on purity of shlokas and yajnas, so priests gained enormous importance; yajnas consumed much wealth and encouraged animal killing.

Sources of Information

  • Vedic texts: the Brahmanas and Upanishads refer to many Janapadas and Mahajanapadas.
  • Buddhist texts: Vinaya, Sutta and Abhidhamma Pitakas. The Anguttara Nikaya (part of the Sutta Pitaka) lists the sixteen Mahajanapadas.
  • Jain text Bhagavati Sutra also lists the Mahajanapadas (with some different names). Both Anguttara Nikaya and Bhagavati Sutra are the texts that carry the list.
  • Not the source of the 16-list: Digha Nikaya, Tripitaka (as a whole), Deepavamsa, Majjhima Nikaya, Khuddaka Nikaya, Samyukta Nikaya, Mahabharata, Chandogya Upanishad, Arthashastra.
  • Archaeology: NBPW (Northern Black Polished Ware) pottery; remains at Ahichchhatra, Hastinapur, Kausambi, Ujjaini.
  • Mahaparinirvana Sutta names the six metropolises of the Buddha's time; Suttanipata calls Rajgriha 'Magadhham Puram'.

The Sixteen Mahajanapadas — Lists, Map & Master Table

The Two Lists

  • Anguttara Nikaya (Buddhist): Anga, Magadha, Kashi, Koshala, Vajji, Malla, Chedi, Vatsa, Kuru, Panchala, Matsya, Shurasena, Ashmaka, Avanti, Gandhara, Kamboja.
  • Bhagavati Sutra (Jain) gives the sixteen under different names: Anga, Banga, Malaya, Acha, Vachha (Vatsa), Magaha (Magadha), Malava, Kochchha, Ladha, Moli (Malla), Koshala, Kashi, Padha, Sambhuttara, Avaha, Vajji.
  • Panini's Ashtadhyayi mentions 22 Janapadas, of which three are most important — Magadha, Ashmaka, Kamboja (Vatsa and Chedi are not among those three).
  • Among the 16, four were very powerful: Koshala, Magadha, Vatsa, Avanti. A power struggle among Magadha, Kosala, Vrijji (Vajji) and Avanti ended with Magadha as the most powerful state; its rise began with Bimbisara of the Haryanka dynasty.

Geography Rapid-Fire

  • Of the 16, eight lay in present-day Uttar Pradesh: Kashi, Koshala, Vatsa, Malla, Kuru, Panchala, Shurasena, Chedi.
  • In the north-west (Uttarapatha) were two Janapadas: Gandhara and Kamboja. Ashmaka was the only Mahajanapada south of the Vindhyas, in Dakshinapatha.
  • Six great metropolises of the Buddha's time (Mahaparinirvana Sutta): Champa, Rajgriha, Benaras (Varanasi), Saket, Kaushambi, Shravasti.
  • Matching rules: Pradyota–Avanti, Udayana–Vatsa, Prasenjit–Koshala, Ajatashatru–Magadha.
  • Rivers (2025 pattern): Ashmaka–Godavari ✔ and Kosala–Sarayu ✔; Kamboja–Vipas ✘ (Kamboja = Kubha/Kabul) and Avanti–Mahanadi ✘ (Avanti = Shipra, Narmada) — so only two pairs are right.

Mahajanapadas in Detail — The Eastern Cluster

Anga

  • Modern Bhagalpur & Munger districts of Bihar; capital Champa, at the confluence of the Ganga and Champa rivers. Ancient name 'Malini' in the Mahabharata and Puranas.
  • Champa was among the six metropolises of the Buddha's time and an important commercial centre — merchants sailed from here to Suvarnabhumi (Southeast Asia); essential for inland and maritime trade.
  • Magadha lay to its west; Bimbisara conquered Anga (East Bihar) by defeating its king Brahmadatta and annexed it to Magadha.
  • One source: Kashi had a long rivalry with Koshala, Anga and Magadha; the other: Magadha had a long rivalry with Koshala, Anga and Kashi — all struggled for supremacy.

Vajji (Vrijji) Confederacy

  • North of the Ganga in Tirhut division — Vaishali, Muzaffarpur, Darbhanga and Mithila area of Nepal; capital Vaishali (modern Basarh, N. Bihar); the Gandak flows through it.
  • A confederacy of the Lichchhavis, Jnatrikas and Vajjis; king/chief Chetaka (Chetak) — head of the Lichchhavi republic. Mahavira belonged to the Jnatrika clan (so Parsvanath–Jnatrika is a wrong pairing; Chetak–Lichchhavi is right; Bindusara–Shakya and Skandagupta–Maurya are wrong).
  • Chetak's daughter Chellana (Chelana) was married to Bimbisara; the Nirayavali Sutra says Chetak was chief when Ajatashatru attacked — he raised a united front of 9 Lichchhavis, 9 Mallas and 18 Ganarajyas (of Kashi-Koshala) against Magadha.
  • Estrangement between Magadha and Vajji began under Bimbisara and turned to war under Ajatashatru; per the Bhagavati Sutra Ajatashatru won over all of them, defeated the Lichchhavis and annexed the Vajjis (Magadhan minister Vassakara, per the Mahaparinibbana Sutta, enabled this conquest).

Malla

  • Gangetic plains, UP: Kushinagar & Deoria; two capitals Kushinara and Pava.
  • Buddha took his last meal and fell ill at Pava and attained Mahaparinirvana at Kushinara (Kusinara). (Buddha's death is dated 483 BC, during Ajatashatru's reign.)

Kashi

  • Varanasi (UP), between the Varuna and Assi rivers. One source calls Kashi the most powerful and one of the richest Mahajanapadas of the early 6th century BC (see conflict below).
  • Later incorporated into Kosala. Kashi came to Bimbisara as dowry with Mahakoshala (sister of Kosala king Prasenjit); Prasenjit took it back after Bimbisara's death, causing war; Ajatashatru won and kept Kashi.
  • Under a treaty-account, Prasenjit gave his daughter Vajira to Ajatashatru with Kashi as dowry (bath-money) and accepted his suzerainty over Kashi. Later Shishunaga appointed his son as deputy-king of Varanasi when he made Vaishali his capital.
MCQ Trap / Source ConflictSource conflict — Ajatashatru–Kosala: one account says he annexed Kosala after defeating his maternal uncle Prasenjit; another says the war ended in a treaty where Prasenjit married his daughter Vajira to him with Kashi as dowry. Learn both: war → treaty; Kashi retained by Ajatashatru. Also conflict: one source calls Kashi, another Magadha, the most powerful early-6th-century state — the exam answer to 'initially most powerful city-state' is Magadha.

Koshala (Kosala)

  • Awadh area / Ayodhya division, E. UP; the Sarayu divides it into two: Northern Kosala — Shravasti and Southern Kosala — Kushavati; capitals also called Saket and Shravasti.
  • King Prasenjit — Buddha's contemporary; his sister Mahakoshala was Bimbisara's first wife. He gave his daughter Vajira to Ajatashatru.
  • Shravasti was identified by Alexander Cunningham at Sahet-Mahet in 1861; excavations show the town plan was crescentic (not circular, triangular or quadratic).
  • Struggle between Magadha and Koshala began in Ajatashatru's early years; Ajatashatru later annexed Kosala per one tradition. Mahapadma Nanda also acquired Kosala.

Mahajanapadas in Detail — Central & Western Cluster

Vatsa

  • Prayagraj & Kaushambi; capital Kaushambi at the Ganga–Yamuna confluence near Allahabad; Yamuna banks. King Udayana (Udayan); famous for fine cotton textiles. Iron tools have been found at Kausambi.
  • Avanti had earlier defeated the Vatsas of Kaushambi, which pushed Ajatashatru to fortify Rajgriha.
  • Udayana–Vasavadatta legend belongs to Ujjain: poet Bhasa wrote it in the drama Svapnavasavadattam — love story of Vatsa king Udayana and Vasavadatta, daughter of Avanti king Pradyota; the story is set while Udayana was imprisoned in Ujjayini. Udayana is different from Magadha's Udayin.

Avanti

  • West & central Malwa; divided by the Vindhyas into North Avanti — Ujjayini (Ujjain) and South Avanti — Mahishmati; Shipra & Narmada flow through it. Ujjain, in Malwa (MP), is also called Avantika (ancient name) and was the capital in the Buddha period; also a centre of trade.
  • King Pradyota (Chandapradyota) — father-in-law of Udayana; son of Pulika, who killed his master at Ujjain to enthrone his son; contemporary of Buddha and Bimbisara; named in the Mahavagga (Vinaya Pitaka) and Puranas; ruled about 23 years.
  • Bimbisara first rivalled, then befriended Pradyota — sending his royal physician Jivaka to Ujjain via Dakshinapatha to cure Pradyota's jaundice.
  • Magadha–Avanti rivalry lasted ~100 years: Shishunaga defeated Avanti (its ruler Nandivardhana, Matsya Purana) and made it part of Magadha, extending Magadha's power over Malwa.

Kuru & Panchala

  • Kuru: Meerut, Delhi, Thaneshwar; capitals Indraprastha and Hastinapur (the Mahabharata's city); the Mahabharata elaborates the conflict between two branches of the reigning Kuru clan.
  • Panchala: modern Rohilkhand — Bareilly, Badaun, Farrukhabad; Ganga divides it: North Panchala — Ahichchhatra (Bareilly) and South Panchala — Kampilya (Farrukhabad). Famous city Kanyakubja (Kanauj).
  • Boundaries: Himalayan foothills to the Chambal in the south, Koshala east, Kuru west.

Matsya & Shurasena

  • Matsya: Jaipur, Alwar, Bharatpur (Rajasthan, not W. UP/Bundelkhand/Rohilkhand); capital Viratnagar (Bairat) founded by King Virata; Viratnagar's history appears in both the Mahabharata and Mahabhashya (as does Madhyamika / Nagari).
  • Shurasena: Braj Mandal, capital Mathura (Yamuna); Greeks called it Sourasenoi and the capital Mathora; ancient Yadava rule; King Avantiputra (Buddha's disciple).
MCQ Trap / Source ConflictThe theory table (Wallah) is garbled in layout; going by row order, Chambal belongs to Matsya (after its founder Virata) and the second Yamuna entry to Shurasena (Mathura). Panchala also touches the Chambal on its southern boundary.

Chedi

  • Eastern Bundelkhand and adjacent parts; capital Sotthivati = Suktimati of the Mahabharata; Ken river; king Shishupala (Mahabharata).
  • Kalinga's Chedi dynasty produced Kharavela (see Nanda chapter).
  • Kalpi (Jalaun district, UP, on the Yamuna) was anciently Kalpriya / Kalpriyanath, built by King Vasudeva.

Mahajanapadas in Detail — North-West & South

Gandhara & Taxila

  • Gandhara: W. Pakistan to E. Afghanistan; capital Takshashila (Taxila) — a major centre of trade and learning; Ramayana says Taxila was founded by Taksha, son of Bharata.
  • Persians conquered Gandhara in the later 6th century BC (Behistun Inscription of Darius).
  • Panini was born at Salatura in Pushkalavati, Gandhara; wrote the Ashtadhyayi (6th–5th century BC) at Taxila — one of the earliest Sanskrit grammars: 4000 sutras in eight chapters, each with four padas.
  • Panini mentions wage (vetan) and wage earners (vaitanika) — evidence of money use.

Kamboja

  • Rajouri, Hajra (Poonch) in J&K and NWFP/Hazara (Pakistan); capital Rajpur / Hatak, also Poonch; river Kubha (Kabul).
  • Famed for its excellent horses and remarkable horsemen; in Uttarapatha.

Ashmaka (Assaka)

  • Between Godavari and Manjira (Anguttara Nikaya/Bhagavati Sutra place it between Godavari and Narmada); modern Bodhan, Nizamabad and parts of Adilabad (Telangana); capital Potali / Potan (Paithan, ancient Pratishthana). The only Mahajanapada south of the Vindhyas (Dakshinapatha).
  • [Outside source] Do not confuse Ashmaka (Godavari) with the frontier republic Ashvaka (capital Massaga) that fought Alexander in the north-west.

Rise of Magadha — Causes, Capitals & Dynastic Sequence

How Magadha Won

  • Among the 16 Mahajanapadas a power struggle among Magadha, Kosala, Vajji and Avanti ended with Magadha as the most powerful state; its political supremacy began with Bimbisara of the Haryanka dynasty.
  • The first Magadhan empire flourished in the 6th century BC; its real founder was Bimbisara (c. 544–492 BC), of the Haryanka dynasty. The Magadha region = modern Patna and Gaya, with the Ganga and Son flowing through it; ancient Magadha was the area south of the Ganga.
  • Ancient region south of the Ganga = Magadha; Takshashila lay between the Indus and Jhelum, Chola south of the Godavari, and Anarta was a Vedic-era kingdom — none of them is the answer.
  • Prominent dynasties: Haryanka, Shishunaga, Nanda (then Maurya, Shunga).

Causes of Magadha's Success

  • Ambitious rulers — Bimbisara, Ajatashatru, Mahapadma Nanda — used both diplomacy and war.
  • Iron: abundant ore (e.g. Mayurbhanj, Singhbhum) gave effective weapons and tools; iron ploughshares opened forested, hard-soil mid-Gangetic land.
  • Strategic capitals: Rajgir was ringed by five hills (impregnable); Pataliputra was surrounded by rivers — a water fort (Jala Durga).
  • Central position in the Gangetic plains: southern forests gave timber and elephants; Magadha was the first state to use elephants on a large scale against neighbours; timber aided boat building.

Capitals of Magadha

  • Girivraja = Rajgriha (Rajgir) was the first capital (not Pataliputra, Vaishali, Champa, Anga, Pratishthana or Kaushambi — Kaushambi belonged to Vatsa). It was the site of the first Buddhist council and of Mahavira's first sermon; Magadhham Puram in Suttanipata.
  • Puranas: the first dynasty of Magadha was Brihadratha; its king's son Jarasandha made Girivraja his capital (Mahabharata's Sabha Parva calls Rajgriha Jarasandha's capital).
  • Pataliputra (Patna) lies at the confluence of the Ganga (north) and Son (west). It later served the Shishunaga, Nanda and Maurya dynasties; Ashoka held the Third Buddhist Council here (247 BC) under Moggaliputta Tissa; the first Jain council was also here; Megasthenes's Indica describes Chandragupta Maurya's rule; it was the prime Buddhist centre under the Guptas and declined in the later Gupta period.
  • Shishunaga temporarily shifted the capital to Vaishali.

Where options omit Udayin, the key gives Chandragupta Maurya for 'first capital of his empire'. The theory notes say Udayabhadra founded the new capital — read it as 'shifted capital'. Haryanka Dynasty (544–412/413 BC)

Bimbisara (c. 544–492 BC)

  • First major ruler/real founder of the Haryanka dynasty and of the Magadhan empire (Buddhist texts call him the first great ruler); capital Rajagriha; contemporary of Buddha and Mahavira.
  • First king with a regular, standing army.
  • Conquered Anga (East Bihar) from Brahmadatta; Champa essential for inland and maritime trade.
  • Matrimonial alliances: (1) Mahakoshala, sister of Kosala's Prasenjit, brought Kashi as dowry; (2) Lichchhavi princess Chellana of Vaishali (daughter of Chetak) — mother of Ajatashatru; (3) daughter of the chief of the Madra clan (Punjab).
  • Jivaka, famed royal physician and Buddha's contemporary, served his court; Bimbisara sent him to Avanti's Pradyota for his jaundice.
  • He was killed by his son Ajatashatru, who succeeded him.

Ajatashatru (Kunika) (c. 492–460 BC)

  • Most potent and aggressive Haryanka king, an imperialist like his father; was a patricide who was himself killed by his son Udayin (this Udayin is different from Vatsa's Udayana).
  • Conquests: Kosala (Prasenjit, maternal uncle) — Kashi retained; Vaishali / Lichchhavis (Chetaka, maternal grandfather) — Vajjis annexed, with the help of minister Vassakara.
  • War engines: Mahashilakantaka (catapult hurling stones) and Rathamusala (chariot with mace for mass killing).
  • Fortified Rajgriha against Avanti's threat. Met the Buddha; Buddha's Parinirvana (483 BC) in his reign; he arranged the First Buddhist Council (at Rajgriha).
  • Succeeded by Udayin.

Udayabhadra / Udayin (460–444 BC)

  • Shifted/founded the new capital at Pataliputra at the confluence of the Ganga and Son. Succeeded Ajatashatru (460–444 BC in one source; another Buddhist table gives Udayin and his successors together as 460–412 BC).
  • Nagadasaka was the last Haryanka ruler; his minister Shishunaga founded the next dynasty.
8

Shishunaga Dynasty (c. 412/413–344/345 BC)

Shishunaga, Kalasoka & Successors

  • Shishunaga: initially an Amatya (minister) of Nagadasaka; founded the dynasty (413 BC); temporarily shifted the capital to Vaishali; defeated Avanti and ended the 100-year rivalry; extended Magadha to Malwa. Per Buddhist reign table 412–394 BC. He made Varanasi a second seat by appointing his son as deputy-king there — the exam key credits Shishunaga with making Varanasi his second capital.
  • Kalasoka (Kakavarna in Puranas; 394–366 BC): held the Second Buddhist Council at Vaishali.
  • 10 sons of Kakavarna ruled 366–344 BC; Mahapadma Nanda usurped the throne by murdering the last Shishunaga king.
9

Nanda Dynasty (c. 344/345–321 BC)

Mahapadma Nanda & the Nine Nandas

  • Mahapadma Nanda = Ugrasena (large army) in the Puranas; born to a Shudra woman (Brahmanical: non-Kshatriya; Buddhist: Annatakula, unknown lineage).
  • First empire builder of India: titles Ekarat (sole sovereign), Sarva-kshatrantaka and Aparoparashurama (uprooter of Kshatriyas).
  • Conquered Kalinga, bringing a Jina image as trophy (Jinasena); also acquired Kosala.
  • Nine Nandas: Ugrasena (Mahapadmananda), Panduka, Pandugati, Bhutapala, Rashtrapala, Govishanaka, Dashasiddhaka, Kaivarta, Dhanananda; succeeded by his eight sons (Navanandas).
  • Dhanananda: last ruler; huge army; Agrammes / Xandrames to Greeks (Diodorus); contemporary of Alexander; Bhaddasala his commander; Xenophon describes him as wealthy; credited with Nandopakramani (measuring standard).
  • Last years: extortion and heavy taxes on small items made people turbulent; Chandragupta Maurya with Chanakya killed Dhanananda and founded the Maurya empire.

Hathigumpha Inscription (Kharavela)

  • At Udayagiri near Bhubaneswar, Odisha; main source for Kharavela, the most important king of the Chedi dynasty of Kalinga.
  • Records a canal/aqueduct dug in Kalinga by Mahapadma Nanda (first inscription to mention canals) and his Kalinga victory (Jina statue taken). The other options — Rummindei, Junagarh (Rudradaman) and Ayodhya (Dhanadeva) — are not Nanda evidence.
  • Mentions village grants to Jain monks; Kharavela defeated Chola, Chera, Pandya.

Administration, Law & Society under Magadha

Administration & Law

  • Higher officials, Mahamatras / Amatyas, held many roles — minister (mantrin), commander (Senanayaka), judge, chief accountant — assisted by Ayuktas; recruited from Brahmanas and held great authority. Vassakara of Magadha (Mahaparinibbana Sutta) enabled Ajatashatru to conquer the Gana-Sangha of the Vajjis.
  • Legal and judicial systems replaced tribal laws; royal agents administered law based on the Dharmashastras; punishments included scourging and beheading.
  • Law followed social hierarchy: crimes by Shudras against upper varnas were punished severely; crimes against Shudras treated leniently.

Society

  • Four varnas (Brahmana, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra); Shudras barred from high posts and often slaves, artisans, agricultural labourers.
  • A new category — untouchables — appeared, below the Shudras: lived on the fringes of settlements, hunting and gathering, menial jobs, with their own language different from the Indo-Aryans'.
  • Kinship was incorporated into caste hierarchy: Kula = extended patrilineal family; Nataka = mother's and father's relatives; extended kin Nati, Nati-Kulani.
  • Patriarchal society; women accorded inferior status; the endogamous caste system increased their subjugation; sons preferred for continuing lineage and funerary rites.
12

Economy under Magadha

Towns & Villages

  • Town types: Pura/Nagara (fortified), Nagaraka (small town), Nigama (market town). Functions — political/administrative: Rajgriha, Shravasti, Kaushambi, Champa; trade: Ujjain, Taxila; holy: Vaishali.
  • Pali texts (Vinaya Pitaka) show three village types: typical (headed by Gramabhojaka / Gramini / Gramaka), suburban craft villages (Vaddhaki-grama carpenters, Nalakara-grama reed-makers, Lonakara-grama salt-makers) and border villages (Aramika-grama).

Trade & Money

  • Towns lay on riverbanks and trade routes. Uttarapatha: north-west across the Indo-Gangetic plains to the port Tamralipti (Bay of Bengal). Dakshinapatha: Pataliputra → Pratishthana (Godavari), joined to western-coast ports.
  • Sea travel is mentioned in Pali texts: Bengal–Myanmar trade in the east; Taxila traded with Afghanistan, Iran, Mesopotamia. Rajabhatas protected travellers. Imports: gold, lapis lazuli, jade, silver. Exports: finished crafts, textiles, sandalwood, pearls. Merchant street = Vessa.
  • Earliest extant Indian coins: silver punch-marked (Aahat) coins — metal pieces punched with marks of hill, tree, bull, fish, crescent, elephant etc.; also copper; issued c. 6th century BC by Mahajanapadas, in circulation 7th–6th century BC to 1st century AD.

Taxation, Agriculture & Guilds

  • Taxes in cash and kind; Kshatriyas and Brahmanas exempt, burden on peasants (Vaishyas / Grihapatis). Bali compulsory — one-sixth of produce. Collected by Balisadhakas with village headmen; no intermediaries. Forced labour for royal works; artisans worked one day a month for the king; Kammikas (customs) and Shulkadhyaksha (toll) taxed goods; villages granted as Brahmadeya to Brahmanas and Setthis — revenue rights only.
  • Agriculture: paddy transplantation + iron ploughshare; rice staple, also barley, pulses, millets, cotton, sugarcane; Grihapatis employed Dasas/Karmakaras; small farmers = Kassakas/Krishakas.
  • Guilds (Shreni): specialised crafts, hereditary, led by a headman; artisans lived in fixed localities. Shreni = organisation of traders and artisans that controlled quality, prices and members' conduct, found in both north and south India.

The north-west under the Achaemenids and Alexander's invasion

13

Iranian (Achaemenid) Invasion & Contact

Invasion

  • The fertile, resource-rich north-west attracted invaders in the 6th century BC; small states (Kamboja, Gandhara) had weak leadership and disunity and resisted little; invaders came through the Hindu Kush.
  • Cyrus II (558–529 BC), founder of the Achaemenid empire in central Iran, was the first to invade the subcontinent. Darius I (522–486 BC) annexed the Punjab in 516 BC; north-west India stayed Iranian till Alexander. Taxila was part of that empire.
  • Inscriptions of Darius I and later — Behistun, Persepolis, Naqsh-e-Rostam — give India–Persia information; Behistun lists 23 provinces. Herodotus: India was the twentieth province of Darius's empire. Ctesias was state doctor of Artaxerxes II.
  • [Outside source] Taxila excavations are usually credited to John Marshall; the source dates them to the 1940s.

Results of Indo-Iranian Contact (almost two centuries)

  • Economic: more trade; Persian coins found in NWFP; sigloi (silver) imitations; Indian word karsa for coin is of Persian origin.
  • Cultural: Kharoshthi script came from Aramaic (written right to left) — Ashoka's Mansehra and Shahbazgarhi edicts are in it. Maurya art shows Iranian influence — bell-shaped capitals, Sarnath Lion capital, Rampurva Bull capital. Edict terms: Iranian dipi → Ashokan lipi.
  • Through Iranians the Greeks learnt India's wealth, helping Alexander's invasion.
  • Rig Veda–Avesta linguistic similarity: Thomas Burrow says only phonetic change occurred. Boghaz-Koi inscription (1380 BC) records a Hittite–Mitanni treaty naming Vedic gods Indara, Uruvna (Varuna), Mitira, Nasatiya (Ashvins).

Alexander's Invasion (327–325 BC)

Causes & Campaign

  • Greek–Iranian conflict: after defeating the Persian army the Macedonian marched on India. India was divided into numerous monarchies and republics (Taxila, Porus's Punjab kingdom, Gandhara); the Khyber Pass was unguarded; India's wealth drew him (Herodotus).
  • Entered via the Khyber (326 BC, in Dhanananda's reign). Fought Porus (Puru), whose realm lay between Jhelum and Chenab, at Hydaspes (Jhelum); impressed by his valour he restored his kingdom and added territory, becoming his friend. Ambhi was among the Indian kings who aided him.
  • Massaga, capital of the frontier republic Ashvaka: women took up arms after many men fell; the source says Alexander killed the town's women.
  • The army refused to go beyond the Beas (Magadha's might, war-weariness, disease, homesickness) — soldiers were war-weary after continuous campaigning and had just faced Porus's elephants, and on hearing of the far larger Nanda forces awaiting them in the Ganga valley they refused to advance. Alexander was compelled to turn back.
  • Return route: via the Indus and the Makran coast; he left in 325 BC from Patala after ~two years and died of typhoid in Babylon.
  • Nearchus commanded the fleet that sailed along the Makran coast; his account, preserved by Arrian, is an early description of the Arabian Sea coast. Eudemus was left behind as a commander in the Indian satrapies.

  • The Indus appears in Greek sources as Sindhus / Indos — the ultimate source of the names "India" and "Hindu".

Why Alexander Succeeded & Companions

  • Success factors: no central power (Hemachandra Raychaudhuri counts 28 independent powers), superior army, traitor kings like Ambhi — not 'good administrator'.
  • He did not lose to Chandragupta; Nanda (Dhanananda) was the Magadhan contemporary. Companions: Nearchus (fleet commander), Onesicritus, Aristobulus; Deimachus did not accompany him (Seleucid Antiochus's ambassador to Bindusara per Strabo).

Effects

  • Political: Greek satrapies in the north-west; Greek settlements — Alexandria (Sindh, Kabul), Boukephala (Jhelum); small north-west states weakened, paving the way for Mauryan expansion.
  • Direct Europe–India contact: four routes — three by land and one by sea; the invasion opened communication routes between India and the western world and led to Greek settlements in the north-west.
  • Historiographical note: no contemporary Indian source mentions the invasion — the whole modern account rests on Greek writers, one reason its real impact on India is judged limited despite its weight in Greek history
