0. General Properties of Non-metals

Elements with a tendency to accept electrons and form anions are called non-metals. In the modern periodic table there are 22 non-metals, placed towards the right side.

Physical Properties

  • Non-metals are either solids or gases, except bromine which is liquid; 10 exist as solids and 11 as gases.
  • Display a variety of colours.
  • Poor conductors of heat and electricity, except graphite.
  • Not lustrous, sonorous or malleable, however iodine is lustrous.
  • Low melting/boiling points and densities, except diamond and graphite (much higher m.p./b.p.).

Chemical Properties

  • Electronegative in nature — form anions by gaining electrons.
  • Combine with oxygen to form oxides, e.g. S + O₂ → SO₂ (sulphur dioxide); SO₂ + H₂O → sulphurous acid (turns blue litmus red).
  • Acidic oxides: CO₂, SO₂, SO₃, NO₂, P₂O₅. Neutral oxides: CO, NO, N₂O, H₂O.
  • Generally do not react with water though may be reactive in air — e.g. phosphorus is stored in water as it catches fire on exposure to air.
  • Reactions of non-metals with bases are complex.

1. Carbon (C) and Its Allotropes

Atomic No. / Mass

6 / 12

Group / Period / Block

Group-14 (IVA), 2nd period, p-block

Abundance

17th most abundant element by mass in earth's crust

Config.

1s²2s²2p²

  • Occurs free as coal, graphite, diamond; combined as metal carbonates, proteins, carbohydrates, hydrocarbons, CO₂ (0.03% in air).
  • Isotopes: ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C (¹⁴C is radioactive, half-life 5770 years, emits β-rays); ¹²C is the international standard of atomic weight (amu). Two further radioactive isotopes ¹⁵C, ¹⁶C also discovered.
  • Tetravalent non-metal, unaffected by water; forms multiple bonds with itself and other small atoms (C=C, C≡C, C=O, C=S, C≡N).
  • Catenation: carbon atoms self-link via covalent bonds into chains/rings — this property allows carbon to form more than 5 lakh compounds vs only ~50,000 for all other elements combined — carbon forms the maximum number of compounds of any element.
  • Allotropy: existence of same element in 2+ forms with similar chemical but different physical properties. Carbon's crystalline allotropes: diamond, graphite, fullerene. Amorphous forms: carbon black/coke, wood charcoal, coal.

Diamond

  • Occurs naturally as kimberlite stone; purity measured in carat (1 carat = 200 mg). Famous stones: Cullinan (3032 carat), Hope (445 carat), Kohinoor (186 carat), Pitt (136.2 carat).
  • Each C atom bonded to 4 others in tetrahedral fashion — rigid 3-D covalent lattice → hardest natural substance on earth.
  • Colourless, transparent; density 3.67, refractive index 2.44; inert but highly poisonous.
  • Bad conductor of electricity (no free electrons) but CAN conduct heat.
  • Pure diamond transparent to X-rays; impure diamond is not — X-rays used to distinguish pure vs artificial diamonds.
  • Heating with K₂Cr₂O₇ + conc. H₂SO₄ at 200°C, or in air at 700–900°C → gives CO₂ gas.
  • Uses: abrasive (rock drills, glass cutters, gem cutters), dyes, tungsten filaments for bulbs; sparkle due to total internal reflection.
  • Impurities impart colour; black diamond = carbonado / Boor (commercial name).
  • Moissan prepared the first artificial diamond in 1893.

Graphite

  • Each C atom bonded to 3 others in the same plane → hexagonal array; the 4th (free) electron moves through the lattice → graphite is a good conductor of electricity.
  • 2-D sheet-like layered structure → soft, smooth, slippery — used for marking (lead pencil), also called black lead (though pencil "lead" contains 0% actual lead).
  • Inert to most chemicals (acids, alkalies, dil. HNO₃) but gives CO₂ when heated alone or with K₂Cr₂O₇ + conc. H₂SO₄.
  • Under very high pressure + catalyst → converts to diamond; the reverse (diamond→graphite) is NOT possible under any circumstances.
  • Uses: electrodes (good conductor), dry lubricant (prevents rusting/friction at high temperature), refractory crucibles, lead pencils, moderator in nuclear reactors.
  • Graphite is thermodynamically the most stable allotrope of carbon.
  • Graphite fibres embedded in plastic (composites) used in tennis rackets, fishing rods, aircraft. Artificial graphite obtained by Acheson process.

Graphene & Fullerene

  • Graphene: one-atom-thick planar sheet of carbon in a honeycomb lattice; term coined by Hanns-Peter Boehm (1962); two-dimensional, good conductivity, very strong — used in conducting material for touchscreens/LCD/LED. Graphene is made up of carbon only and is considered the strongest known material.
  • Fullerene: only pure form of carbon (per allotrope classification); made by heating graphite in an electric arc in inert gas (He/Ar); cage-like molecules. C₆₀ (Buckminsterfullerene) is soccer-ball shaped, named after architect Buckminster Fuller. Fullerenes discovered by H.W. Kroto, E. Smalley, R.F. Curl.
  • Fullerene uses: microscopic ball bearings, lightweight batteries, new lubricants/plastics/drugs.
  • "Fullerene = fluorine-containing polymer" is an INCORRECT pairing — fullerene is pure carbon, not a fluorine compound (that description fits Teflon).

2. Oxides of Carbon

Carbon Monoxide (CO)

  • Formed by 2C + O₂ → 2CO (direct oxidation in limited O₂ supply); chief pollutant from motor vehicle smoke; also formed in incomplete combustion of fuel.
  • Present in water gas (CO+H₂) and producer gas (CO+N₂) — both industrial fuels.
  • Colourless, odourless, tasteless, almost water-insoluble; weight equal to air.
  • Burns with blue flame; reacts with Cl₂ in sunlight to form poisonous phosgene (carbonyl chloride).
  • Highly poisonous — forms a stable complex with haemoglobin ~200× more readily than oxygen, cutting oxygen-carrying capacity of blood → death.
  • Bond order of C≡O (carbon monoxide) = 3 (triple bond).
  • Uses: reducing agent in metallurgy, metal carbonyls, Mond process (Ni purification), phosgene/methanol/sodium formate manufacture.

Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)

  • From complete combustion of carbon/fuels, heating limestone, respiration, fermentation.
  • 0.03% by volume in atmosphere; removed via photosynthesis.
  • Colourless, odourless, acidic, non-poisonous; a greenhouse gas responsible for global warming — and, per exam key, responsible for the expansion of ocean water (thermal expansion).
  • Aqueous solution = carbonic acid (H₂CO₃).
  • Turns limewater milky (CaCO₃ formed); excess CO₂ removes the milkiness (soluble Ca(HCO₃)₂ formed) — same trap applies to SO₂.
  • Uses: dry ice (solid CO₂ / drikold) as refrigerant; carbonated soft drinks; fire extinguisher (heavier than air, non-inflammable, cuts off air supply); urea & hard-steel manufacture.
  • Everyday science: avoid sleeping under a tree at night — photosynthesis stops, plants respire and release CO₂.
WATER GAS
CO + H₂
A mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen — a classic MCQ definition.
Incomplete Combustion
CH₄+O₂→CO+H₂O
Insufficient O₂ supply yields poisonous carbon monoxide instead of CO₂.

4. Nitrogen (N₂) and Its Compounds

Atomic No. / Mass

7 / 14

Discovered by

D. Rutherford (1772)

Group / Period / Block

Group-15 (VA), 2nd period, p-block

Config.

1s²2s²2p³

  • 78% by volume of the atmosphere — the highest-volume gas in the lower atmosphere.
  • In earth's crust: chile saltpetre (NaNO₃) and Indian saltpetre (KNO₃); in living things as proteins; main component of urea (46% N). Isotopes: ¹³N, ¹⁴N, ¹⁵N.
  • Industrial prep: liquefaction & fractional distillation of air. Lab prep: aqueous ammonium chloride + sodium nitrite.
  • Colourless, odourless, tasteless, diatomic, non-polar, non-toxic; inert at room temperature; reacts with O₂/H₂ at high temperature (oxides/hydrides) and with metals like Mg, Li → nitrides (Mg₃N₂, Li₃N).
  • Uses: ammonia & industrial nitrogen chemicals (calcium cyanamide); inert atmosphere in iron/steel industry; liquid N₂ as refrigerant (biological material, food, bull sperm, cryosurgery); electric bulbs, high-temperature thermometers.
  • Nitrogen fixation: conversion of atmospheric N₂ into usable nitrates/nitrites — naturally by lightning and nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium); artificially via fertilizers.
  • Denitrification: conversion of nitrogenous compounds back into gaseous N₂ by denitrifying bacteria.

Ammonia (NH₃)

  • First isolated by J. Priestley (1774) from heating ammonium chloride + calcium carbonate; called alkaline air; identified later by Berthelot (1785).
  • Manufactured on large scale by Haber's process from N₂ + H₂; catalyst Fe (or Fe₂O₃ today), promoter Mo (or Al₂O₃ + K₂O today).
  • Tetrahedral electron geometry but pyramidal molecular shape due to a lone pair; colourless, pungent, highly water-soluble gas.
  • Concentrated aqueous solution = liquor ammonia; ammonium carbonate = smelling salt; complex of NH₄Cl + conc. chlorostannic acid = pink salt.
  • Used to make nitrogenous fertilizers and nitric acid; liquid ammonia used as a refrigerant.
  • Everyday science: bad smell near toilets/urine due to ammonia from urea decomposition; liquid ammonia bottles cooled before opening (high vapour pressure).
  • Airbags use sodium azide (NaN₃), not ammonia, for inflation on impact.

Oxides & Oxoacids of Nitrogen

  • Oxides: N₂O (nitrous oxide), NO (nitric oxide) — both neutral; N₂O₃, NO₂, N₂O₄, N₂O₅ — all acidic.
  • N₂O = "laughing gas" — inhalation causes excessive laughter; used as an anaesthetic in surgery/dental work; excess inhalation can be fatal.
  • Oxoacids: hyponitrous acid, nitrous acid (HNO₂), nitric acid (HNO₃) — the most important.
  • Nitric acid prepared industrially by Ostwald's process (catalytic oxidation of NH₃); also by Birkeland-Eyde and Retort processes.
  • Strong oxidising agent, attacks most metals except gold and platinum; Fe, Al, Cr do NOT dissolve in conc. HNO₃ — form a passive oxide film. Called aqua fortis ("strong water") since it attacks nearly all metals.
  • Used to prepare explosives: nitroglycerin (dynamite), TNT, TNP (picric acid).
  • Nitrous acid (HNO₂) is very unstable; its salts = nitrites; acts as both oxidising and reducing agent; used with CS₂ in flash photography.

Ammonium Chloride (NH₄Cl)

Also called sal ammoniac / nausadar; white crystalline powder used in dry cells, as a cleaner before metal welding, lab reagent, drug manufacture, and electroplating. Ammonium ion (NH₄⁺) is a cation.

5. Phosphorus (P) and Its Allotropes

Atomic No. / Mass

15 / 31

Group / Period / Block

Group-15 (VA), 3rd period, p-block

In Human Body

Avg adult contains ~0.7 kg phosphorus

Bones

58% calcium phosphate

  • Occurs as stable phosphates — minerals: phosphorite [Ca₃(PO₄)₂], chlorophite, Redonda phosphate [AlPO₄]; extracted mainly from phosphorite (modern electrothermal process) or bone ash (old process).
  • Isotopes: ³¹P, ³²P, ³³P. Phosphorus does NOT occur as a free element in nature — always found combined.
  • Uses: synthesis of DNA & RNA; phosphor bronze alloy; essential for photosynthesis and leaf colour (deficiency → dry, colourless leaves); aluminium phosphide used as a foodgrain preservative; vast majority of phosphorus compounds are consumed as fertilizers; used in safety matches.
  • "Phossy jaw" — a disease (jaw bone decay) affecting workers handling phosphorus.

"Blue phosphorus" is NOT a real allotrope — the genuine set is White/Yellow, Red, Black, Scarlet, Violet.

Oxides, Oxoacids & Phosphine

  • Phosphorus trioxide (P₄O₆) — dimer, formed when white P burns in limited air.
  • Phosphorus pentoxide (P₄O₁₀) — acid anhydride of phosphoric acid, sublimes on heating, reacts vigorously with water → orthophosphoric acid; regular tetrahedral structure; strong dehydrating agent (H₂SO₄→SO₃, HNO₃→N₂O₅, cellulose→carbon).
  • Basicity: orthophosphoric acid = 3, orthophosphorus acid = 2, hypophosphorus acid = 1.
  • Orthophosphoric acid is used as a souring/acidulant agent in soft drinks.
  • Phosphine (PH₃): prepared by hydrolysis of calcium phosphide with water/dil. HCl, or lab-heating white P with conc. NaOH under CO₂; colourless, rotten-fish smell, highly poisonous; burns in air with dazzling light; explodes on contact with oxidisers like chlorine; weakly basic.
  • Hypophosphite compounds used in body-strength-promoting drugs; main bone component = hydroxyapatite + amorphous calcium phosphate.

6. Oxygen (O₂) and Ozone (O₃)

Atomic No. / Mass

8 / 16

Discovered by

Scheele (1772); named by A.L. Lavoisier (1777)

Group / Period / Block

Group-16 (VIA), 2nd period, p-block

Abundance

Most abundant element on earth — 46.6% of crust by mass; most abundant element in the human body

  • Dry air: 20.946% O₂ by volume; main atmospheric source = photosynthesis.
  • Lab prep: heating (375°C) KClO₃ with catalyst MnO₂ — note: MnO₂ decomposition alone is NOT itself a standard oxygen-preparation method (it's the catalyst, not the direct source in this reaction). Industrial: fractional distillation of air.
  • Isotopes: ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O, ¹⁸O.
  • Colourless, odourless; converts to deep blue liquid on cooling; slightly heavier than air.
  • Does not burn itself but is a supporter of combustion; paramagnetic in nature; reacts with nearly all metals except Au, Pt and non-metals.
  • Uses: oxyacetylene welding, steel manufacture, hospital/high-altitude/mountaineering oxygen cylinders (diluted with helium), liquid oxygen as rocket oxidiser.

Ozone (O₃)

  • Allotrope of oxygen; triatomic; formed at ~20 km altitude from atmospheric O₂ in sunlight.
  • Ozone layer protects earth from UV radiation; destroyed by NO from supersonic jet exhausts and CFCs.
  • Acts as both oxidising and reducing agent; combines with unsaturated compounds (ethene, ethyne) to give ozonides; on exposure to silver, gives extremely bright silver-black.
  • Uses: germicide, disinfectant, water sterilisation, bleaching (oils, ivory, flour, starch), artificial silk manufacture.

7. Sulphur (S) and Its Compounds

Atomic No. / Mass

16 / 32

Group / Period / Block

Group-16 (VIA), 3rd period, p-block

Crustal %

0.03–0.1%

Extraction

Frasch process / Sicilian process

  • Found in eggs, proteins, garlic, onion, mustard, hair, wool; elemental sulphur occurs near hot springs/volcanoes (Italy, Sicily, Japan) — i.e. sulphur DOES occur as a free element in nature. Also occurs combined as FeS, HgS, PbS, ZnS.
  • Isotopes: ³²S–³⁶S; ³⁵S is radioactive. At elevated temperature, S₂ is the dominant, paramagnetic species (like O₂).
  • Uses: production of SO₂, H₂SO₄, CS₂; medicines, ointments, bleaching agents; dye/colour industry; germicide, fungicide/pesticide; vulcanisation of rubber; found in vitamins biotin & thiamine; gunpowder = sulphur + charcoal + potassium nitrate.

Oxides & Oxoacids of Sulphur

  • Sulphur dioxide (SO₂): main gas of volcanic eruption; colourless, pungent, suffocating, toxic, heavier than air; angular structure; anhydride of sulphurous acid; also turns limewater milky (calcium sulphite), excess SO₂ removes milkiness (calcium bisulphite) — same trap as CO₂.
  • Uses of SO₂: refrigerant (liquefiable), antichlor, disinfectant, preservative, temporary bleaching agent for wool/silk (bleaching is via its reducing property — contrast with chlorine's permanent oxidative bleaching); petroleum/sugar refining; H₂SO₄ manufacture.
  • Sulphur trioxide (SO₃): acidic oxide, anhydride of sulphuric acid; dissolves in water → H₂SO₄ + heat; used in H₂SO₄/oleum manufacture, drying agent for gases.
  • Oxoacids: sulphurous acid (H₂SO₃), sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), peroxodisulphuric acid (H₂S₂O₈), pyrosulphuric acid/oleum (H₂S₂O₇).
  • Sulphurous acid (H₂SO₃): strong dibasic acid, forms sulphites & bisulphites; acts as both oxidising and reducing agent.
  • Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) = "oil of vitriol" / "king of chemicals"; made by Lead Chamber process (catalysed by nitrogen oxides) or Contact process (catalysed by Pt, platinised asbestos, V₂O₅, ferric oxide) — both convert SO₂→SO₃. Colourless, dense, oily; dissolves in water with heat evolution; pure H₂SO₄ decomposed at anode gives Marshall's acid (H₂S₂O₈).
  • H₂SO₄ uses: oil refining, wastewater treatment, mineral extraction; explosives (picric acid, TNT, nitroglycerine, guncotton), drugs, disinfectants, paints, fertilizers, detergents; oxidising/dehydrating agent; alums, ethers, metal sulphates; storage batteries.
  • Sugar + conc. H₂SO₄ → chars black (dehydration of sugar).
  • Pyrosulphuric acid (oleum, H₂S₂O₇) = fuming sulphuric acid — SO₃ dissolved in conc. H₂SO₄; hydrolyses back to H₂SO₄.
  • Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S): released in small amounts during volcanic eruptions; colourless, toxic, rotten-egg smell; fatal in large doses; antidote = dilute chlorine solution.

9. Inert / Noble Gases

Group-18 (zero) — He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn; called noble (not fully inert) since some (e.g. Xe) form compounds with O and F, e.g. via clathrate/cage compounds. All are monoatomic. All except radon occur in the atmosphere — obtained as by-products of air liquefaction + fractional distillation; radon is a decay product of Ra-226.

10. Metalloids

7 metalloids — possess properties of both metals and non-metals, placed between them in the periodic table: Boron, Silicon, Germanium, Arsenic, Antimony, Tellurium, Polonium (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, Po).

  • Boron: obtained by cosmic ray spallation; used as control rods in nuclear reactors, bulletproof jackets, semiconductor devices. Borax = flux, borax bead test, water softening, antiseptic, glass/pottery.
  • Orthoboric acid = antiseptic/eye lotion ("boric lotion"), food preservative, makes carrom-board powder slippery.
  • Boron carbide ("Norbide") = one of the hardest known artificial substances.
  • Triborine triammine (B₃N₃H₆) = "inorganic benzene" / borazole / borazine.
  • Germanium & gallium arsenide used as semiconductors, solar cells, photodiodes, transistors, computer chips.

11. Consolidated MCQ Facts & Traps

  • Match: Diamond→Carbon, Marble→Calcium, Sand→Silicon, Ruby→Aluminium (rubies/sapphires = corundum/Al₂O₃ with trace impurities).
  • Chronological order of first lab synthesis: Kevlar → Fullerene → Graphene → Black gold.
  • Percentage of lead in a "lead pencil" = 0% (it's graphite).
  • Statements on diamond vs graphite: diamond is hard, graphite is soft; diamond is a bad conductor, graphite is a good conductor — both correct.
  • Diamond uses correctly matched: jewellery (light reflection), cutting glass/marble/hard materials, drilling rocks — but diamond is NOT a good conductor of electricity (that statement is false).
  • "Graphite is hard" is a trap — graphite is actually soft & slippery; it is fullerenes, not graphite, that are correctly described as cage-like molecules among the classic option sets.
  • Metals below hydrogen in the electrochemical series generally do not react with conc. HNO₃, since HNO₃ is a strong oxidising agent (exam-context statement — note noble metals like Au/Pt truly resist it, while Cu/Ag do react with HNO₃ in practice).
  • Methods of preparing oxygen include electrolysis of water, fractional distillation of liquid air, and decomposition of KMnO₄ — decomposition of manganese dioxide alone is NOT a standard oxygen-preparation method (MnO₂ is the catalyst for KClO₃ decomposition).
  • Oxygen belongs to paramagnetic materials (in terms of magnetic properties).
  • Burning of a magnesium ribbon to form MgO (with heat & light) is an example of combustion / a chemical reaction.
  • SO₂ bleaches by its reducing property (temporary); contrast with chlorine's oxidative (permanent) bleaching — exam keys sometimes label SO₂ bleaching loosely as "oxidation," so read the specific option set carefully.
  • Statements on oxygen — all TRUE: promotes rapid combustion; toxic at elevated partial pressures; rockets use liquid oxygen as oxidiser; colourless & odourless.
  • Statements on sulphur — sulphur is used to produce sulphuric acid, and is used as a fungicide/pesticide (both true); it is also found combined as sulphides in meteorites and does occur as a free element near volcanic regions.
  • Ammonium ion (NH₄⁺) is a cation; iodide, fluoride, chloride are anions.
  • Ammonium sulphide is NOT used as a fertilizer — ammonium nitrate, ammonium phosphate, and ammonium sulphate ARE.
  • N₂O (nitrous oxide) is used as an anaesthetic.
  • Statement analysis on phosphorus: it does NOT occur as a free element on earth; most phosphorus compounds go into fertilizers; phosphorus is used in safety matches — the latter two are correct.
  • Liquid HF is corrosive, etches glass, and causes painful skin wounds because it reacts with silica and dehydrates tissue.
  • Deep-sea divers use an oxygen + helium mixture for respiration.
  • Argon, xenon, and nitrogen are all present in the atmosphere; fluorine is not found free/naturally in the same context.

1. Fuels: Basics & Calorific Value

Materials used as a source of heat energy for domestic/industrial purposes — mainly wood, charcoal, petrol, kerosene, LPG, coal gas. Almost all fuels contain carbon, responsible for heat generation on burning in air.

Characteristics of a Good Fuel

  • Readily available and cheap.
  • Burns easily in air at a moderate rate.
  • Produces a large amount of heat.
  • Leaves behind no undesirable substances.
  • Does not have volatile substances.
  • Easily storable and transportable.
  • High and specific calorific value.

There is probably no fuel that could be considered an "ideal fuel." Gaseous fuels are the most suitable/preferable over solid or liquid fuels.

Defined as the heat obtained when 1 g of fuel is burned in excess of oxygen, expressed in kcal/g or kJ/g.

  • Hydrogen has the maximum calorific value but is NOT commonly used as a fuel — inflammable nature + storage difficulty (though some sophisticated light vehicles do run on it).
  • Fats have a higher calorific value than proteins and carbohydrates.
  • Kerosene's calorific value (~48 kJ/g) is closest to petrol's (~50 kJ/g) among common comparisons.

2. Fossil Fuels & Coal

Fuels generated from residues of living organisms buried for millions of years = fossil fuels: coal, petroleum, natural gas. Present in limited quantity → non-renewable natural resources.

  • Coal formed from buried vegetation via carbonisation (flooding → burial → absence of air + high temp/pressure over millions of years).
  • Contains 60–90% carbon, plus small quantities of nitrogen/sulphur compounds.
  • Grades (repeated from Tab I): Peat (50–60%C) → Lignite/brown coal (60–70%C) → Bituminous (78–86%C, most common, soft, domestic use) → Anthracite (94–98%C, best, high heat, no smoke).
  • Processed by destructive distillation into coke, coal gas, coal tar; also used directly as fuel in boilers, engines, furnaces.
  • Coal tar: black, thick liquid, mixture of ~200 substances — used for synthetic dyes, drugs, explosives, paints, naphthalene balls.

3. Petroleum & Fractional Distillation

Petroleum = dark oily liquid, also called "black gold" / crude oil / rock oil; unpleasant odour; mixture of several hydrocarbons + sulphur, very high calorific value; formed from marine organisms buried under sand/clay over millions of years (absence of air + high temp/pressure).

  • Biogas is formed through fermentation (anaerobic decomposition), not oxidation, aerobic respiration, or reduction.
  • Methane + air mixture is the cause of most explosions in coal mines (firedamp), not H₂+O₂ or O₂+acetylene.
  • Open coal stoves ("sigrees") need fanning to sustain burning due to a lack of oxygen in the immediate surroundings.

5. Petrol, Diesel, LNG & Crude Grades

Petrol

  • Used in light automobiles (scooters, cars); quality measured by octane number — % by volume of iso-octane in an iso-octane/n-heptane mix with equivalent anti-knock quality.
  • Knocking = metallic sound from pre-ignition of fuel in the cylinder, damages the engine.
  • Tetraethyl lead (TEL) or BTX (benzene-toluene-xylene) mixed with petrol as an anti-knock compound — 0.15 mL TEL with ethyl bromide per litre is typical; high-octane fuel is rich in lead content.
  • Ethylene dibromide is added along with TEL to serve as a lead scavenger — it helps eliminate lead oxide deposits from the engine.

Diesel & Biodiesel

  • Used in heavy vehicles (trucks, tractors, submarines) — high power, lower cost; quality measured by cetane number.
  • Higher cetane number, lower octane number fuel is ideal for diesel engines (opposite requirement to petrol engines).
  • Biodiesel: from vegetable oils/animal fats/recycled grease via trans-esterification; safe, biodegradable, fewer pollutants than petro-diesel but currently costlier; pure form = B100, usually used as a diesel additive.
  • Green diesel = best quality (lowest pollutants); Ultra-low-sulphur / "city" diesel = purest form.

LNG & Brent Crude

  • LNG = natural gas (mainly methane) liquefied at ~25 kPa, –162°C; odourless, colourless, non-toxic, non-corrosive.
  • Energy density 2.4× CNG and 60% of diesel — cost-efficient for transport; transported via cryogenic sea vessels/road tankers.
  • Dahej is India's first LNG regasification project (promoted by BPCL, GAIL, IOCL, ONGC) — not Hassan, a common trap.
  • Natural Gas Liquids (NGL) — separated from LPG — include ethane, propane, butane, natural gasoline.
  • Brent Crude: light crude oil, ~0.37% sulphur, called "sweet crude"; specific gravity 0.835; suited for petrol/middle distillates; sourced from North-West Europe.

6. Cracking & Fuel Advancements

Cracking: process converting high-boiling hydrocarbons into a mixture of low-boiling hydrocarbons — used to improve petrol yield/quality. E.g. Dodecane (C₁₂H₂₆) --973K, Pt/Pd/Ni--> Heptane (C₇H₁₆) + Pentene (C₅H₁₀) + other products.

  • Microbes used to extract ethanol from glycerol, cleaning wastewater simultaneously — reincorporates ethanol/water into fuel-making.
  • New solid material (lithium boron crystal phase) developed to store hydrogen compactly, releasing it easily on heating with minimal by-products.
  • E-20 fuel (20% ethanol blend) reduces tailpipe hydrocarbon & CO emissions vs traditional gasoline/E-10, with no measurable drivability/maintenance impact.
  • E-85 fuel (85% ethanol) used in Indianapolis 500 race cars (previously 100% ethanol); reduces pollutants: sulphur emissions 80% lower, CO 40% lower, particulate matter 20% lower, VOCs 15% lower, NOₓ 10% lower vs conventional fuel.
  • Gasohol = petrol (10%) + alcohol (90%) mixture — minimises CO₂ and SO₂ pollutants.
  • Solar Jet project (launched June 2011, EU-funded): uses concentrated sunlight to convert CO₂ + water into syngas (H₂+CO), converted to kerosene via Fischer-Tropsch technology — first proof-of-concept for solar kerosene.

Ethanol as a viable biofuel is obtained from sugarcane (not potato, rice, or wheat, per the standard exam answer).

7. Combustion

Combustion: a chemical process (oxidation) in which a substance reacts with oxygen to release energy as heat/light; the substance is called a fuel. E.g. burning of magnesium ribbon → MgO + heat + light.

Combustible vs Non-Combustible

  • Combustible substances burn in presence of air/oxygen — e.g. wood, paper, kerosene, charcoal, sulphur, magnesium.
  • Non-combustible (incombustible) substances cannot burn in air — e.g. stone, sand/soil, bricks.

Conditions Required for Combustion

  • Air (oxygen) — supporter of combustion.
  • Ignition temperature — lowest temperature at which a substance catches fire; inflammable substances (LPG, petrol, alcohol) have very low ignition temperature.
  • Fuel itself.
  • "A candle" is NOT itself a necessary condition for combustion — fuel, air, and ignition temperature are the actual three conditions.

Types of Combustion

TypeDescriptionExample
Rapid CombustionFuel burns rapidly in a very small time interval, producing heat + lightMatchstick burning, firecracker explosion
Slow CombustionSlow rate, no light produced, heat doesn't raise temperature muchInhalation/respiration (sucking oxygen)
Spontaneous CombustionOccurs at room temperature without external heat (auto-combustion); ignition temp lower than room tempWhite phosphorus, coal dust in mines, forest fires
Incomplete CombustionOccurs in insufficient O₂ supply; forms monoxidesCH₄ + O₂ (insufficient) → CO + H₂O
  • A cracker's ignition producing sudden heat, light, sound, and gas release is termed an explosion.
  • Never sleep in a room with burning/smouldering coal fire — produces CO, which hinders oxygen supply in the body → can cause death.
  • Charcoal does not vaporise, so it does not produce a flame (only glows/smoulders).
  • Combustion of a candle is an exothermic reaction.
  • Non-combustible substances cannot burn in oxygen; combustible substances CAN undergo combustion — both statements are simply definitional and TRUE.

8. Propellants (Rocket Fuels)

Propellants: combustible substances with very high calorific value that undergo rapid combustion on ignition, releasing large energy + gases. Combustion increases internal pressure/temperature; gases (CO, steam etc.) depart from the rear orifice at high velocity as a jet — momentum lost by the jet is gained by the rocket, propelling it forward.

9. Flame & Fire Extinguishers

Substances that vaporise during burning give flames — e.g. kerosene oil, molten wax. Charcoal does not vaporise → no flame, only glows.

Three Zones of a Flame

ZoneColourCause / Note
Innermost RegionBlackUnburnt carbon particles; lowest temperature
Middle RegionYellow, luminousPartial combustion of fuel
Outermost RegionBlue, non-luminousComplete combustion; hottest zone; used by goldsmiths to melt gold/silver

A glass plate held in a candle flame for 10–12 s develops a circular black ring — due to deposition of unburnt carbon particles (not CO₂ evolution).

Fire Extinguishers

  • Water is the most common extinguisher — cools the fuel below ignition temperature AND cuts off air supply.
  • Water CANNOT be used for electric fires (conducts electricity) or oil fires (oil is lighter, floats above water and keeps burning in contact with air) — use CO₂ instead; petroleum fires use foam-type extinguishers.
  • CO₂ gas is heavier than air and non-inflammable — makes it an effective fire extinguisher by cutting the air/fuel contact.
  • When a person's clothes catch fire, cover them with a blanket to cut off the air supply.
  • Fire can be controlled by removing fuel, air, or both, or by lowering the fuel's temperature.
  • Chloroform: anaesthetic in nature, produces phosgene gas (toxic) — it is not itself classified as a fire extinguisher or purely a liquid fuel in this context.
  • Dry grass catches fire more easily than green grass in summer heat due to its lower ignition temperature.
  • Diesel, coal, and kerosene have high carbon % and form oxides of carbon (CO, CO₂) on burning; hydrogen produces only water on combustion → called a "pollution-free" fuel.
  • Kerosene oil does not catch fire at room temperature on its own because its ignition temperature is not reached at that temperature; heating it reaches the ignition point and it catches fire.
