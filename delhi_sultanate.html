Slave (Mamluk) Dynasty

1206–1290 AD · Aybak → Iltutmish → Raziyya → Balban
Naming Note All rulers were Turks, but their houses/lines were separate — historians consider it more accurate to call them the Mamluk Sultans of Delhi rather than "Slave dynasty" as a single bloodline. Qutbuddin Aybak and all his successors were slaves to their respective predecessors. It was the first of five unrelated dynasties to rule India in the Sultanate period (1206–1526 AD), and it ruled for 84 years (1206–1290 AD).

Qutbuddin Aybak r. 1206–1210 AD · Capital: Lahore

  • Born in Turkistan, of a Turk tribe called Aybak; though ugly in appearance, was intelligent and impressive in behaviour.
  • Taken as a prisoner in childhood and sold as a slave to the Qazi of Nishapur, Fakhruddin Abdul Aziz Kufi, who educated him and gave him military training; he read the Quran so melodiously he was nicknamed "Quran Khwan".
  • After the Qazi's death, his sons sold Aybak to Muhammad Ghori at Ghazni. Well-versed in Islamic theology, horse-riding and swordsmanship, he impressed Ghori and was made commander of a troop, then promoted to Amir-i-Akhur (master of the royal stable) on the basis of his talent, passion and honesty.
  • Provided active support to Ghori during the invasion of India; Ghori appointed him governor of the Indian empire.
  • Ghori died in 1206 AD on his way to Ghazni with no son to succeed him; his empire was inherited by his slaves. Aybak, viceroy of the Indian territories, was invited by the Amirs of Lahore to assume power. His coronation was held in June 1206 AD, three months after Ghori's death.
  • Ghori's nephew Ghiyasuddin Mahmud ascended Ghori's own throne at Ghazni after his death.
  • Aybak bestowed on himself only the titles 'Malik' and 'Sipahsalaar' — he never took the title 'Sultan'. After obtaining a liberation letter from Ghiyasuddin Mahmud, he got rid of slavery in 1208 AD and ruled as an independent sovereign.
  • Kept his capital at Lahore (never Delhi) to frustrate the ambition of Taj-ud-din Yalduz (governor of Kirman, who had ascended the throne of Ghazni) to annex Punjab; strengthened his position through matrimonial alliances with rival Turkish chiefs.
  • Nasiruddin Qabacha of Multan refused to accept Aybak as ruler of the Delhi Sultanate — so Aybak always stayed in Lahore and never got the chance to move to Delhi.
  • Spent most of his brief four-year reign suppressing revolts of nobles like Taj-ud-din Yalduz, Nasir-ud-din Qabacha, and a few Hindu chiefs; nonetheless established a firm administrative system.
  • Because of his generosity he donated so much money that he was called 'Lakh Bakhsh' (giver of lakhs); he was the first Muslim ruler who settled in India and actually ruled, unlike earlier Muslim invaders who only raided and plundered.
  • Patronised scholars Hasan Nizami and Fakhr-i-Mudabbir, both of whom dedicated their works to him.
  • Built the 'Quwwat-ul-Islam' Mosque near the Raipithora Fort in Delhi and the 'Adhai Din Ka Jhopra' mosque in Ajmer; started construction of the Qutub Minar in Delhi (completed only its basement) — finished later by Iltutmish.
  • Died in 1210 AD after falling from his horse while playing Chaugan (a game like modern polo) — the game of Polo was introduced in India by the Turks. Buried in Lahore, near the Anarkali Bazaar.
MCQ Traps & Corrections
  • 'Adhai Din ka Jhonpra/Zhonpda' = a mosque, not a temple, saint's hut, or tower.
  • Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque is at Delhi, Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra is at Ajmer — a frequently swapped pair.
  • Ghiyasuddin Tughluq and Alauddin Khalji did NOT contribute to building the Qutub Minar — only Aybak (started it), Iltutmish (completed it) and Firuz Shah Tughluq (rebuilt the lightning-damaged 4th floor and added the 5th floor) did.
  • Qutub Minar is named after the Sufi saint Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki — not after Aybak himself.
  • One MCQ statement calling the Minar "180 feet, five storeys" was judged incorrect — its actual height is 238 feet (72.5 m), a mix of polygonal/circular shapes, largely red and buff sandstone with marble in the upper storey. (Chand Minar — a different, 15th-century, 210-feet, four-storey tower with chevron patterning and Quranic bands — is a distinct monument, unrelated to Qutub Minar.)

Iltutmish r. 1211–1236 AD · Capital: Delhi (shifted from Lahore)

  • An Ilbari Turk of the Eurasian Steppe of Turkestan; before becoming Sultan was governor of Budaun, appointed in recognition of his services during Muhammad Ghori's 1205–06 AD campaign against the Khokhar tribe rebellion — Ghori was so impressed by his courage that he freed him from slavery.
  • Called 'Slave of a slave' — he was the slave of Qutbuddin Aybak (who was himself a slave); he later married Aybak's daughter and became Sultan. After Aram Shah's death (Aybak's immediate, short-lived successor), Iltutmish took control as independent ruler; Amirali Ismail invited him for coronation in Delhi with the approval of Turkish nobles. He declared himself Sultan in 1211 AD.
  • Received approval as Sultan not from any Ghor ruler but directly from the Khalifa (Caliph) of Baghdad, who recognised his rule over all won territories and gave him the title 'Sultan-e-Azam' in 1229 AD via a certificate of 'Khilat' — this made him the first legitimate/legally independent Sultan of Delhi and made the Delhi Sultanate theoretically part of Dar-ul-Islam, subject to the Caliph.
  • Assumed the title 'Amir-ul-Mommini' ("Commander of the faithful") and had the Khalifa's name inscribed on his coins — the name of Caliph Al-Mustansir appears on his coins.
  • First to shift the Sultanate's capital from Lahore to Delhi — the first Sultan to do so, and (per one explanation) the "real founder of the Delhi Sultanate" even though Aybak ruled first, from Lahore. Dr. R.P. Tripathi: history of Muslim sovereignty in India begins with Iltutmish. Dr. Ishwari Prasad: Iltutmish was undoubtedly the founder of the Slave Dynasty.
  • Genghis Khan (original name Temujin/Temuchin) loitered near the Indus river for three months chasing the Khwarazmshah ruler Jalaluddin Mengubirni, but never crossed into India during Iltutmish's reign. Mengubirni sought asylum from Iltutmish, who prudently refused it, unwilling to risk inviting so dangerous an invader. Mengubirni's stay in the north-west (departing ~1223–24) still led to Mongol attacks such as the siege of Multan (~1224 AD).
  • Defeated Ghiyas-ud-din Iwaz (Hisamuddin Awaz) in the mountains of Rajmahal near Teliyagarhi (1225 AD), annexing Bihar Sharif and Barh after Awaz had accepted subordination; appointed Malik-Jani as the new Subedar of Bihar.
  • First Turk ruler to introduce pure Arabic coins in the Sultanate period; introduced the silver Tanka and copper Jital (ratio 1:48), and began the tradition of inscribing the mint's (Tankshal's) name on coins.
  • Per Ibn Battuta's account, set up two marble lion statues in front of his palace, each with a bell that anyone could ring to seek justice from the Sultan; appointed officials Qazi and Amir-i-dad in all cities.
  • Introduced the Iqta system in the Delhi Sultanate (see Administration chapter); also formed the Chahalgani/Turkan-i-Chahalgani — the 40 Turkish slave-nobles later abolished by Balban.
  • His main rivals were Tajuddin Yalduz (Ghazni), Nasiruddin Qabacha (Sindh/Multan) and Ali Mardan (Lakhnauti/Bengal).
MCQ Traps & Corrections
  • Malik Altunia (Governor of Bhatinda) was NOT a rival of Iltutmish — he was the noble who later led the revolt against Raziyya.
  • The Iqtadari system was introduced by Iltutmish, not Balban — a commonly confused attribution.
  • Name of the last Caliph of Baghdad (Al-Mustasim, 1242–58 AD) first appeared not on Iltutmish's coins but on those of Alauddin Masud Shah (1242–46 AD); it is the earlier Caliph Al-Mustansir's name that is on Iltutmish's coins.

Raziyya Sultan r. 1236–1240 AD

  • First female ruler of the Delhi Sultanate and of medieval India — Iltutmish had named her his successor, but on his death Rukn-ud-din became ruler first (1236 AD) before she took the throne.
  • As an individual, she violated the prevailing gender norms of Islam for the first time in India; politically, she insisted power be held by the Sultan alone rather than divided among the Chiefs (Sardars)/Iqtadars, and tried to exercise real personal authority — this provoked the powerful Turkish slave-nobles, the Chahalgani (40 nobles).
  • The Chahalgani played the key role in overthrowing her; nobles under Malik Altunia, Governor of Bhatinda, rebelled and overthrew her. It was Turkish nobles (not Afghans, Mongols, or Arabs) behind her dethroning.

Ghiyasuddin Balban r. 1266–1287 AD · Titled Ulugh Khan, Zil-i-Ilahi

  • Original name Baha-ud-din; an Ilbari Turk like Iltutmish; ninth Sultan of the Mamluk dynasty of Delhi.
  • Caught by Mongols in childhood and sold at Ghazni to Khwaja Jamaluddin of Basra; brought to Delhi in 1232 AD; bought by Iltutmish in 1233 AD after Gwalior's victory, who gave him the title Khasdaar.
  • Rose through titles: Amir-i-Shikar during Raziyya's reign; Amir-i-Akhur after backing the Turkish warlords' conspiracy against Raziyya that made Bahramshah Sultan; received the Jagir of Rewari through Badruddin Rumi's favour; got the fief of Hansi for helping Alauddin Masudshah become Sultan.
  • Married his daughter to Sultan Nasiruddin Mahmud in 1249 AD, receiving the title 'Ulugh Khan' and the post 'Nayab-i-mamlikat' — he was Wazir and heir of this last Shamsi Sultan before becoming Sultan himself in 1266 AD.
  • Followed a policy of 'blood and iron', built on two ideas: the divine right of the Sultan and that the Sultan had to be autocratic. His political ideology was inspired by Persian kingship: the King as God's representative on earth — 'Niyabat-i-Khudai'; he assumed the title 'Zil-i-Ilahi'/'Zille Allah' ("Shadow of God") — the first Sultan of Delhi to establish a theory of state/kingship akin to the divine right of kings. He told his son Bughra Khan: "The post of Sultan is a living symbol of autocracy."
  • Introduced Iranian court rituals: 'Sijada' (saluting the Sultan prostrate) and 'Paibos' (kissing the Sultan's feet); began celebrating the Persian festival 'Nawruz' every year at court.
  • Established the military department 'Diwan-i-Arz' — separating it from the finance department (Diwan-i-Wizarat) — and made Imad-ul-Mulk his Military minister (Arz-i-Mamalik), keeping him free of the Wazir's financial control to ensure his autonomy; redeployed the army across the kingdom to suppress rebellion.
  • After attaining power he abolished the Turkan-e-Chahalgani established by Iltutmish; the only revolt of his reign was by the Subedar of Bengal, Tughril Khan, in 1279 AD (protesting the appointment of Amin Khan in Bengal) — Balban quelled it and hanged the rebels.
  • Called himself the 'Helper of a Caliph' in an inscription on the Mosque at Garhmukteshwar. His tomb, in Mehrauli, New Delhi, built by himself in rubble masonry (1287 AD), was the first tomb built in purely Indo-Islamic style in India, and the first place true arches appeared in India. He also built the Lal Mahal.
MCQ Traps & Corrections
  • The branding system of horses was introduced by Alauddin Khalji, not Balban.
  • Assertion–Reason MCQs confirm Balban centralised all authority AND wanted to secure the north-west frontier from Mongols — both true, but the frontier motive is not the correct explanation of his centralising policy (his kingship theory was the deeper driver).

Jalaluddin Khalji Founder, 1290 AD

  • Established the Khalji dynasty in 1290 AD; crowned at the Palace of Kilokhari (Kilugarhi), which had been built by Qaiqubad.
  • According to Dr. A.L. Srivastava, he was the first Turkish Sultan of Delhi who believed in the ideal of benevolent despotism.
  • Offered the post 'Amir-i-Tuzuk' to Alauddin and married his own daughter to him; Alauddin was honoured with the governorship of Kara-Manikpur for his role in suppressing the revolt of Malik Chhajju.

Alauddin Khalji r. 1296–1316 AD

  • First Sultan of Delhi to establish control of the state over religion; called himself 'Yamin-ul-Khilafat, Nasir-e-Amir-ul-Mumanin'; remained free from the influence of the Ulema class. Barani said of him: "when he attained Kingship, he was quite independent of rulers and orders of Shariat."
  • An ambitious Sultan who assumed the title 'Sikandar-i-Sani' (second Alexander), inscribed on his coins; he wanted to launch a new religion but abandoned the idea on the advice of his faithful friend and Kotwal, Alla-ul-Mulk.
  • Faced early rebellions and issued four ordinances to pre-empt them (see table below).
  • His commander Zafar Khan (original name Malik Hizabruddin) — the best and most courageous commander of his time — died fighting the Mongols; Mongols were so impressed by his bravery and the Khalji army's persistence that they retreated 30 miles.
  • His first attempt to conquer Warangal (1303 AD) ended in disaster: the Kakatiya king Prataprudradev II's army defeated the Khalji force led by Fakhruddin Juna and Malik Chhajju.
  • According to Jayasi, Padmini was the wife of Rana Ratan Singh of Chittor; she committed Jauhar after Rana Ratan Singh's death during Alauddin's invasion of Chittor (capital of Mewar). Malik Muhammad Jayasi wrote Padmavat, based on her story, in 1540 AD — Amir Khusrau had earlier compared Alauddin to Suleman and Padmini to Queen Shaiba in an unrelated love story, which may have inspired Jayasi.
  • Assertion–Reason MCQ conclusion: his Deccan expeditions were financial ventures (per Dr. K.S. Lal, driven by greed of wealth and lust of pride) — not attempts to annex the southern states, over which he had no wish for direct possession.

Revenue & Market Reforms
  • First Sultan to start collecting revenue by measuring the land; established the department 'Diwan-i-Mustakharaj' (revenue) to implement it — but this measurement system was limited to Delhi and nearby areas, not applied empire-wide.
  • Took away tax-collection authority and privileges from traditional officers (Khut, Mukaddam, Chaudhary), reducing the difference between Khut (zamindar) and Balahar (ordinary farmer); took land granted as 'Milq' (property/pension) and 'Waqf' (charitable land), greatly expanding Khalisa land.
  • Fixed 50% of yield as land tax (Kharaj) — first Muslim ruler of India to fix revenue on the land's real income, based on measurement (unlike Muhammad-bin-Tughluq's later Doab demand, which was based on estimate).
  • Initiated a market/price-control policy — per Barani, to support a huge standing army against the Mongols and to check potential Hindu rebellion. Appointed Malik Kabool as Shahna-i-Mandi (market inspector) with cavalry/infantry support; also received daily reports from independent barids (intelligence officers) and munhiyans (secret spies). Four separate markets were set up in Delhi: grain (Mandi); cloth/groceries; horses/slaves/cattle; miscellaneous goods. The policy was highly successful.
  • Started state granaries and price-controlled grain sales — effectively a Public Distribution System (PDS), the first such scheme by a medieval Indian king.
  • Levied two new taxes: 'Ghari' (House) Tax on houses/huts (first Sultan to do so) and 'Grazing (Charai) Tax' on milch animals.
  • Established 'Diwan-i-Riyasat', the market-control department, and appointed a special army to protect the empire's boundaries; the Ravi river became the Delhi–Mongol boundary after 1306 AD (Malik Kafur and Ghazi Malik repelled a Mongol attack under Kabak at the Ravi).
  • Ain-ul-Mulk Multani served under Alauddin Khalji, Muhammad-bin-Tughluq and Firuz Tughluq — not under Iltutmish; he was sent to central India in 1305 AD, where he won Malwa.

Qutbuddin Mubarak Khalji r. 1316–1320 AD

  • Imprisoned by Malik Kafur after Alauddin's death; later ascended the throne on release.
  • Declared himself Khalifa, assuming the titles 'Al Imam', 'Ul-Imam', 'Khalifatullah' — the only ruler of the Delhi Sultanate who assumed the title of Caliph himself.
  • Per Dr. K.A. Nizami, he named Devgiri 'Qutbabad' (before Muhammad-bin-Tughluq renamed it Daulatabad); the mosque Ukha Mosque (Bayana, Rajasthan) was built by Malik Kafur Sultani during his reign.

Nasiruddin Khusrau Shah Apr–Sep 1320

  • A converted Muslim from Hinduism; assumed the title 'Commander of the Prophet' (Paigamber-Ka-Senapati); opponents called him "enemy of Islam" with the slogan "Islam is in danger."
  • During his reign, Hindu gods and goddesses were worshipped in the royal palaces of the Delhi Sultanate.

Ghiyasuddin Tughluq (Ghazi Malik) r. 1320–1325 AD

  • Among Alauddin's generals, became the first ruler of the new dynasty. His mother was a Hindu Jat woman and his father a Qaraunah Turk who was a slave of Balban.
  • Chief of several important campaigns under Alauddin; appointed governor of Dipalpur (and Multan); earned the epithet 'Ghazi Malik' for repeated frontier fighting against the Mongols — Barani credits him with 20 routs, Ibn Battuta claims 29 victories.
  • Took Delhi's throne by defeating Khusrau Shah, becoming Sultan in 1320 AD; also called Ghazi Beg Tughluq/Ghazi Tughluq — hence his successors and lineage were named 'Tughluq'.
  • Fixed farmer tax at 1/10 or 1/11 of the yield; in famine, land tax was collected with regard to capacity to pay; tax-free jagirs granted to government employees without a revenue share; abolished Alauddin's harsh punishments, though thieves and defaulters were still severely punished. Restored the old rights of Khuts and Muqaddams; credited as the first ruler of the 14th century to build canals for irrigation.
  • Founded the city of Tughluqabad in Delhi (built 1320–1325 AD).

Muhammad-Bin-Tughluq r. 1325–1351 AD

  • Considered the most learned and educated ruler among all Sultans of Delhi — well versed in astronomy, mathematics, medicine and other disciplines.
  • Established 'Diwan-i-Amir-i-Kohi' (also Diwan-i-Kohi) — a new agriculture department to support farmers and bring more land under cultivation; a tract of about 30 karohs × 30 karohs was chosen for the experiment, with crop rotation attempted.
  • His most significant experiment: shifting the capital from Delhi to Daulatabad (Devgiri). Reasons cited: per Barani, Devgiri was probably central to the empire; per Ibn Battuta, Delhi's people wrote disrespectful letters to the Sultan and he wished to punish them; he was also the first Sultan to attempt administrative/cultural unity of north and south India. (Per Dr. K.A. Nizami, Mubarak Khalji had already renamed Devgiri 'Qutbabad'; Muhammad Tughluq renamed it 'Daulatabad'.)
  • Coins bore the inscription 'Al Sultan Zilli Allah' ("Sultan is the shadow of God — God is the supporter of Sultan"); his gold coins were called 'Dinar' by Ibn Battuta.
  • Per Barani, five major policies defined his reign: (1) tax increase in the Doab, (2) making Daulatabad the new capital, (3) issuing token currency, (4) a planned Khurasan expedition, (5) the Qarachil expedition.
  • Ibn Battuta (1304–77 AD), a Moroccan traveller, visited India 1333–42 AD during his reign; was appointed Qazi of Delhi, later sent to China as ambassador (1342 AD); recorded his journey (including details of the postal system) in Kitab-ul-Rihla.
  • Per Isami, was the first Delhi Sultan to participate in Hindu festivals, especially Holi; appointed non-Turks and Indian Muslims to government posts — Barani harshly criticised him for promoting "low-born" people (gardener, weaver, barber, cook, etc.).
  • Died 20 March 1351 near Thatta, having chased the Gujarat rebel Taghi into Sindh after falling sick on the way; Badayuni wrote of his death (in Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh): "The king was freed from his people, and the people from their king."
  • Succeeded by his cousin Firuz Shah Tughluq.
Token Currency & Land Revenue
  • First Sultan to introduce token currency (copper/bronze coins) in India — inspired by its successful use under Kublai Khan in China (an earlier attempt under Gaykhatu in Iran had failed); driven by a worldwide/Indian silver shortage plus his expansionary policy (per Barani) — not a scheme to promote trade with West Asia/North Africa.
  • The scheme failed because the state had no real control over minting — many forged mints sprang up, and land revenue (Lagan) began to be paid in counterfeit coin.
  • Raised land revenue on Doab farmers to half the produce, but — unlike Alauddin's measurement-based Kharaj — his demand was based on estimate, not actual measurement.
Mongol-invasion timeline, disambiguated
  • The first Mongol invasion of India happened during Iltutmish's reign (Genghis Khan reaching the Indus), not Jalaluddin Khalji's.
  • During Alauddin Khalji's reign, a Mongol force under Qutlugh Khwaja marched up to Delhi and besieged the city while Alauddin was encamped at Chittor.
  • During Muhammad-bin-Tughluq's reign, Tarmashirin led a Mongol attack, temporarily costing him north-western territory, though he recovered by occupying Kalanaur and Peshawar.
  • During Ghiyasuddin Tughluq's reign, a Mongol army under Shir Mughal crossed the Indus (1325 AD) but was defeated by Malik Shadi, Subedar of Samana.

Firuz Shah Tughluq r. 1351–1388 AD

  • Launched welfare programmes: opened an employment office matching people to posts by qualification/merit; established 'Diwan-i-Khairat' for financial aid to poor Muslims, orphaned women and widows, and to arrange marriages for poor Muslim girls; first Indian ruler to organise the Hajj at state expense; built the charitable/free hospital 'Dar-ul-Shafa/Shifa' staffed with skilled Hakims.
  • Very fond of slaves — kept about 1,80,000 of them, cared for through a dedicated department, 'Diwan-i-Bandagan'; each slave paid 10–100 Tankas, sometimes given estates — a "hobby" that historians judge detrimental to the state.
  • Primarily established the Public Works Department; built (per theory) 200 new cities — Fatehabad, Hisar, Firozpur, Jaunpur, Firozabad among the prominent ones. Per Firishta: 40 mosques, 30 schools, 20 palaces, 100 inns, 200 towns, 100 hospitals, 5 tombs (Makbara), 100 public baths, 10 pillars, 150 bridges. Chief architect: Malik Ghazi Shahana. Every building plan was vetted by 'Diwan-i-Wazarat' against its expenditure estimate before funds were released.
  • Built the largest canal network in India of the era — five major canals: Sutlej→Ghaggar (96 mi), Yamuna→Hisar (150 mi), Sirmor Hills→Hansi, Ghaggar→Firozabad, Yamuna→Firozabad; also dug 150 wells. Per Firishta, also built 50 dams and 30 lakes.
  • First Sultan of Delhi to impose the irrigation tax 'Haqq-i-Sharb' (after consulting the Ulema) — farmers using royal canals paid 1/10 of their produce.
  • Levied Jizya on Brahmins — previously exempt from this tax.
  • Keen horticulturist: planted 1,200 new gardens near Delhi, restored 30 of Alauddin's old orchards, and worked to improve fruit quality.
  • Brought two Ashoka Pillars to Delhi from Meerut and Topra (Yamuna Nagar district, Haryana) — the Topra pillar re-erected near the mosque/palace at Firozabad; the Meerut pillar re-installed near the present Bara Hindu Rao Hospital, Delhi.
  • Commissioned Sanskrit scholars to translate Sanskrit works into Persian, including some 300 manuscripts collected from the Jawalamukhi temple during the Nagarkot campaign — translated by Azizuddin Khan. Established a Translation Department to help Hindus and Muslims understand each other's ideas.
  • Wrote his own memoirs, Futuhat-i-Firuz Shahi, to present himself as an ideal Muslim ruler; abolished 21 grievous taxes of his time, including the grain/octroi levy. Under him, the office of Wazir reached its peak — the Tughluq period is called the golden age of Indian Muslim Wizarat.
  • Levied taxation per Islamic law, permitting four Quran-sanctioned taxes: Kharaj, Jizya, Khums, Zakat (Khums = one-fifth share of war booty). In his time, 4/5 of loot went to soldiers and 1/5 to the treasury — the reverse of the ratio under Alauddin Khalji and Muhammad Tughluq, who kept 4/5 in the treasury and gave soldiers only 1/5.

Nasiruddin Mahmud Shah r. 1394–1412 AD · Last Tughluq ruler

  • During his reign, Khwaja Jahan founded an independent state at Jaunpur; another grandson of Firuz, Nusrat Shah, challenged him, splitting the dynasty in two — Nasiruddin ruled from Delhi, Nusrat Shah from Firozabad.
  • Timur, the Turco-Mongol conqueror, invaded India in 1398 AD during his reign; his own dominion was mocked in the saying, "The sultanate of the Shahanshah extends from Delhi to Palam." Timur's objective was to fight the "Kafirs" and plunder wealth; before leaving, he appointed Khizr Khan as subedar of Multan (and later Lahore and Dipalpur).
  • Timur's invasion destroyed Delhi and began the dynasty's fall; it ended with Nasiruddin's death in 1412 AD. The warlords of Delhi then elected Daulat Khan as Sultan (1413 AD), but he was defeated by Khizr Khan, who founded the Sayyid Dynasty.

Khizr Khan Founder, 1414 AD

  • Was Subedar of Multan, appointed by Timur during his 1398 invasion; later also granted control of Lahore and Dipalpur.
  • Defeated Daulat Khan (elected Sultan by Delhi's warlords in 1413 AD) and took control of Delhi in 1414 AD, laying the foundation of the new Sayyid lineage.
  • Did not assume the title of Sultan himself, ruling as Timur's nominal subordinate.

Mubarak Shah, Muhammad Shah & Ala-ud-Din Alam Shah 1421–1451 AD

  • The dynasty ruled a shrunk, weakened kingdom for barely 37 years (1414–1451 AD) before giving way to the Lodis.
  • Yahiya Bin Ahmad Sarhindi wrote Tarikh-i-Mubarakshahi — the principal source for the history of the Sayyid rulers, composed under the patronage of Sayyid Mubarakshah.
A separate Khizr Khan — architecture note The Jamat Khana Masjid, the oldest monument in the dargah complex of Hazrat Nizamuddin Aulia, was built by a different Khizr Khan — son of Sultan Alauddin Khalji — not the Sayyid-dynasty founder of the same name. The two men are easily confused in MCQs because of the shared name.

Bahlul Khan Lodi Founder, 1451 AD

  • Established the Lodi Dynasty in 1451 AD; sat on the Delhi throne as 'Bahlul Shah Ghazi' with the Khutba proclaimed in his name; his most important victory was over Jaunpur.
  • Did not treat Hindus with religious fanaticism — his court included prominent Hindu Sardars: Rai Karan Singh, Rai Pratap Singh, Rai Nar Singh, Rai Trilokchandra, Rai Dadu.
  • Introduced the 'Bahlul coin' — the main medium of exchange prior to Akbar's reign.
  • Appointed his third son Nizam Khan as successor despite some chiefs' opposition (his mother, Jaiband/Bibi Ambha, was a goldsmith's daughter); Nizam Khan ultimately took the throne with the backing of powerful warlords, becoming Sultan Sikandarshah on 17 July 1489 AD.

Sikandar Lodi r. 1489–1517 AD · Greatest of the Lodi rulers

  • In 1504 AD founded the city of Agra along the Yamuna to command eastern Rajasthan and the routes toward Malwa/Gujarat, making it his capital.
  • Hard-working and generous, but religiously orthodox; worked to advance agriculture and trade; introduced the land-measurement scale 'Gaj-i-Sikandari', later adopted by Sher Shah Suri and Akbar; arranged free meals for the poor.
  • Prohibited 'Tazia' during Muharram and banned Muslim women from visiting Peers and Mazaars; maintained a roster of qualified/skilled persons reviewed every six months.
  • An educated, learned man; had an Ayurvedic treatise translated into Persian as 'Farhange Sikandari'; the musical masterpiece 'Lajjat-i-Sikandarshahi' was composed in his reign; he loved hearing the Shehnai and wrote poetry under the pen-name 'Gulrukhi'.
  • Abolished the octroi duty on grain and several other grain levies. Built the Moth Masjid; his own tomb was later built by his son, Ibrahim Lodi.

Ibrahim Lodi r. 1517–1526 AD · Last Sultan of Delhi

  • Defeated by Maharana Sanga at the Battle of Khatauli/Khatoli in 1518 AD.
  • Defeated and killed by Babur at the First Battle of Panipat on 21 April 1526 AD, ending both his reign and the Delhi Sultanate. Per Nayamatullah, no other Sultan of India besides Ibrahim was ever killed on the battlefield.
Chronology of Afghan (Lodi) rulers Bahlul Khan Lodi (1451–1489) → Sikandar Shah (1489–1517) → Ibrahim Lodi (1517–1526). The Lodi Dynasty, of Afghan origin, was the last ruling family of the Delhi Sultanate; it ruled 1451–1526 AD.

Wizarat & the Army

  • Wizarat was an institution the Islamic administration recognised generally; the first Wazir was Abbas Fazal-Bin-Ahmad, under Mahmud Ghazni's (Ghaznavid) administration.
  • Army organisation followed a decimal system: Sarkhel (head of a troop of horsemen) → Sipahsalar (over 10 Sarkhels) → Amir (over 10 Sipahsalars) → Malik (over 10 Amirs) → Khan (over 10 Maliks). The Sultan was Chief Commander of the army.
  • Barani did not consider the Sultanate a "truly Islamic" state, since the majority of the population did not follow Islam.
  • Government of the medieval period was a composite structure — a fusion of Perso-Arabic, Turko-Mongol (Central Asian), and Indian elements.

The Iqta System

  • Introduced in Delhi Sultanate India by Iltutmish; originally an Islamic-world institution from the 9th century AD, meant to relieve the state treasury when tax revenue/war booty fell short of what was needed to pay salaries — not an ancient indigenous Indian institution.
  • Land was granted to army officials (Iqtadar/Muqti) for limited periods in lieu of a regular wage; the Muqti collected revenue and taxes in his Iqta and used it to meet his own administrative and military expenses, maintaining troops for the Sultan's service — revenue from an Iqta was not deposited directly in the Sultan's account.
  • Siyasatnama, authored by Abu Ali Hasan Ibn Ali Tusi (Nizam-ul-Mulk), is the primary source of information on the original Iqta system.
  • Sultanate territory was broadly divided into Khalisa (land under the state's direct control) and Jagirs/Iqta (revenue assignments to a Mansabdar/Jagirdar, not under direct state control).
  • 'Fawazil' = the excess revenue paid to the exchequer by an Iqtadar over his due share.

Medieval Administrative Hierarchy & Revenue Terms

  • Ascending order of size in medieval administration: Pargana → Sarkar → Suba (province). Suba headed by Subedar; Sarkar headed by Fauzdar; Pargana/Mahal headed by Shiqdar. Villages under a Pargana were called 'Mavada'/'Deeha'; small colonies under a Mavada were 'Nagla'. Under Shahjahan, a new unit, 'Chakla', was formed between Pargana and Sarkar.
  • 13th–14th century Indian peasants (per Ibn Battuta) cultivated rice, wheat, sugarcane and cotton (up to three crops a year), and various spices in the south — but not maize, which the Portuguese introduced later.

Currency of the Sultanate Period

  • Two main currencies: Jital (copper) and Tanka (silver) — ratio 1:48. Iltutmish was the first Turk ruler to introduce Arabic coins and the first to start these two coins; Shashgani was also a silver coin of the period.
  • The name of the last Caliph of Baghdad, Al-Mustasim (1242–58 AD), first appeared on the coins of Alauddin Masud Shah (1242–46 AD); the earlier Caliph Al-Mustansir's name appears on Iltutmish's coins (r. 1226–1242 AD in this context).

The Qutub Minar — full construction history

  • Started by Qutbuddin Aybak (completed only its basement/foundation); completed by Iltutmish. Its fourth storey was damaged by lightning and rebuilt by Firuz Shah Tughluq, who also added a new fifth floor.
  • Named after the Sufi saint Qutbuddin Bakhtiar Kaki — not directly after Aybak.
  • Actual dimensions: 238 feet (72.5 m) tall, five storeys, a mix of polygonal and circular shapes, largely red/buff sandstone with marble in the upper storey — the commonly seen "180 feet" figure in some MCQs is incorrect.
  • Ghiyasuddin Tughluq and Alauddin Khalji made no contribution to its construction.
Chand Minar — a different, unrelated tower Built in the 15th century, 210 feet tall, four storeys, painted peach; its facade once had chevron patterning and bands of Quranic verses in encaustic tile-work.

Literature

Amir Khusrau · Chroniclers · Author–work matching sets

Amir Khusrau b. 1253 AD, Patiyali (present-day Kasganj district, UP)

  • Full name Abul Hassan Yaminuddin Khusrau; called 'Tuti-i-Hind' ("Parrot of India"); referred to himself as a 'Turkish-Indian'; started composing poems at age nine.
  • Court poet specifically of Alauddin Khalji, though he was associated across his career with the courts of Nasiruddin Mahmud, Balban, Kaiqubad, Samsuddin Kayumars, Jalaluddin Khalji, Alauddin Khalji, Shihabuddin Umar, Mubarakshah, Ghiyasuddin Tughluq and Muhammad-Bin-Tughluq — he personally witnessed the reign of more than seven Delhi Sultans (as did his teacher, Shaikh Nizamuddin Auliya, whose disciple he was).
  • Poet, historian and musician all at once; the first Persian poet to depict the Indian environment in his poetry; a savant of both Hindi (Hindavi) and Persian, and knew Sanskrit too.
  • Originator in India of the early form of Qawwali; invented the Sitar (combining the Irani Tamboora with the Indian Veena — called the finest example of Hindu-Muslim harmony among instruments) and made the Tabla prevalent in the 13th/14th century; introduced new melody styles such as Iman, Zilf, Sajgari.
  • Started the new Persian poetry style 'Sabk-i-Hindi' (Hindustani style) — "father" of this style; said "Na Lafze Hindiwast aj Farsi Kam" ("the words of Hindi are not less than those of Persian"); also considered the pioneer/"father" of Khari Boli Hindi.
  • Works: Khazain-ul-Futuh (aka Tarikh-i-Alai), Miftah-ul-Futuh, Ashika, Nuh Sipihr, Qiran-us-Sadain (his first Masnavi, on a historical subject) and Tughluq-Namah (his last historical Masnavi).
  • Compiled a record of Shaikh Nizamuddin Auliya's conversations — actually this compilation, Fawaid-ul-Fawad, was authored by his contemporary Amir Hassan Sizzi (Al Sizzi), not Khusrau himself.

Sport, Social Custom & Titles

  • The game of Polo (Chaugan) was introduced in India by the Turks; Qutbuddin Aybak was fond of it and died playing it (1210 AD).
  • High religious/judicial authorities (Ulema) were collectively called 'Dastar Bandan', for wearing an official turban; among them, Sayyids specifically, who wore pointed caps, were called 'Kulah-Daran'.
  • Jauhar — committed by Rajput women to protect their honour from Muslim invaders, to avoid becoming "booty for the captor" — began specifically during the Rajput period, as a direct response to Muslim invasions (unlike Sati and child-marriage, which pre-date this period).

  • Events in order: Construction of Qutub Minar (Aybak→Iltutmish) → Death of Firuz Tughluq (1388 AD) → Arrival of the Portuguese/Vasco da Gama (1498 AD) → Reign of Krishnadeva Raya of Vijayanagara (1509–1529 AD).
