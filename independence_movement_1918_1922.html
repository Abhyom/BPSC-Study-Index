The Technique of Satyagraha

  • Gandhi's South African struggle was first termed "passive resistance"; later replaced with "Satyagraha" — the two were not identical for Gandhi: passive resistance was a political weapon, Satyagraha a moral power (compared by him to the North and South poles).
  • Satyagraha combined Indian traditions, Christian teachings, and Tolstoy's philosophy — emphasising truth, non-violence, and fearlessness.
  • Methods: boycotts, non-payment of taxes, refusal of authority; Satyagrahis were to embrace suffering without hatred.
  • In the Gandhian strategy, fasting was the first weapon ("Agni-Baan"/fire-arrow), while strike was the last step (U.P. Lower Sub. Mains 2013).
  • The objective of Satyagraha was never to defeat the enemy, but to change the enemy's heart via the protester's own self-suffering — a Satyagrahi should hold no ill feeling toward opponents.
  • Assertion-Reason trap (U.P.R.O./A.R.O. 2023): "South African struggles prepared Gandhi to lead the National Movement" is true; but the given reason — "seeing Indian militancy in South Africa, Gandhi believed Indians were ready for militant struggle" — is false, since his South African struggle was itself completely non-violent, not militant.
Context — Gandhi's political-economic-philosophical self-description
  • Gandhi told Louis Fischer: "I am a true socialist and the aim of my socialism is progress of all" (some sources render it "Sarvodaya"). He is described (U.P. Lower Sub. Mains 2013) as "an Individualist among Socialists, and a Marxist among Socialists" — sharing Marx's stateless/classless end-goal, but rejecting violent means in favour of non-violence.
  • Supported Philosophical Anarchism (U.P. Lower Sub. 2009) — in his ideal non-violent state, citizens would have no need for state-enforced law.
  • On truth and non-violence: "Ahimsa is the means; Truth is the end... if we take care of the means, we are bound to reach the end sooner or later." Non-violence, per Gandhi, is a way to attain Truth, not an end in itself (U.P.P.C.S. 1994).
  • According to Gandhi, the cruellest form of violence is the persistence of poverty (U.P. Lower Sub. Mains 2015).
  • For family planning, Gandhi's prescribed method was self-control (not sterilisation or contraceptive devices).
  • Politics, for Gandhi, meant activity for public welfare — his key structural suggestion was the decentralisation of power to Gram Panchayats; his ideal political model combined morality, religion, and humanity, but had no place for state authority — he wanted a stateless society.
  • Trusteeship principle: applied from his Johannesburg law-firm years (1903) onward — economic policy should be shaped by each country's own climate, land, and people's nature; opposed exploitation of any group by another, or of one country by another. He supported cottage industries (jaggery-making, oil extraction, papermaking, leatherwork) as the path to social and economic uplift of the poor.
Part II
Return to India & the First Satyagrahas

Champaran, Ahmedabad, Kheda — Gandhi's local experiments before he went national.

3

Gandhi's Return to India (1915) & Early Years

  • Returned to Bombay 9 January 1915, welcomed warmly at the Kumbh Mela, Haridwar — his South African reputation had already made him a hero and martyr figure in India.
  • First attended a Congress session much earlier, actually, in 1901 at Calcutta — presided by Dinshaw Wacha — where a resolution of his on Africa was passed. He established a law office at Bombay in 1902 during a visit, before returning to South Africa.
  • Spent his first year back (1915–16) touring India to understand the masses, deliberately not taking a political stance yet, honouring his promise to Gokhale; he was sceptical of moderate politics and opposed the Home Rule Movement during WWI (see the 1905–17 dossier for full Home Rule detail).
  • 20 May 1915: founded the Satyagraha Ashram at Kochrab, Ahmedabad (on barrister Jivanlal Desai's bungalow); relocated 17 June 1917 to a 36-acre site on the banks of the Sabarmati River — later known as the Sabarmati Ashram.
  • Gandhi abstained from active politics through 1916; his first major public speech in India was at the opening of Banaras Hindu University, February 1916.
  • He adopted "Seva Dharma" during his time in South Africa — serving as a volunteer compounder in Rustomji's charity hospitals and tending wounded Boer War soldiers.

Champaran Satyagraha (1917) — First Civil Disobedience

  • Issue: Champaran (Bihar) farmers exploited by European indigo planters under the Tinkathia system — compelled to cultivate indigo on 3/20th of their landholding (64th BPSC) at fixed, unfavourable prices; planters also imposed Tawan (illegal lump-sum levies/cesses) to entrench the arrangement.
  • Gandhi's invitation: local indigo cultivator Rajkumar Shukla persuaded Gandhi (met him at Lucknow, 70th BPSC Re-exam) to investigate; Gandhi arrived in Champaran 10 April 1917.
  • Colleagues who joined the investigation: Rajendra Prasad, Mazharul Haq, Mahadev Desai, Narhari Parekh, J.B. Kripalani, Brajkishore Prasad, Anugrah Narayan Sinha, C.F. Andrews, Ramnavmi Prasad, Shambhusharan Varma.
  • On arrival, Gandhi was ordered to leave but defied the order — a genuine act of civil disobedience, unlike earlier leaders (Tilak, Annie Besant) who had complied with expulsion orders when confronted by British authority.
  • The provincial (Bihar & Orissa) government then formed a formal inquiry — the Champaran Agrarian Committee (also called the Champaran Agrarian Inquiry Committee), whose formation itself resulted from an agreement between Gandhi and the government (71st BPSC) — Gandhi himself served as one of its members. Committee chair: F.G. Slay; other members included D.J. Reid, L.C. Adami, Raja Harihar Prasad, Narayan Singh, G. Rainny, M.E.L. Tainer (Anugrah Narayan was not a member — a repeated matching trap, U.P.R.O./A.R.O. 2017).
  • Gandhi gathered evidence from 8,000 peasants. Outcome: abolition of the Tinkathia system, and planters agreed to a 25% compensation refund of illegally extracted dues (not the full amount) — planters eventually left the area within a decade.
  • Significance: Gandhi's first victory in civil disobedience on Indian soil; also historically significant for joining peasant unrest to India's National Movement for the first time (I.A.S. 2018) — not for all-India lawyer/student/women participation, Dalit/tribal involvement, or a drastic fall in commercial-crop cultivation, all incorrect distractors for that specific question.

Related facts

  • N.G. Ranga opposed Gandhi's Champaran involvement, while Rabindranath Tagore gave him the title "Mahatma" in this same episode.
  • Champaran was, notably, the only movement of Gandhi's first-three local experiments (Champaran, Ahmedabad, Kheda) confined entirely to one district — the Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919) was his first genuinely all-India-level movement (U.P.P.C.S. Mains 2011).
  • The Champaran movement was, at root, a struggle against enforced cash-crop cultivation (66th BPSC Re-exam) — not against land revenue or land grants per se.
Context — the poisoning attempt no other question links to Champaran directly A cook named Batak Mian, working for a British indigo-plantation manager remembered as "Erwin," was reportedly coerced into poisoning Gandhi's milk during Champaran, 1917 — but broke down and confessed before Gandhi drank it, saving him unhurt (69th & 68th BPSC). This detail is corroborated by Dr. Rajendra Prasad in a 1950s speech at Motihari, and cited in Prof. Girish Mishra's writing on Gandhi's Champaran struggle — it doesn't appear in most standard textbook narrations of Champaran, which is why it's easy to miss.

Ahmedabad Mill Strike (February 1918) — First Hunger Strike

  • Dispute: cotton-mill owners vs. workers over the withdrawal of the wartime plague bonus; workers sought a 50% wage increase (given wartime inflation), owners offered only 20%.
  • Anusuya Sarabhai, sister of mill owner Ambalal Sarabhai (Gandhi's friend), sought Gandhi's intervention to mediate.
  • Gandhi, despite his friendship with Ambalal, backed the workers, proposing a 35% increase as compromise; as attendance at strike meetings dropped and worker resolve weakened, Gandhi undertook his first-ever hunger strike ("fast unto death") to rally spirits (69th BPSC Prelims) — this is settled as the first use of hunger strike as a weapon, ruling out the Non-Cooperation Movement, Bardoli Satyagraha, or Rowlatt Satyagraha as the "first" instance in various PYQ phrasings.
  • Owners eventually agreed to submit the dispute to a tribunal, which ruled in favour of workers — granting the full 35% wage hike Gandhi had proposed, after a 21-day strike.
  • This is Gandhi's only early struggle directly involving industrial workers (39th BPSC) — Champaran and Kheda were both farmers' movements.
6

Kheda Satyagraha (March 1918) — First Non-Cooperation Movement

  • Issue: severe crop failure and drought in Kheda, Gujarat; peasants refused to pay land revenue.
  • Government did not suspend land revenue collection despite the drought (correct fact per I.A.S. 2011), and threatened property seizure for non-payment — a proposal to introduce Permanent Settlement in Gujarat was not the actual cause (a distractor in the same question).
  • Gandhi served as the spiritual leader, advocating non-payment of taxes; Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel (along with Narahari Parikh, Mohanlal Pandya, Ravi Shankar Vyas) provided the practical, on-ground organisational leadership.
  • Diverse ethnic/caste groups in Kheda backed the revolt with notable discipline and unity; Gujaratis elsewhere sheltered protesting farmers' families and property, while buyers of confiscated land faced social ostracism.
  • Resolution: government agreed to suspend land taxes for that year and the next, moderating the rate increase, and returning confiscated property.

Chronology traps: Champaran → Ahmedabad → Kheda → Non-Cooperation

Multiple PYQs (I.A.S. 1993, Uttarakhand PCS 2010, U.P.P.C.S. 2017) test the correct order of Gandhi's early movements: Champaran Satyagraha (1917) → Ahmedabad Mill Strike (Feb–Mar 1918) → Kheda Satyagraha (Mar–Jun 1918) → Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919) → Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22). Gandhi's first-ever Satyagraha on Indian soil is settled uniformly across every PYQ as Champaran — never Ahmedabad, Kheda, Bardoli, or "Individual Satyagraha" (all appear as wrong-answer distractors).

Part III
Rowlatt Act & Jallianwala Bagh

From "No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal" to the massacre that changed everything.

7

The Rowlatt Act (March 1919)

  • Official name: Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act; based on recommendations of the Sedition Committee, chaired by Justice Sidney Rowlatt (constituted 1917), tasked with investigating "seditious conspiracy" tied to revolutionary movements.
  • Passed March 1919, during the viceroyalty of Lord Chelmsford (1916–1921), just before the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms.
  • Purpose: give the British extraordinary powers to suppress dissent and quell political unrest.
  • Key provisions: imprisonment without trial and summary trial procedures; trials without juries; arrests without warrants on suspicion of treason; suspension of Habeas Corpus; secret trials with no legal assistance. Popularly nicknamed "No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal" — no argument, no lawyer, no appeal.
  • Opposition: unanimous opposition from all elected Indian members of the Imperial Legislative Council, including Mohammed Ali Jinnah, Madan Mohan Malaviya, Mazhar Ul Haq — all of whom resigned in protest.
  • Gandhi denounced it as the "Black Act."
  • Swami Shraddhanand suggested launching the protest with a no-tax campaign (non-payment of Lagaan) as one method of resisting the Act.
8

Satyagraha Against the Rowlatt Act — First Mass, All-India Strike

  • Gandhi's first venture into all-India politics (I.A.S. 1999) — unlike Champaran/Ahmedabad/Kheda, which were geographically limited, this drew participation across religions, castes, age groups, and genders nationwide, though (per I.A.S. 2023) it remained largely restricted to cities.
  • Gandhi mobilised through existing networks: Home Rule Leagues, Pan-Islamist groups, and the Satyagraha Sabha (I.A.S. 2015 confirms both these facts as correct, alongside the correct claim that Simon Commission protests came later, not simultaneously with Rowlatt Satyagraha).
  • Launch date: 6 April 1919 — a nationwide hartal (strike); a preceding hartal on 30 March in Delhi turned violent, setting a precedent for unrest elsewhere. Violent demonstrations followed in Calcutta, Bombay, Delhi, Ahmedabad.
  • Punjab saw heightened tensions from wartime repression, forced recruitment, and disease; the government responded with a violent crackdown, including aircraft used against protestors by Sir Michael O'Dwyer, Lieutenant Governor of Punjab.
  • Gandhi tried to travel to Punjab to calm the situation but was deported to Bombay; finding turmoil there and in his native Gujarat too, he stayed to address the unrest instead.
9

Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13 April 1919)

  • Context: growing unrest in Amritsar — shop closures, halted trade.
  • 9 April 1919: British officials arrested nationalist leaders Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal (64th BPSC) — triggering widespread protest.
  • 10 April: police fired on protestors; Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer deployed troops, enforcing martial law.
  • 13 April: Dyer's proclamation prohibited gatherings/demonstrations of more than 3 people, requiring movement passes. On Baisakhi day, a large crowd — unaware of the prohibitory order — gathered at Jallianwala Bagh to celebrate and pass resolutions against the Rowlatt Act and the 10 April firing.
  • Dyer ordered troops to fire directly into the unarmed crowd (men, women, children). British official figures: 379 deaths, ~1,100 injured; INC's own estimate: over 1,000 deaths, ~1,500 injured. Over 1,650 bullets fired into the crowd.
  • Martial law followed in Punjab, with brutal reprisals — public floggings and other degrading punishments.
  • National reaction: Rabindranath Tagore renounced his Knighthood (letter to the Governor-General, 31 May 1919 — a specific date some PYQs list, though the resignation-title event is also cited as 30 May in one question); Gandhi surrendered his Kaiser-i-Hind title (65th BPSC); Sir Chettur Sankaran Nair resigned from the Viceroy's Executive Council in protest — a distinct act from Tagore's/Gandhi's, and a frequent matching trap (only Nair resigned from the Council; Tagore and Gandhi surrendered personal titles, not council seats).
  • Gandhi withdrew the Rowlatt Satyagraha on 18 April 1919, realising cooperation with the British regime was untenable — he privately called his approach a "Himalayan Blunder" (though one PYQ answer key attributes the exact "Himalayan Blunder" phrase specifically to the earlier Nadiad Satyagraha/martial-law period rather than Jallianwala Bagh directly — treat both contexts as connected, since they occurred within the same crisis window).
  • Secretary of State Edwin Montagu controversially termed the massacre a "Preventive Murder."
Context — the poem that followed the massacre The popular song "Sarfaroshi Ki Tamanna Ab Hamare Dil Mein Hai" was written by Bismil Azimabadi (66th BPSC) — an Urdu poet from Patna, Bihar, born 1901 — specifically after the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre. It was first recited at the 1920 Calcutta Congress session, and published in the Urdu daily Sabah in 1921. This is the same poem repeatedly, wrongly attributed to the revolutionary Ram Prasad "Bismil" (Kakori fame) purely because of the shared pen-name "Bismil" — the two are different men entirely.
10

Hunter Committee (1919–1920)

  • Triggered by the Jallianwala Bagh massacre; established by Edwin Montagu on 14 October 1919.
  • Chairman: Lord William Hunter (a Senator of the College of Justice of Scotland). Total 7 members — 4 British, 3 Indian: Sir Chimanlal Setalvad, Pandit Jagat Narayan, Sardar Sahibzada Sultan Ahmad Khan.
  • Purpose: investigate disturbances in Bombay, Delhi, and Punjab.
  • Inquiry began 29 October 1919 in Delhi, moved to Lahore by November.
  • Report (March 1920): condemned Dyer for firing without warning, prolonged firing, and exceeding his authority — a minority report went further, calling his actions inhuman; but the majority report still agreed with imposing martial law in Punjab and criticised Gandhi's Satyagraha method as partly responsible for the deteriorating law-and-order situation.
  • Outcome: no penalties for Dyer, due to the Indemnity Act; he was merely asked to resign his Brigade Commander appointment.
  • Public reaction split sharply: Winston Churchill condemned Dyer, leading to his dismissal (March 1920); but British newspapers hailed Dyer as the "protector of the British Empire," and the House of Lords praised him as the "Lion of the British Empire," even funding a Sword of Honour for him.
  • Congress formed its own parallel inquiry committee (including Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das) to criticise Dyer and oppose Punjab's martial law; Mahatma Gandhi was specifically entrusted with drafting this Congress Inquiry Committee's report (U.P.P.C.S. 2014).
  • March 1940: Udham Singh — an eyewitness survivor of the massacre — assassinated Michael O'Dwyer (Punjab's Lieutenant-Governor at the time, and the massacre's chief political backer) at Caxton Hall, London, in delayed revenge; hanged in London afterward.
Part IV
Khilafat & the Non-Cooperation Movement

Hindu-Muslim unity, a national programme, and the withdrawal that shocked a nation.

11

The Khilafat Movement

  • Root cause: Indian Muslims viewed the Sultan of Turkey as the Caliph/Khalifa of the Muslim world; Turkey fought against the Allies in WWI; Britain had promised fair post-war treatment of Turkey to secure Muslim wartime loyalty, but instead dismembered the Ottoman Empire — the Treaty of Sèvres (May 1920) stripped Turkey down, and the Caliphate was formally abolished in 1924 (after the Treaty of Lausanne, 1922, when the last Ottoman Sultan was deposed and left Constantinople on a British warship).
  • Khilafat Committee formed early 1919 by the Ali Brothers (Shaukat, Muhammad Ali), Maulana Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan, Hasrat Mohani, Dr. Ansari — demands: retention of the Khalifa's control over sacred places, adequate territory for him.
  • Mahatma Gandhi was elected President of the All India Khilafat Conference, held under the chairmanship of Fazlul Haq, Delhi, 23–24 November 1919.
  • Gandhi viewed Khilafat as a "golden opportunity" for Hindu-Muslim unity — an opportunity he believed would not recur for a hundred years (40th BPSC) — and threw Congress's weight behind it to secure Muslim support against the British.
  • During this campaign, Hakim Ajmal Khan renounced the title "Haziq-ul-Mulk" (conferred by the British in 1908) as a gesture of protest.
  • M.A. Jinnah (then still a Congress Nationalist) opposed linking Khilafat with the national freedom struggle, warning Gandhi against "encouraging fanaticism of Muslim religious leaders" (per K.M. Munshi's account) — he believed religion should be kept separate from politics.
  • Madan Mohan Malaviya also did not support the Khilafat Movement — a frequent matching trap, since he otherwise cooperated closely with Gandhi on other fronts (e.g. defending the Chauri Chaura accused, Ch.14).
  • 4 April 1919: Swami Shraddhanand delivered a Hindu-Muslim unity speech to 30,000 Muslims from the pulpit of the Jama Masjid, Delhi.
  • Congress's motive in backing Khilafat: reinstatement of the Caliph, and — crucially — securing Muslim sympathy/support for the wider freedom struggle (U.P.P.C.S. 2000); it was not about removing the Caliph or specifically marginalising Jinnah.
  • Outcome: the movement's most cited result is that Hindu-Muslim differences were narrowed at least temporarily (U.P.P.C.S. 1996) — Aitchison's remark, "In this instance, we could not play off the Mohammedans against the Hindus," refers to exactly this Khilafat/Non-Cooperation period (I.A.S. 2000).
  • The Moplah Rebellion (1921) in Malabar is identified as a direct offshoot of the Khilafat Movement (43rd BPSC) — Moplah Muslim peasants revolted against Hindu landlords allied with the British, and the two agitations became difficult to distinguish on the ground.
Context — Tilak's internal disagreement with linking Khilafat and Swaraj Within Congress, Tilak personally opposed aligning with Muslim leaders over what he saw as a purely religious matter, and questioned Satyagraha's effectiveness as a tool — Gandhi had to actively persuade him and other sceptics before securing formal Congress approval for the combined Khilafat–Non-Cooperation programme. This internal friction is a useful bridge fact: it shows the 1920 Non-Cooperation resolution (Ch.13) wasn't an automatic, unanimous decision — it required real internal negotiation even among nominally allied leaders.

Underlying Khilafat–Congress joint objectives

The Khilafat Committee and Congress united around three shared goals: address the Khilafat issue, rectify Punjab's wrongs (Jallianwala Bagh/martial law), and achieve Swaraj. The plan was to run in stages — civil disobedience and non-payment of taxes were to follow only if Swaraj failed to materialise within a year and the government resorted to repression (I.A.S. 2025 confirms this staged, conditional design as accurate).

Spread & Social Response to the Non-Cooperation Movement

Mobilisation & Institution-Building

  • Gandhi, alongside the Ali brothers, led a nationwide tour to mobilise support; ~30,000 arrests in 1921 alone as the campaign gathered strength.
  • Educational boycott: thousands of students left government schools for ~800 national schools/colleges — e.g. Jamia Millia Islamia (founded Aligarh 1920, later moved to Delhi), Kashi Vidyapeeth (Varanasi, 1921), Gujarat Vidyapith (Ahmedabad, 1920), Bihar Vidyapeeth — under leaders like Acharya Narendra Dev, C.R. Das, Subhash Bose. (Banaras Hindu University, founded 1916 by Malaviya, is a separate, pre-existing institution — not one of the Non-Cooperation-era new foundations, a frequent matching trap, U.P.P.C.S. Mains 2005.)
  • Professional participation: prominent lawyers — Motilal Nehru, Jawaharlal Nehru, C.R. Das, Vallabhbhai Patel (also Vithalbhai Patel) — abandoned legal practice.
  • Public demonstrations included burning foreign cloth (significantly reducing imports) and picketing foreign-liquor shops; the Tilak Swaraj Fund raised over ₹1 crore.
  • The Congress Volunteer Corps functioned almost as a parallel police force, directly challenging government authority.
  • July 1921: the Ali brothers called on Muslims to resign from the army on religious grounds — resulting in their arrest that September.
  • November 1921: the visit of the Prince of Wales incited strikes and demonstrations nationwide — Gandhi called for its boycott (M.P.P.C.S. 2024); Provincial Congress Committees were empowered to approve mass civil disobedience.

Response by Class/Group

  • Middle class: initially led the movement, but scepticism arose in elite centres (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras) over resigning government roles; Rajendra Prasad and Vallabhbhai Patel saw non-cooperation as a viable, non-terrorist alternative.
  • Business class: the economic boycott benefited Indian Swadeshi businesses, but larger firms were cautious over possible labour unrest.
  • Peasants: merged their own class struggle with the Non-Cooperation agenda, notably in Bihar (rising against landlords) — Swami Vidyanand and Raj Kumar Shukla-linked local leaders connected peasant grievances directly to the movement.
  • Students: actively engaged, leaving government institutions for national ones (e.g. Jayaprakash Narayan left Patna College just 20 days before his exams, joining the Congress-run Bihar Vidyapeeth instead, 56th–59th BPSC).
  • Women: abandoned purdah, contributed jewellery to the Tilak Fund, and picketed foreign goods.
  • Hindu-Muslim unity: significant Muslim participation despite incidents like the Moplah Uprisings.
  • The Justice Party of Madras did not support the movement — it preferred working within the existing system and disagreed with Gandhi's perceived pro-Brahmin leanings (68th BPSC).
  • An interesting government counter-tactic: to offset lost liquor-tax revenue from the boycott, the Bihar and Odisha provincial government circulated a list of famous historical drinkers (Alexander, Julius Caesar, Napoleon, Shakespeare) to encourage people back to alcohol (64th BPSC).

Government Response & Movement's Last Phase

  • May 1921: talks between Gandhi and Viceroy Reading broke down when government sought to curb the Ali brothers' violent rhetoric.
  • December 1921: government crackdown — volunteer corps declared illegal, public gatherings banned, press censored, most leaders arrested (Gandhi himself was not arrested at this point).
  • 1921 Ahmedabad Session: presided over by C.R. Das — while still in jail (for his defence of Aurobindo Ghosh in the Alipore case earlier); Hakim Ajmal Khan presided in his physical absence — this session designated Gandhi as the sole authority on civil disobedience.
  • 1 February 1922: from Bardoli, Gandhi threatened to launch civil disobedience unless political prisoners were released and press restrictions lifted.

Chauri Chaura Incident & the Withdrawal (Feb 1922)

  • Location: Chauri-Chaura, Gorakhpur district, United Provinces (67th BPSC Re-exam).
  • Date: 4 February 1922.
  • Inciting event: police violently confronted volunteers protesting liquor sales and high food prices; the crowd retaliated by attacking and burning the police station, killing 22 (some sources say 23) policemen and 3 civilians in total.
  • Madan Mohan Malaviya and Krishna Kant provided legal defence for those arrested — of 225 accused, the Sessions Judge initially sentenced 170 to death; Malaviya's defence before the Allahabad High Court's Chief Justice saved 151 from the gallows, though 19 were still executed (I.A.S. 2025).

Withdrawal — sequence and reasoning

  • Correct chronological order: Chauri-Chaura incident (4 Feb) → Bardoli Resolution (12 Feb) → suspension of the Non-Cooperation Movement (U.P.P.C.S. 2001) — the Congress Working Committee met at Bardoli and resolved to halt mass civil disobedience, instructing local committees to advise cultivators to resume paying revenue.
  • Reasons for withdrawal: the movement's growing violence (Chauri Chaura being the tipping point); Gandhi's fear that continued unrest would invite harsher colonial repression; signs of fatigue and declining mass enthusiasm by late 1921; growing revolutionary/labour unrest pressuring leaders toward more radical positions, which Gandhi actively sought to restrain.
  • 24 February 1922: at a Delhi meeting of the All India Congress Committee, further limits were placed on law-violating activities; Dr. Moonje tabled a formal vote of censure against Gandhi for calling off the movement — reflecting real internal backlash.
  • Gandhi was arrested 10 March 1922, sentenced to 6 years in prison.

Diverse contemporary views on the withdrawal

  • Subhas Chandra Bose: viewed it as a national calamity.
  • Marxist interpretation: read the withdrawal as an effort to control/contain revolutionary potential building up within the movement.
  • Strategic-withdrawal view: Gandhi's decision seen as a necessary tactical retreat to regroup, not a betrayal.
Context — Rabindranath Tagore's dissent, kept separate from his 1919 Knighthood renunciation Tagore is often (correctly) remembered for renouncing his Knighthood after Jallianwala Bagh (Ch.9) — but he was also a critic of Non-Cooperation's specific methods, in a separate episode: he wrote to Gandhi that burning foreign cloth was "a ruthless waste" (or "indiscriminate/callous waste," depending on the exact PYQ phrasing), preferring constructive programmes over mass agitation and destruction of goods. An Assertion-Reason PYQ (U.P.R.O./A.R.O. 2021) confirms these are two distinct episodes: Tagore did not renounce his Knighthood in order to join the Non-Cooperation Movement — the renunciation (1919) predates and is unconnected to his later critique of the movement's methods (1920–22).
Fact Box · Overall evaluation of the Khilafat-Non-Cooperation Movement
Positive
Politicised diverse social groups, instilled a genuinely revolutionary spirit, challenged colonial myths of invincibility, reduced popular fear of repression, gave Congress a mass character it had never had before.
Negative
Engaged urban Muslims but arguably fuelled communalism rather than resolving it; struggled to shift Muslim political identity from religion toward secular nationalism; critics (including some contemporaries) argued it prioritised Islamic/Khilafat interests over purely national interests, deepening rather than healing communal divisions in the long run.

Major peasant movements — chronological

Bijolia Movement (from 1897, Mewar/Rajasthan)India's first peasant movement (R.A.S./R.T.S. 1992) — against feudal exploitation; ran for ~44 years, one of the longest. Early leaders Jhinguri Singh and Durgapal (Singh); Baba Ramchandra (a Sanyasi who had worked as an indentured labourer in Fiji, carrying a Tulsidas Ramayana to recite to rural audiences) emerged as its defining leader by mid-1920, founding the Oudh Kisan Sabha (1920).

Grievances: rents 50% higher than recorded rates, oppression by revenue-collecting Thikedars, share-rent practices. Demand: convert rent-in-kind to a fixed cash payment, paid on time (39th BPSC) — peasants did not refuse to pay rent outright, they insisted on cash collection given high inflation. Severe repression ended it by March 1922.

Published the Hindi weekly Hunkar from Patna (63rd BPSC) — not Lok Sangraha (a distractor from another question, 70th BPSC, that is actually incorrect per the more specific 63rd BPSC answer). Assisted by Karyanand Sharma, Rahul Sankrityayan, Panchanan Sharma, Yadunandan Sharma.
All India Kisan Congress / Sabha (11 April 1936, Lucknow)Swami Sahajanand Saraswati — first President; N.G. Ranga — first General Secretary. Later renamed All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS); held its second session at Faizpur alongside the 1936 Congress session there, chaired by N.G. Ranga. Jawaharlal Nehru addressed its first meeting. Just before his death (1950), Sahajanand formed the All India United Kisan Sabha, demanding nationalisation of land, waterways, and energy sources.
Bardoli Satyagraha (1928, Surat district)Led by Vallabhbhai Patel — a peasant tax-refusal campaign; its success led the women of Bardoli to confer on Patel the title "Sardar."

Most affected: districts of North Bengal.
Bhoodan Movement (from 18 April 1951, Pochampalli, Telangana)Started by Acharya Vinoba Bhave at Pochampally (Nalgonda district, then a communist-activity hub); landlord Ramachandra Reddy donated 100 acres to 40 Harijan families — the movement's founding gift. Vinoba set a target of 50 million acres by 1957; most successful in Odisha. Jaya Prakash Narayan joined the movement in 1954, leaving active politics.

Trade Unions & the Communist Party of India

Early Trade Unionism

  • Madras Labour Union (1918) — India's first modern trade union, founded by B.P. Wadia and V. Kalyanasundaram, covering workers of the Buckingham and Carnatic Mills; soon followed by two unions in Bombay, one in Calcutta (the Indian Seamen's Union), and four more in Madras.
  • Ahmedabad Textile Labour Association — established 25 February 1920 by Anasuya Sarabhai (its President) and Mahatma Gandhi, emerging directly out of the 1918 Ahmedabad Mill Strike (Ch.5).
  • All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) — founded 31 October 1920, Bombay, by N.M. Joshi; first President Lala Lajpat Rai, Vice-President Joseph Baptista, General Secretary Diwan Chaman Lal. Motivated by the founding of the International Labour Organization (ILO) in 1919, prompting Indian workers to organise formally.
  • AITUC first split at its Nagpur session, 1929 — presided by Jawaharlal Nehru — a leftist-vs-moderate power struggle; the moderate faction (N.M. Joshi, V.V. Giri, B. Shiva Rao) broke away to form the Indian Trade Union Federation — not founded by S.A. Dange, a repeated matching trap (Uttarakhand PCS 2025).

The Communist Thread

  • Communist International (Comintern) founded March 1919 by Vladimir Lenin and Russia's Bolshevik Party.
  • M.N. Roy — the first Indian to join the Communist International, visiting Russia at Lenin's invitation; on 17 October 1920, Roy headed a group at Tashkent (with Abani Mukherjee, Mohd. Ali, Mohd. Shafique, Evelyn Roy Trent, Rosa Fitingov, M.P.T. Acharya) founding the Communist Party of India there.
  • Kanpur Conspiracy Case (1924): British government prosecuted early communists — M.N. Roy, S.A. Dange, Ghulam Hussain, Ramcharan Lal Sharma, Muzaffar Ahmed, Shaukat Usmani, Singaravelu Chettiar, Nalini Gupta, Shamuddin Hassan, M.R.S. Velayndhun, Dr. Manilal, Sampurnanand, Satyabhakta — charged with conspiring "to deprive the Emperor of his sovereignty over British India... through a violent revolution."
  • Revolutionary phase of the Trade Union Movement: 1926–1939 (48th–52nd BPSC) — key leaders M.N. Roy, Muzaffar Ahmed, Shripad Amrit Dange, Shaukat Usmani.
  • Meerut Conspiracy Case (1929–33): 32 labour leaders charged, 31 arrested (including two Britishers) — drew worldwide sympathetic attention from figures like Albert Einstein, H.G. Wells, Harold Laski, and (F.D.) Roosevelt (U.P.P.C.S. 2017) — a rare instance of an Indian political trial drawing this level of international celebrity solidarity.
  • Workers and Peasants Party: formed regionally (Bengal 1926, Bombay 1927, U.P. 1928, Punjab as the Kirti Kisan Party) before uniting as the All India Workers and Peasants Party at a Calcutta conference, December 1928 — its actual purpose was to work within Congress to push it toward a more revolutionary, mass-organisation orientation (U.P.P.C.S. 2024) — not as an independent all-India body from the outset.
  • Splinter/successor parties: M.N. Roy founded the Radical Democratic Party (1940); Saumyendranath Tagore founded the Revolutionary Communist Party of India (1934), breaking away from the CPI.
