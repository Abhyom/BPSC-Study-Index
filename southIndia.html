01 Sangam Age — Society, Assemblies & Literature

Includes facts drawn from Q47–Q54, Q60–Q66, Q72, Q76–Q78, Q92.

According to Sangam texts, the terms Kon, Ko and Mannan were all attributed to the King. Sangam literally means a "Conference of Poets" or "Forum of Scholars." The literature produced at these conferences — the oldest in South India, made of several anthologies of poems — is called "Sangam Literature." Three Sangams were held under the patronage of the Pandya kings.

Agastya is the sage credited with having "Aryanised" South India. Born in Kashi, tradition holds he travelled south at the request of the Gods and settled there. He is also known as the "Father of Tamil Literature."

Tolkappiyam

Tolkappiyam is both the residual text of the (Second) Sangam era and the most ancient available text of Tamil literature / oldest extant Tamil grammar text. Its author, Tolkappiyar, was one of the twelve disciples of Saint Agastya. It is a treatise on grammar and poetry, written in formula (sutra) style, structured in three parts across nine sections, each dealing with Ezhuttu (letter), Col (word) and Porul (subject matter).

Major Sangam-era Tamil Works

Author & theme ledger

  • Silappadikaram — author Ilango Adigal; narrates the tragic story of Kannagi and her husband Kovalan. Classified under theme: Love Story.
  • Kural (Thirukural) — considered the "Ethical Bible of Tamil" and the "Laghuveda of Tamil literature"; also called Muppal; author Tiruvalluvar. Classified under theme: Philosophy. By hearsay, Tiruvalluvar was considered an "advent of Brahma."
  • Ramavataram (Tamil Ramayanam) — authored by Kamban.
  • Manimekalai — classified under theme: Merchant Story.

⚠ Source conflict — Silappadikaram's author

One part of the text calls Ilango Adigal the "second son of King Nedun-ceralatan of the Chera dynasty." An MCQ explanation elsewhere in the same source instead calls him the "grandson of Chola king Karikal." Both versions appear verbatim in the source — remember both for objective-question purposes; the safe common ground is simply "author of Silappadikaram."

Devotional & Later Tamil Traditions

Thirumurai is considered the "Fifth Veda" — a twelve-volume compendium of Saivite hymns from the 6th to 11th century CE. Along with the Vedas and Saiva Agamas, it forms the basis of Saiva Siddhanta philosophy in South India. Devotee-poets are grouped as Alvars (Vishnu devotees, 12 in number) and Nayanars (Shiva devotees, 63 in number).

Siddhas (Sittars) of the Tamil region trace back to the Sangam period (though the word "Siddha" itself is not found in Sangam literature) and are closely linked to Shaivism, Siddhanta philosophy and Tantra. They were monotheistic and condemned idolatry. Separately, Lingayats of the Kannada region questioned the theory of rebirth and rejected caste hierarchy.

Sangam Society & Custom

Sangam poems are not devoid of material culture — they actively describe it. The social classification of Varna was known to Sangam poets, and the poems reference both warrior ethic and magical forces in nature.

Vattakirutal was a Tamil ritual practice, prevalent mainly during the Sangam period, describing a king defeated in battle committing ritual suicide by starving himself to death.

⚠ Trap — which dynasties appear in Sangam Literature?

Chera, Chola and Pandya are all mentioned in Sangam literature. Kadamba and Pallava are NOT — the Pallavas, though a major South Indian dynasty, were not rulers of the Tamil country during the Sangam Age itself.

Udiyanjiral belonged to the Chera dynasty and is considered the first historical king of the Sangam period; legend holds he fed all the fighters on both sides of the Mahabharata war.

02 Chera Dynasty

The Chera kingdom was located in present-day Kerala, extending into parts of Tamil Nadu. Its capital is recorded as Vanchi — also identified with Karuvur/Karur, and in some sources as Thondi.

India's trade relations with the Roman Empire began with the Chera kingdom during the Sangam period; large numbers of Roman objects have been recovered from Arikamedu (Puducherry).

Its first historical king, per legend, was Udiyanjiral (see Sangam Age section above).

03 Chola Dynasty

Draws on Q1–Q36 and administrative/military detail scattered across the source.

Origins

The history of the early Cholas belongs to Sangam literature; there is an interregnum between the fall of the Sangam Chola and the rise of the Medieval Chola under Vijayalaya (850–871 AD) in the 9th century. Vijayalaya is the answer given across multiple MCQs (67th BPSC 2022, RAS/RTS 2016) as the founder of the Chola dynasty.

⚠ Trap — "founder" vs "established"

One explanation in the source states plainly that "Vijayalaya-I" established the Chola dynasty — consistent with the MCQ answers. But a different explanation (Q17) instead says "Chola dynasty was established by Parantak-I" before describing Parantaka-I's own conquests. Treat Vijayalaya as the safe MCQ answer for "founder"; treat Parantaka-I's achievements as separate (below) rather than as founding acts.

Parantaka-I (reign 907–953 AD) defeated the Madurai/Pandya king Rajasimha and assumed the title "Madurai-Kond." His son Rajaditya fought and died at the Battle of Takkolam (see Military section).

Rajaraja-I — the Golden Era Begins

The golden era of the Chola empire began with Rajaraja-I's accession (985 AD). He was the first Chola king to create a Naval Army. He defeated the Chera naval fleet at Kandalur Salai (Trivandrum) — recorded in his inscriptions as "Kāndalūr śālai kalam-arutta" — and, per the Larger Leiden Copper Plates and Tiruvālangādu inscription, captured the Pandya king Amarabhujanga (or his general) and overran Madurai, assuming titles such as "Mummudi Cholan" (Three-crowned Chola) and "Pandya-kula-shani." He also invaded Sri Lanka and destroyed Anuradhapura, capturing only the northern part of the island (the south stayed independent until his son's reign). He built the Brihadeeswara Temple at Thanjavur.

Because Rajaraja-I began the practice of inscribing victory-narratives on temple walls — a tradition his successors continued — historians have far more information about the Cholas than about their predecessors.

Rajendra-I — "Gangaikonda Chola"

Rajendra Chola-I, son and successor of Rajaraja-I, is considered one of the greatest rulers and military generals of India. He completed the conquest of Sri Lanka, bringing its king Mahinda-V to the Chola kingdom as a prisoner, and defeated the Pala ruler Mahipala, after which he adopted the title "Gangaikonda Chola" and founded a new capital, Gangaikonda Cholapuram. His navy — the strongest in the region — effectively converted the Bay of Bengal into a "Chola Lake." He built the artificial lake Chola Gangam Ponneri to commemorate his victory over the rulers of the Ganga river basin, including Bengal and Kalinga kings. He also sent an expedition against the Sailendra empire of South-East Asia (partly to ease trade friction with China), and in 1025 CE led a successful naval campaign against Kadaram (Kedah) and the maritime empire of Srivijaya (Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Java).

Later Rulers

Under Kulottunga-I, in 1077 AD, a Chola Buddhist trade delegation of 72 merchants was sent to China. When Vijayabahu, the Ceylon king, proclaimed Sinhala independence during Kulottunga-I's reign, Kulottunga did not retaliate — instead marrying his daughter to the Sinhala prince Virapperumal.

Generally, Chola emperors elected their "Yuvraj" (heir) during their own tenure.

Administration

A defining feature of Chola governance was autonomy of village administration — implemented uniquely and systematically during the Chola era. The Uttaramerur inscription gives a detailed account of the executive committee of the Gram Sabha (village assembly): every village had its own 'Sabha', largely independent of central command.

Administrative hierarchy (largest → smallest)

  • Mandalam — province; the empire was divided into six such provinces.
  • Kottam / Valanadu — subdivision of a Mandalam, functioning like a commissionerate.
  • Nadu — district; several Nadus made up a Kottam. The assembly of a Nadu was called Nattar.
  • Kurram — village association.
  • Gram Sabha — the smallest administrative unit.

Land & institution terms (I.A.S. 2016 matching set)

  • Eripatti — land whose revenue was set apart for maintenance of the village tank.
  • Taniyurs — villages donated to a single Brahmin or group of Brahmins; a very large village administered as a single unit.
  • Ghatikas — colleges generally attached to temples.

Territorially, Chola lands stretched from the banks of the Tungabhadra to the Krishna river, spanning the Coromandel Coast and parts of the Deccan — including Uraiyur, Kaveripattinam and Thanjavur. Capitals shifted over time: Uraiyur (Sangam era) → Thanjavur / Tanjore → later Gangaikondacholapuram.

Military — the Battle of Takkolam

The Battle of Takkolam (949 AD) was fought between Rajaditya, son of Parantaka-I, and a confederacy of the Western Gangas, Banas and Vaidunbas led by the Rashtrakuta king Krishna-III. The Cholas were defeated and Rajaditya died on the battlefield.

Navy

The Chola Kings maintained a powerful standing army and navy. The Chola navy was central to imperial expansion — the conquest of Ceylon and naval raids on Srivijaya, Sinhala and the Maldives.

Art & Iconography

Chola artists (sthapatis) excelled at carving stone and, even more prolifically, casting bronze sculptures. The Nataraja (dancing Shiva) bronzes are counted among the world's finest sculptures, invariably depicted with four arms: the damru (drum of creation) in the upper right hand, agni (destructive fire) in the upper left, the lower right hand forming abhayamudra, and the lower left pointing to the raised left foot — signifying refuge for the troubled soul. In the right ear he wears a man's earring, in the left a woman's — a symbol of Ardhanarishwara. A single Nataraja image thus depicts Shiva as creator, preserver and destroyer at once.

The Dakshinamurti idol depicts Shiva as a mentor/Guru/Teacher, imparting knowledge to devotees, and is conventionally installed facing south.

Decline

Around 1250 AD, the Hoysala and Pandya kingdoms defeated the Chola king Rajendra III; the dynasty effectively ended by ~1279 AD. Malik Kafur subsequently conquered South India around 1310 AD, overrunning the Yadava, Hoysala, Kakatiya and Pandya kingdoms — the source itself marks the question of who "devastated" the Choles as unresolved/ambiguous (answer marked "*").

04 Pallava Dynasty

Capital: Kanchipuram (Kanchi).

Mahendravarman-I (600–630 AD) — titles Vichitrachitta, Gunabhara, Mattavilasa; composed the Sanskrit comic play "Mattavilasaprahasana"; a noted scholar and patron of art/literature; titles recorded in the Mandagapattu rock-cut Trimurti temple inscription.
Simhavishnu (575–600 AD) — assumed the title Avanisimha; defeated the kings of Chola, Pandya, Singhal (Ceylon) and Kalabhra.
Narasimhavarman-I (630–668 AD) — title "Mahamalla" (great wrestler); built the famous Panch Ratha temples; captured and destroyed the Chalukya capital Vatapi, assuming the title "Vatapikonda"; sent two naval expeditions to Ceylon in 642 AD to aid a Sri Lankan prince.
Parameshvaravarman-I (~670–700 AD) — titles Lokaditya, Ekmalla, Ranajaya, Atyantakama, Ugradanda, Gunabhajana.
Nandivarman-II (731–795 AD).

Correct chronological order of these rulers: Mahendravarman I → Narasimhavarman I → Parameshvaravarman I → Nandivarman II.

Panamalai is associated with Pallava-era art/architecture.

05 Chalukya Dynasty (of Vatapi/Badami)

Capital: Vatapi — today's Badami, Bagalkot district, Karnataka — Chalukya capital through the 6th–8th centuries. Pulakesin-I was the real founder of the Vatapi Chalukya line.

Pulakesin-II (reign 610–642 AD) was the most capable and powerful Chalukya ruler; he defeated Harshavardhana in 618 AD. The Aihole inscription, composed by Ravikirti (in Sanskrit and southern Brahmi script), records his achievements — the author claims fame comparable to poets Kalidasa and Bharavi, making this the source where Kalidasa's name is epigraphically attested.

The Chalukyas never ruled North India — their base remained the Deccan and South India; a later, prominent offshoot were the Chalukyas of Kalyani.

Women in Chalukya administration

Chalukya administration was notable for assigning women high office. Vijaya Bhattarika, queen of Chandraditya (brother of Vikramaditya-I), issued two copper plates in her own name and was an accomplished poetess; she ran the administration after her husband's death. Vijayaditya (per one line) / Vikramaditya (per another) granted a village to a Jain monastery on the recommendation of his younger sister Kumkumdevi.

Other Chalukya notes

Ma-twa-lin, a Chinese traveller, recorded the relationship between China and India during the Chalukya period.

Kumarapala, a later Chalukya king who reigned 29 years, was a Jain and disciple of the scholar Hemachandra; per Jain tradition, he opposed animal cruelty and sent ministers to suppress cruelty against animals in the Kashi region.

06 Pandya Dynasty

The Pandyas had the longest reign of any South Indian dynasty — roughly 200 BC to 1378 AD. Their capital moved from Korkai (early phase) to Madurai (later phase). A Pandya king sent an ambassador to Rome in 26 BC. Kadungon is listed among Pandya rulers.

The Meenakshi Temple at Madurai was originally built by Kulasekara Pandya; the "lotus-shaped city" of Madurai is said to completely surround it.

⚠ Trap — the Pandya lifeline river's name

One passage names the lifeline river of the Pandya kingdom as the Vaigai (originating near the Varushanadu hills, Madurai); another MCQ explanation in the same source calls it the Vengi river (originating at "Kandan Manikanur" near Madurai) and separately says the Pandya region "was famous for its fertility." Both names are given verbatim in the source for the same river/context — the geographically standard river associated with Madurai is the Vaigai, so treat "Vengi" as a source inconsistency.

The Pandya kingdom lay to the south of the Cauvery/Kaveri, comprising the districts of modern Madurai and Tirunelveli, and extending into parts of Travancore/Kerala.

07 Trade, Ports & Indo-Roman Contact

The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, written by an anonymous Greek sailor, lists the ports of the Indian coasts. On the western coast, the main ports were Tondi and Mushiri (Muziris), along with Naura and Nelisanda. Korkai and Puhar/Poompuhar, by contrast, were eastern-coast ports, not western.

Arikamedu

Arikamedu, an archaeological site near Pondicherry, yielded an intaglio carved with a figure identified as Augustus Caesar, along with beads and gems — strong evidence of Indo-Roman trade contact. Scholar Dubreuil called Arikamedu "a true Roman city." The Periplus refers to it as Poduke.

An Amphora Jar — a tall jar with a long, narrow neck and two handles — was used in ancient Rome to carry wine or oil; such jars are among the finds linking these sites to Roman trade.

Guilds & Merchant Bodies

Trade associations

  • Manigramam, Nanadesi(s), Valanjiyar — merchant guilds/trade unions of South India, encouraging trade and business; Nanadesis and Manigrama were among the biggest trading associations of the early medieval period, with significant foreign trade links.
  • Sreni — an association of traders, merchants and artisans.
  • Nagaram — a local association of merchants conducting commercial activity.

Trading Towns

Uraiyur, on the banks of the Cauvery in Tiruchirappalli, was a major Sangam-era town — also known as Koli/Varanam — and the chief centre of the cotton trade; the Periplus names it Argaru. Tagara was a significant trade centre on the route connecting Kalyana and Vengi. Korkai, Poompuhar (Kaveripattinam) and Muziris/Muchiri were all well-known ancient port cities — Poompuhar additionally served as the early capital of the Chola kings, and Muziris was among the oldest ports in the world.

Black pepper was prized in the most expensive Roman cookery; the Greeks knew of it from as early as the 4th century BC. Because of this Roman/Greek demand, Sanskrit called it "Yavanapriya" — "dear to the Yavanas (Greeks)."

08 Temples & Architecture

The Brihadeeswara Temple at Thanjavur — built during the reign of Rajaraja-I — is an outstanding example of Dravidian-style Chola architecture, considered the largest and tallest temple in India. It has idols of Dwarpals around the main shrine, and a gigantic Nandi carved from a single rock outside the sanctum — believed to be the tallest Nandi idol in India.

The mighty gateways of South Indian temples are called Gopurams. Dravidian temple style flourished especially under the Pandyas and Cholas. In the Pandya period, temples themselves were relatively small but ringed with many ramparts, with grand, spacious and richly ornamented Gopuram gateways; by the Chola period, the architectural centre of gravity shifted so that the Gopuram, rather than the temple proper, became the defining feature.

09 Minor Dynasties & Miscellaneous

Kadamba

Capital: Banavasi (also called Vaijayanti). Founded by Mayurasharman; the Kadamba state was eventually annexed by Pulakesin-II of the Chalukyas. (Note: Tanjore = Chola capital, Kanchi = Pallava, Badami = Chalukya, Banavasi = Kadamba — a recurring matching set.)

Kakatiya

Motupalli was an important seaport of the Kakatiya kingdom; Marco Polo visited the Kakatiya kingdom through this port and wrote about the prosperity and power of the Andhra region in his travelogue.

Language note

Halbi belongs to the (Eastern) Indo-Aryan language family — not Dravidian. It is spoken in the Bastar region of Chhattisgarh, is transitional between Odia and Marathi, and is written in both the Odia and Devanagari scripts.
