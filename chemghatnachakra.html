01Ancient Atomism → Dalton → Lavoisier

Kanad (कणाद)

  • Kanad was also called Kashyap, Uluka and Kanabhuk; an Indian natural scientist and philosopher who founded the Vaisheshika school of Indian philosophy.
  • Lived somewhere between the 6th century BC and 2nd century BC; little is known of his life; he developed the foundation of an atomistic approach to the universe.
  • Everything can be subdivided, but not forever — there must be smallest, indivisible, eternal entities called Parmanu.
  • Parmanu aggregate in different ways to give complex substances with unique identity — a process involving heat, the basis of all material existence.

Dalton's Atomic Theory (early 19th century)

  • John Dalton proposed all matter is made of atoms — indivisible and indestructible building blocks; his theory was based on experiments and observations on gases.
  • All atoms of an element are identical (size, mass, properties); atoms of different elements differ in size and mass.
  • Atoms are neither created nor destroyed — so in a chemical reaction no atom is created or destroyed; a reaction is only a rearrangement of atoms.
  • Atoms of different elements combine in a fixed ratio to form compounds; new products form by rearrangement of existing atoms.

What Dalton's theory explained

  • Law of conservation of mass, Law of constant composition, Law of multiple proportions — it gave a scientific basis for why elements combine in fixed proportions.
  • Dalton's theory does not account for radioactivity — atoms are divisible and nuclei can be unstable.

Drawbacks

  • An atom is NOT indivisible: it splits into electrons, protons and neutrons — yet the atom remains the tiniest particle that takes part in a chemical reaction.

Father of Modern Chemistry

  • Antoine Lavoisier — French nobleman and chemist; discovered the role of oxygen in combustion; named oxygen (1778) and hydrogen (1783); wrote the first extensive list of elements and reformed chemical nomenclature.

02Atomic Models — Thomson → Rutherford → Bohr

  • J.J. Thomson gave the first model of the atom (late 19th c.) — the plum-pudding model: a positive sphere with negative electrons embedded like plums in a pudding; a direct result of his discovery of the electron, which challenged the idea of an indivisible atom.
  • Rutherford's nuclear model superseded it — positively charged nucleus with electrons orbiting it.
  • Bohr's model then introduced quantized energy levels; electrons exist in spherical shells of various radii = energy levels.

03Electron · Proton · Neutron — Full Facts

Electron

  • Negatively charged sub-atomic particle; can be free or bound to a nucleus; charge −1.6022×10⁻¹⁹ C; symbol e⁻; mass ≈ 1/1837 of proton = 9.109×10⁻³¹ kg; atomic mass unit 0.0005486.
  • Discovered by J.J. Thomson in 1897; its negligible mass is why atomic mass is concentrated in the nucleus.
MCQ TrapThe source text credits electron to "James Chadwick (1932)" — this is an ERROR. Electron = Thomson 1897; Chadwick = neutron 1932.

Proton

  • Positively charged; symbol p / p⁺; charge +1.6022×10⁻¹⁹ C; mass 1.6726×10⁻²⁷ kg; atomic mass unit 1.00727.
  • Eugen Goldstein observed positive-ion beams incl. H⁺ as canal / anode rays in 1886; Rutherford identified the proton as the hydrogen nucleus (c. 1917; some sources give 1919) and proposed the name "proton" in 1920. New NCERT (April 2026): proton discovered and named by Rutherford.
MCQ TrapIf credit went to Goldstein (1886), the order would be Proton → Electron → Neutron → Pi meson — which no exam option offers, so the accepted order is Electron → Proton → Neutron → Pi meson.

Neutron

  • Neutral particle (hence the name); symbol n or n⁰; mass 1.0087 amu — slightly larger than a proton.
  • Chadwick (1932) bombarded beryllium with high-speed particles; electrically neutral particles emerged — neutrons.

Neutrality and amu

  • Number of electrons = number of protons → equal and opposite charge → atom is electrically neutral.
  • Atomic mass unit (amu) = exactly 1/12 of the mass of a Carbon-12 atom.
  • Electrons have negligible mass; atomic number = number of protons; isotopes are useful as nuclear fuel, in medicine, carbon dating (all correct statements).

04The Nucleus and What an Atom Contains

  • Nucleus = small, dense, positively charged region at the centre of the atom consisting of protons and neutrons; together called nucleons; held by the nuclear force.
  • Discovered by Rutherford in 1911 from the Geiger–Marsden gold-foil experiment (1909).
  • Dmitri Ivanenko proposed the proton–neutron model of the nucleus in 1932.
  • Nucleus of an atom = protons + neutrons; electrons revolve around it.

Parts of an atom — what is NOT

  • Photon is not a part of an atom — it is the energy packet (quantum) of light.
  • Deuteron is not a sub-atomic particle — it is a nucleus of deuterium = one proton + one neutron.
  • Sub-atomic = smaller than atom: the three main ones are proton, neutron, electron.

Elements vs compounds

  • A native element consists of a single type of atom; a compound has two or more types.
  • Diamond = pure carbon (crystal) → a fundamental element; sand = Si + O; marble = Ca + C + O; sugar = C + H + O (compounds).
  • Gases: only some are elements (H, He, N, O, F, Ne, Cl, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn — all non-metals); most gases are compounds. Types of element: metals, non-metals, metalloids — "gases" is NOT a type.
  • Helium nucleus = 2 protons + 2 neutrons; hydrogen (protium) has no neutron — the only common element without neutron.

05Atomic Number · Mass Number · Atomic Mass/Weight

Atomic Number (Z)

  • Symbol Z — from German Zahl / Atomzahl (number); = number of protons in the nucleus; fixed and constant for every element; characteristic of the element; decides its place in the periodic table; different elements have different Z.
  • Hydrogen Z = 1 → one proton.

Mass Number (A)

  • Symbol A — from German Atomgewicht (atomic weight); = protons + neutrons = total nucleons; approximates atomic mass in amu.
  • Mass number is not a fundamental character of an element — one element can have atoms of different mass numbers (isotopes).

Atomic Mass vs Atomic Weight

  • Atomic mass = mass of a single atom (≈ mass number for a single nuclide); atomic weight = weighted average of isotopic masses of all atoms of an element — not equal to a single A.

07Radioactivity & α · β · γ Radiation

  • Radioactivity = spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable nuclei during decay — a nuclear phenomenon, not a chemical reaction; such elements are radioactive substances.
  • Natural radioactivity — unstable nuclei occurring in nature; artificial radioactivity — man-made unstable nuclei.
  • α, β and γ emissions together are ionizing radiation.
  • Discovered by Henri Becquerel (1896) in uranium while working with phosphorescent materials.
  • Marie & Pierre Curie discovered Polonium (1898); Radium identified 1898, radium chloride isolated 1902 from pitchblende (uranium ore, ≈ U₃O₈ / mainly UO₂).
  • Measured by the Geiger–Müller (Geiger) counter — detects α, β, γ via ionization in a G-M tube.

08Advanced Particles — Positron · Neutrino · Quark · Higgs

  • Positron = antiparticle (antimatter) of the electron — positive charge, same mass, spin ½. Electron–Positron is the particle–antiparticle pair (not proton–neutron, photon–electron, neutron–neutrino).
  • Neutrino — zero charge, very tiny (possibly zero) mass, produced in radioactive decay; extremely abundant but very hard to detect as it barely interacts with matter.
  • Quark — fundamental particle; combine to form hadrons (proton, neutron most stable); fractional charge +2/3 e or −1/3 e. Neutron and proton are NOT fundamental; π-meson is not either.
  • Fundamental / elementary particle = no substructure; includes fundamental fermions (quarks, leptons, antiquarks, antileptons) and fundamental bosons.
  • Phonon — quantum of vibrational energy in a crystal; integral spin, like photon (quantum of light).

Higgs Boson ("God Particle")

  • Particle associated with the Higgs field, which gives mass to elementary particles like electrons and quarks; called the God Particle from Leon Lederman & Dick Teresi's 1993 book.
  • Hypothesised 1964; confirmed 14 March 2013; Nobel Prize in Physics 2013 to Peter Higgs and Francois Englert.
  • Importance of its discovery: it explains why elementary particles have mass — it does not enable teleportation or better fission fuel.

Charge-free particles (MCQ)

  • Neutron and neutrino carry no charge; α (+2), proton (+), electron (−) and positron (+) do.

09Electron Arrangement — Aufbau & Quantum Numbers

  • Aufbau principle governs the order of filling of orbitals: electrons fill the lowest available energy level first (1s before 2s) — atom is "built up" progressively.

  • A chemical reaction becomes faster at higher temperature because molecular motion is more rapid and molecules have higher kinetic energy; rate also depends on concentration, physical state and nature of reactants.

Exothermic vs endothermic

  • Exothermic (release heat): dilution of conc. H₂SO₄; quicklime (CaO) + water → Ca(OH)₂ (large heat); decomposition/hydrolysis question treats heat as the energy produced.
  • Endothermic (absorb heat): evaporation of water; sublimation of camphor.
  • When diluting sulfuric acid always add acid to water, never water to acid — water has high specific heat and absorbs the heat; the reverse can boil violently and splash acid.
  • Chemical change examples in the MCQs: reaction of iron with air (rusting) and of a metal with an acid. In a chemical change the starting materials change into an entirely different substance with a different composition.
  • Hydrolysis = ions of a salt dissolved in water combine with water to give an acidic or basic solution; the energy produced in the decomposition/hydrolysis question is heat.

Displacement reaction

  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt. Cu + 2AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag — copper is more reactive than silver. No reaction: NaCl + Cu; FeSO₄ + Ag; MgCl₂ + Al.

Catalysts

  • Catalytic promoter increases the activity of a catalyst; homogeneous catalyst = same phase as reactants; heterogeneous catalyst = different phase. All of these facilitate reactions — none slows a reaction down.

Corrosion

  • Corrosion = gradual decay of metals by air or chemicals: black coating on silver, green coating on copper, brown coating on iron (rust).
  • In the displacement reaction copper is more reactive than silver because it has a stronger tendency to lose electrons, so copper replaces silver from silver nitrate solution, forming copper nitrate and solid silver.

04Solution — Types, Saturation, Solubility

  • Solution = special homogeneous mixture of two or more substances: solute particles are not visible, do not scatter light, solution is stable, solute cannot be filtered out. A salt-in-water solution is homogeneous (same composition throughout); a mixture with different composition at different places is heterogeneous.
  • Solvent dissolves solutes — can be solid, liquid or gas; solvent particles push solute particles apart until uniformly distributed.

Degrees of saturation

  • Unsaturated: can still accept more solute. Saturated: holds the maximum solute that dissolves at a given temperature and pressure — extra solute stays undissolved. Concentrated: large amount of solute relative to solvent. Dilute: relatively small amount of solute.
  • Solubility = amount of solute needed to saturate a given amount of solvent at specified temperature/pressure; expressed as g per 100 g or 100 mL, or as molarity, molality, mole fraction.
  • Concentration of a solution = amount of solute per unit volume or mass of solution (or solvent).
  • Answer key: for an unsaturated sugar solution (100 g sugar in half litre water) the quantity that "will not change" is volume.

05Boiling/Freezing Point, Ideal Solution, Electrolytes

  • Boiling point = temperature at which vapour pressure equals the pressure above the liquid; impurities raise it. Salt in pure water raises the boiling point and lowers the freezing point.
  • Freezing mixture: ice + salt (NaCl, KNO₃) gives temperatures below 0 °C because salt lowers (not raises) the freezing point.
  • Methyl alcohol added to water lowers the boiling point (hydrogen bonding).

Ideal solution

  • Solute–solvent interactions are identical to those in the pure components; enthalpy of mixing ΔH_mix = 0 — no heat absorbed or released; obeys Raoult's law (partial vapour pressure ∝ mole fraction).

Strong electrolytes

  • Strong acids, strong bases and soluble ionic salts (NaCl, KCl) dissociate completely; on dilution the number of ions stays the same but ions per unit volume fall — so conductivity decreases on dilution.
  • Water dissolves ionic salts because of its dipolar nature / high dipole moment; it hydrates ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻) — that is why water dissolves more substances than any other liquid.
  • Ionic compounds are insoluble in alcohol (low dielectric constant) and are bad conductors in solid state.
  • Ideal solution = homogeneous mixture in which solute–solvent interactions are roughly equal to solute–solute and solvent–solvent interactions; the enthalpy of mixing (heat change on mixing) is zero, so the molecules mix with no energy change.
  • Conductivity of an electrolyte depends on the concentration and mobility of ions. On dilution the distance between ions grows and their mobility improves slightly, but the fall in ion concentration per unit volume outweighs this — net conductivity decreases.

07Osmosis, Reverse Osmosis, Semipermeable Membrane

  • Osmosis: solvent moves through a semipermeable membrane from lower solute concentration (pure solvent) to higher solute concentration until osmotic equilibrium (equal chemical potentials). Pure solvent → solution is osmosis, not reverse osmosis.
  • Semipermeable membrane: biological or synthetic polymeric membrane passing some molecules/ions and blocking others — generally passes solvent, blocks solute; selective pores/channels depend on pore size, solubility, charge.
  • Reverse osmosis: external pressure higher than osmotic pressure is applied on the concentrated side, so solvent flows from concentrated to dilute — used for desalination of sea water and potable water; removes bacteria, ions and molecules.
  • Plants absorb water from soil through root hairs by osmosis — cell sap is more concentrated so water potential is lower.
  • Salt on cut cucumber or onion draws water out by osmosis — surroundings become more concentrated than cell sap.
  • Desalination — sea water; reverse osmosis — potable water; denaturation — proteins; pasteurization is not used for tea.
  • Large-scale drinking water is made from saline water by desalination.
  • Root hair cells have a high concentration of minerals/sugars and little water, so water potential inside is lower and water moves in down the gradient; xylem then carries it through the transpiration stream to the leaves.
  • Sublimation: the vapour cools and turns directly back into solid — used to separate sublimable solids from non-sublimable ones.

08Separation Techniques, Pasteurization, Phosphatase Test

Separation

  • Distillation — purifies liquid impurities (heating and cooling); sublimation and crystallization — purify solid impurities.
  • Chromatography separates components of a mixture by differential partitioning between mobile and stationary phases; preparative (purification) or analytical.
  • Sublimation: solid directly to vapour — camphor, naphthalene, dry ice (CO₂); a sand + naphthalene mixture is separated by sublimation.

Pasteurization

  • Invented by Louis Pasteur, 1864 — heating beer and wine killed most spoilage bacteria. Heat then cool quickly; kills microbes (mainly bacteria) in milk, wine, fruit juices; related to sterilization of milk.
  • Milk: about 63 °C for 30 minutes or 72 °C for 15 seconds.

Phosphatase test

  • Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is present in raw milk and destroyed by heat — slightly above the temperature that kills pathogens — so the ALP test checks that pasteurization was correct; also used for milk tea and water pollution (acid phosphatase for Clostridium perfringens, indicator of faecal pollution).
  • ALP blood test screens liver and bone disease (ALP found in liver, bones, intestine, kidneys).
  • Pasteurization lowers microbial numbers and eliminates pathogens to prolong the quality of milk, wine, fruit juice; Pasteur's 1864 work showed heating beer and wine killed most spoilage bacteria. It is partial sterilization — the MPPSC answer key accepts "all of the above" for the phosphatase test (milk, tea, water).

01Prout · Dobereiner · Newlands · Meyer

Prout hypothesis (1815–16)

  • William Prout observed atomic weights of known elements seemed to be whole multiples of hydrogen's; hypothesised hydrogen is the only truly fundamental object (protyle) and other atoms are groupings of hydrogen atoms.
  • Later precise measurements showed many weights are not exact integers, so the simple form was rejected — though a modified "whole-number rule" for isotopes remains approximately valid.

Dobereiner's Law of Triads (1829)

  • When chemically analogous elements are arranged by increasing atomic weight, they form well-marked groups of three — triads — in which the middle element's atomic weight ≈ arithmetic mean of the other two.
Examples of triads

Newlands' Law of Octaves (1865)

  • Elements arranged by increasing atomic weight: those with similar physical/chemical properties recur after an interval of seven elements (every eighth element). Example rows: Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F then Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl (weights 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 16, 19; 23, 24, 27, 28, 31, 32, 35.5).
  • Newlands was among the first to recognise a periodic pattern in elemental properties, anticipating the periodic law.

Lothar Meyer's atomic volume curve (1870)

  • German chemist Lothar Meyer plotted atomic weight vs atomic volume (atomic/molar mass ÷ density of the solid element); elements with similar properties occupied similar positions on the curve.

02Mendeleev's Periodic Table

  • Dmitri Mendeleev, Russian chemist, arranged 63 elements by increasing atomic weight in 1869; his Law of Periodicity: physical and chemical properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic weight.
MCQ TrapThe source dates the law "1971" — the correct year is 1871 (table first published 1869).
  • 2019 was the International Year of the Periodic Table (UNESCO) — marking 150 years since Mendeleev's 1869 creation.

Salient features

  • Mainly by increasing atomic weight, but deliberately reversed some pairs (Co–Ni, Te–I) to keep chemically similar elements together.
  • Horizontal rows = periods — six (1 to 6); periods 4, 5, 6 had two series of elements. Vertical columns = groups — originally eight (I to VIII); groups I–VII divided into subgroups A and B; group VIII held three elements in each of three periods.
  • Included all 63 elements known then; left blank spaces for undiscovered elements — later filled by germanium, scandium, gallium.

Defects

  • Subgroup A and B of the same group can differ widely in properties; gradation down a subgroup is limited/imperfect with anomalies; heavier element placed before lighter (anomalous pairs); no place for isotopes. So a property more fundamental than atomic mass was needed.

03Modern (Long Form) Periodic Table

  • Moseley (1913) established atomic number as the fundamental basis of arrangement. Modern periodic law: properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers.
  • The most accepted form is the long form (18-column) table, consistent with electronic configuration; columns = groups/families, rows = periods.

Groups

  • 18 vertical columns, numbered 1–18; elements in a group generally have the same valence-shell electronic configuration and (for main-group elements) the same number of valence electrons; transition metals are an exception.

Periods

  • 7 horizontal rows (1–7); consecutive atomic numbers; a new shell starts filling in each period — the period number = number of shells.

02Carbon — Allotropes, Oxocarbons, Coal, Carbon Fibre

Carbon in general

  • Carbon forms the maximum number of compounds among all elements (more than H, N, O) — due to its valency it forms many allotropes.
  • Oxocarbon = inorganic compound made wholly of carbon and oxygen; simplest and commonest: CO and CO₂. An oxocarbon is not an allotrope of carbon.
  • Main allotropes: Graphite, Diamond, Buckminsterfullerene; others: graphene, Q-carbon, amorphous carbon. Coal, charcoal, lamp black are carbon-containing; sand does not contain carbon (finely divided rock/mineral particles).

Graphite

  • Name from Greek graphein (to write); old name plumbago (Latin: lead ore). Pencil "lead" is graphite mixed with a clay binder (leaves grey/black marks that erase easily) — a lead pencil contains no lead, lead oxide or lead sulphide.
  • Crystalline carbon, hexagonal layers; slaty-black, smooth; relative density 2.25; most stable form of carbon under standard conditions — thermodynamically the most stable form (not fullerene/diamond/coal).
  • Layers of carbon (graphene sheets) are only weakly bonded (weak van der Waals forces) → soft, slippery, great anisotropy; Mohs hardness 1–2; atoms layered in trigonal planar arrangement.
  • Under very high pressure and temperature it converts into diamond.
  • Good conductor of electricity; high melting point, modest thermal expansion, high specific heat → used in composites, structural materials, heat shields (incl. nuclear reactors).
  • Dry lubricant — used where wet lubricants such as oil cannot be used; used for heavy machines / industrial and automotive machinery (graphite grease) — graphite, not bauxite, phosphorus or silicon oil.

Diamond

  • Atoms in diamond cubic crystal, arranged tetrahedrally; relative density 3.5; hardest known natural substance — Mohs hardness 10; electrical insulator; thermal conductivity highest among natural materials.
  • High refractive index and high lustre → gemstone (value by cut, colour, clarity, carat weight).
  • Very hard → abrasive: particles embedded in saw blades, drill bits, grinding wheels for cutting, drilling, grinding, polishing; used to scratch/inscribe glass.
  • Carat = unit of mass = 200 mg (0.2 g), used for diamonds, gemstones and pearls.
  • Graphite and diamond are chemically the same (both carbon) and undergo the same chemical reactions; they differ in crystal structure, hardness and conductivity.

Lab-grown / engineered diamonds

  • Seed = a small diamond fragment or a carbon source, mainly graphite — not white sapphire (Al₂O₃), moissanite or cubic zirconia.
  • Two methods: HPHT (high pressure/high temperature) — used more for industrial synthetic diamonds; CVD (chemical vapour deposition) — increasingly popular for gem-quality jewellery diamonds. Catalysts such as chromium, iron, platinum may be used.

Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀)

  • Type of fullerene, formula C₆₀; cage-like fused-ring structure resembling a soccer ball — 20 hexagons + 12 pentagons, a carbon atom at every vertex, bond along each edge; single and double bonds; hollow sphere (fullerenes can also be ellipsoid, tube etc.). Carbon atoms are "fully condensed".
  • Named after American architect and inventor Richard Buckminster Fuller; discovered 1985 by Harold Kroto, Richard Smalley, James Heath, Robert Curl; 1996 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Curl, Kroto and Smalley (not Heath). Called the third allotrope of carbon.
  • Uses/research: drug delivery (conjugation/surface modification), antioxidant/free-radical scavenging, lubricant, catalyst; tube-shaped fullerene = nanotube.
  • Fullerene is not an organic compound containing fluorine nor a fluorine polymer nor a carbon isotope heavier than C-14.

Graphene

  • Allotrope of carbon — a single layer of atoms in a 2-D hexagonal (honeycomb) lattice; a nanostructure of carbon (not an alloy or isotope); basic structural element of graphite, charcoal, nanotubes and fullerenes; made only of carbon (not silicon).
  • Isolated/characterised in 2004 at the University of Manchester by Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov from graphite; Nobel Prize in Physics 2010.
  • Properties: excellent electrical conductor; one of the thinnest yet strongest materials tested — stronger than steel, brass, German silver; almost transparent yet impermeable to even the smallest gas molecule; resistant to heat and acids; 2-D nature makes controlling electricity easier.
  • Uses: conducting electrodes for touchscreens, LCDs, organic LEDs; faster computer chips; communication devices.

04Dry Ice, Water Gas, CO, Soda Water, HF & Glass

Dry ice (solid CO₂)

  • Dry ice = solid (frozen) carbon dioxide; looks like ice, sublimes straight from solid to gas (no liquid) at −78.5 °C (−109.3 °F) at atmospheric pressure. Not solid water, solid CO, frozen oxygen or hydrogen peroxide.
  • Uses: cooling agent; preserves frozen foods where mechanical cooling is unavailable; leaves no residue.

Carbon-based gases

  • Water gas = CO + H₂ (synthesis gas); methanol is formed from it: CO + 2H₂ → CH₃OH.
  • Gas that comes out on opening a soda-water bottle = CO₂ (water infused with CO₂ under pressure) — soda water is acidic (forms carbonic acid H₂CO₃).
  • Carbon monoxide: 10 valence electrons (4 from C, 6 from O); molecular-orbital configuration (σ2s)²(σ*2s)²(σ2pz)²(π2px)²(π2py)²; bond order = ½(Nb − Na) = ½(8 − 2) = 3.

Hydrofluoric acid & glass

  • HF etches/inscribes glass — glass forms silicon fluorides with HF, so HF is never stored in glass containers. Glass can be scratched by diamond and HF, not by aqua regia or conc. H₂SO₄. HF is one of the strongest inorganic acids; used in glass etching, metal cleaning, electronics.

05Hydrogen — Properties, Isotopes, Fuel

  • Hydrogen: symbol H, atomic number 1, configuration 1s¹; the lightest element, atomic weight ≈ 1.008; occupies a unique position in the periodic table — behaves like both alkali metals (Li, Na, K) and halogens (F, Cl, Br).
  • Element X with 4 outer electrons forms hydride XH₄. Hydrogen is 11.11% by weight of water (2/18).
  • Burning of hydrogen gives water; flammable over 4–75% by volume in air; cannot burn without air/oxygen. Hydrogen is the purest, least polluting fuel — coal, kerosene, diesel (fossil/carbon fuels) give CO₂ and other harmful gases.
  • Heat of combustion: 1 mole (2 g) H₂ gives 290 kJ; so 4 g gives 580 kJ.
  • Used in hydrogenation of vegetable oils → vanaspati ghee: H₂ passed through oil at 8–10 atm, 150–180 °C, with nickel (Ni) powder catalyst; unsaturated compounds become saturated (solid fat). Not Zn, Pt, Fe.

06Heavy Water · Hard/Soft Water · Water as Solvent

Heavy water (D₂O)

  • Heavy water = oxide of deuterium (oxygen + heavy hydrogen; deuterium replaces hydrogen); molecular weight ≈ 20 (ordinary water 18). Discovered by Harold C. Urey (1932); G.N. Lewis and R.T. MacDonald isolated the first nearly pure sample (1933).
  • Uses: neutron moderator (slows fast neutrons, low neutron absorption) and coolant in nuclear reactors (ordinary water also serves as both); to prepare deuterium and its compounds.
  • Effects on living things: high concentrations are toxic (reacts slower than ordinary water so body reactions become abnormal); inhibits plant growth and seed germination.

Heavy water plants in India

  • First plant at Nangal (Punjab), 1962 (later closed). Others: Baroda (Gujarat), Tuticorin (Tamil Nadu), Kota (Rajasthan), Hazira (Gujarat), Manuguru (Telangana), Thal (Maharashtra), Talcher (Odisha); production at Baroda and Talcher was later suspended.

Soft and hard water

  • Soft water: very low Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ (naturally soft or treated — often raising Na⁺); lathers easily with soap. Rainwater is naturally soft; underground it picks up chalk, limestone and Ca/Mg salts.
  • Hard water: dissolved bicarbonates, chlorides, sulfates of calcium and magnesium; no froth with soap (soap = sodium/potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids); permanent hardness = chlorides and sulphates of Ca and Mg (do not decompose on heating).
  • Hard water is unsuitable for drinking (salty), washing with soap (needs more soap, leaves scum), boilers (scale deposits reduce efficiency, block pipes, can burst boilers) and irrigation (salt hinders uptake; blocks xylem).

pH, purity and purification

  • pH of pure water = 7 (neutral at 25 °C). Water is a good solvent for ionic salts because it is dipolar with high dipole moment — hydrates ions.
  • Answer key: rainwater is the purest form of water among tap, sea, rain, distilled.
  • Desalination produces large quantities of drinking water; chlorine gas purifies drinking water (kills bacteria/microbes; swimming pools); reverse osmosis converts sea water to pure water — pressure above osmotic pressure on salty side pushes water the opposite way.
  • Alum (aluminium sulphate) clears muddy water by coagulation — destabilises suspended colloids, particles clump into heavy clusters that settle.
  • Desalination removes mineral components (salts) from saline water to make it fit for drinking, irrigation and industry — an effective way to produce potable water from seawater.

07Hydrogen Peroxide (H₂O₂)

  • Formula H₂O₂; discovered by Louis Jacques Thenard in 1818.
  • Pale blue liquid (colourless when dilute), slightly more viscous than water; soluble in water, alcohol, ether; a weak acid; relative density ≈ 1.45 (20 °C); boiling point 150.2 °C; freezing point −0.43 °C.
  • Uses: antiseptic / germicide to clean wounds; bleaching silk, wool, hair, ivory; decreases free SO₂ in juice and wine; fuel or oxidiser with other fuels.

08Sulfur & its Compounds

  • Sulfur (S): abundant, multivalent non-metal; bright yellow crystalline solid; called brimstone (burning stone); used in matches, insecticides, fungicides; main commercial use = making sulfuric acid for sulfate and phosphate fertilizers; a macronutrient for all living organisms.
  • Sulfur dioxide (SO₂): toxic colourless gas, pungent irritating odour; released by volcanoes and by burning sulfur-contaminated fossil fuels; used for sulfuric acid, preservative for dried fruits, winemaking, formerly a refrigerant; reducing agent (decolorises in water), can also act as oxidising agent; major air pollutant; cause of acid rain.
  • Sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆): colourless, odourless, non-flammable, extremely potent greenhouse gas and excellent electrical insulator; octahedral shape — six F around one S; hypervalent; used as gaseous dielectric in the electric industry, inert gas for casting magnesium, filling for insulated glazing windows.
  • Sulfur is a sedimentary-cycle element (with Fe, Ca, P) — not a gaseous cycle.
  • Dead-fish smell is due to amino compounds (trimethylamine) — not sulfur compounds.

09Nitrogen, N₂O, Ammonia, Nitrogen Cycle

  • Nitrogen gas (N₂) ≈ 78–79% of the atmosphere (O₂ ≈ 20.95%, Ar ≈ 0.93%, CO₂ ≈ 0.03% — the lowest); a permanent gas; essential in amino acids (proteins) and nucleic acids; the gaseous cycles are N, O, C and water; sedimentary cycles are Fe, Ca, P, S — so hydrogen (H₂) is the answer to "which gas is not in the gaseous cycle".
  • Aircraft tyres are filled with nitrogen — mostly inert, does not support combustion (prevents wheel fire on landing), diffuses less through rubber so pressure stays stable.
  • Insectivorous plants (Nepenthes, Dionaea, Drosera, Utricularia; ≈ 860 species as of Jan 2020; in India at Darjiling, Nainital, Kashmir) grow in waterlogged, acidic bog soils deficient in nitrogen and obtain it from insects.
  • Nitrogen cycle: fixation, ammonification, nitrification, denitrification — denitrifying bacteria turn nitrate/nitrite back into N₂; so discharge of nitrogen leaves the amounts in lithosphere and atmosphere unchanged.

Nitrous oxide (N₂O) — laughing gas

  • N₂O = laughing gas; colourless, non-flammable, slightly sweet odour and taste, soluble in water; powerful oxidiser at high temperature; used as anaesthetic and sedative in surgery/dentistry (relieves anxiety, pain). Inhalation causes disorientation, euphoria, numbness, dizziness, finally loss of consciousness. Not nitric oxide (NO), N₂O₃ or N₂O₄.

Ammonia (NH₃)

  • Compound of nitrogen and hydrogen; colourless, pungent; common nitrogenous waste; used mainly as fertilizer (salts, solutions, anhydrous); aqueous solution is a general cleaner.
  • Highly soluble in water — polar and hydrogen bonding with water; solution is basic (NH₄OH) and turns red litmus blue; forms white fumes with HCl (ammonium chloride). Ammonia is a Lewis base.

03pH Scale and Indicators

  • pH = logarithmic scale of acidity/basicity: approximates the negative log₁₀ of H⁺ molarity (log of the reciprocal); range 0–14, 7 neutral (temperature-dependent); below 7 acidic, above 7 basic. Each unit = 10-fold change in H⁺. "p" = potenz (power, German) per NCERT.
  • Pure water pH = 7 (neutral, at 25 °C); a neutral solution also has pH 7. A solution turning red litmus blue has pH above 7. Acidic solutions have pH less than 7 and basic more than 7 — not the reverse.
  • Worked: pH 3 → 6 means H⁺ decreases 1000 times; 0.1 N HCl has pH = −log(0.1) = 1.

Indicators

  • Chemical indicator gives a visible (usually colour) change showing presence/absence of an acid or alkali. Natural: turmeric, litmus, shoe flower (Gurhal).
  • Litmus = water-soluble mixture of dyes from lichens, usually on filter paper; red and blue litmus paper. Shoe flower: acid → magenta; base → green.

02Alkenes · Alkynes · Aromatic · Heterocyclic

Alkenes (olefins, –ene)

  • Contain a double bond; general formula CₙH₂ₙ; part of the olefins: Ethylene (C₂H₄), Propylene (C₃H₆), Butylene (C₄H₈).

Ethylene

  • Prepared by heating ethyl alcohol at 170 °C with conc. H₂SO₄ (acid acts as dehydrating agent). Colourless, sweet-smelling, sparingly soluble gas; high concentration causes unconsciousness; molar mass 28; density 1.25 g/L at STP; boiling point −103.7 °C.
  • Uses: natural plant hormone promoting fruit ripening (also used commercially); historically an anaesthetic; manufacture of polythene; preparation of mustard gas (sulfur mustard, HD — blisters skin and lungs). Ripening chemicals: ethephon / ethrel (releases ethylene in water at suitable pH; good substitute for calcium carbide) — not atrazine, isoproturon, malathion.

Alkynes (–yne)

  • Triple bond; formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; first member acetylene / ethyne (C₂H₂).

Acetylene

  • Made when calcium carbide (CaC₂) reacts with water — not silicon carbide. Colourless, faint ethereal odour, sparingly soluble, lighter than air; sublimes; boiling point −84 °C; impure acetylene smells like garlic. Free acetylene at high pressure is unstable and explosive — shipped dissolved in acetone; mixture with oxygen on ignition may explode.
  • Uses: oxyacetylene flame for welding and cutting (oxygen + acetylene — not hydrogen); raw material for plastics (vinyl chloride); carbide lamps used in mines. Carbide acts like ethylene in ripening but is inadvisable (harmful traces of arsenic/phosphorus) — illegal in India for ripening fruit.

Cyclic compounds

  • Homocyclic (carbocyclic): ring of carbon only. Aromatic: alternate C–C and C=C like benzene — benzene, naphthalene. Alicyclic: no alternate bonds, aliphatic-like — cyclohexane, cyclopentane.
  • Heterocyclic: ring has carbon plus N, O, S — furan, pyridine.
  • Benzene (C₆H₆): hexagonal ring with alternating double bonds; 12 sigma bonds (6 C–C + 6 C–H) and 3 pi bonds.

03Organic Basics, Origin of Life, Bhopal Tragedy

  • Carbon is the fundamental element of all organic and bio-compounds (linked covalently to H, O, N mostly).
  • Six elements primarily responsible for the origin of life: Hydrogen, Oxygen, Carbon, Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Sulfur (key option: C, H, N).
  • Sequence of appearance under simulated primitive Earth conditions: Methane → Hydrogen cyanide → Nitrile → Amino acid.
  • Bhopal gas tragedy: night of 2–3 December 1984; leak of 42 tonnes of methyl isocyanate (MIC) from a Union Carbide pesticide plant; over 500,000 people exposed.
  • Matches: biogas — cow dung; electrocardiography — heart disorder; DDT — insecticide; nicotine — tobacco.
  • Calcium carbide is used for artificial ripening because it gives acetylene; ripening is promoted by ethylene (not CO₂, SO₂ or N₂).
  • Origin of life: the matter in every living creature consists mainly of compounds of these six elements — proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, vitamins, hormones.
  • Methane: colourless, tasteless, odourless; found in marshy areas, in decomposition of organic matter and in gas released by coal; used to manufacture carbon black.

  • Ethyl alcohol (C₂H₅OH) = commonly "alcohol"; occurs as ethyl esters in fruits, plants, fragrant oils; main constituent of wines — spirits of wine; drinking alcohol.

06Polymers — Natural Polymers

  • Polymerization = reacting monomers to form polymer chains or 3-D networks; reactants are monomers, products polymers.

08Bulletproof Materials, Hydrogels, PFAS, BPA

  • Kevlar = para-aromatic polyamide (poly-paraphenylene terephthalamide) — many inter-chain bonds make it extremely strong; used for lightweight bulletproof helmets, vests, jackets (also stab-resistant). Not Terylene, nylon, Dacron, rayon, Bakelite or Teflon.
  • Polycarbonate (Lexan) + laminated glass layers → bullet-resistant windows; laminated glass also used in jackets.
  • Hydrogels: polymer networks in water, high water retention, flexible. Used in controlled drug delivery and as water-based/industrial lubricants; not used in mobile air-conditioning at present (passive cooling research only).
  • PFAS (perfluoro/polyfluoroalkyl substances): widespread in drinking water, food, food packaging; do not degrade easily (C–F bond among strongest); persistent exposure leads to bioaccumulation; used in aerospace, automotive, food contact materials, cosmetics, textiles, firefighting.
  • Bisphenol A (BPA): key component of polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins; chemical used in food packaging (can linings, water/baby bottles, shatterproof windows, eyewear); linked to brain disorder, infertility, heart disease, type 2 diabetes; main exposure through diet.
  • Hydrogels = a sparse network of polymer chains in an aqueous medium; they hold large amounts of water because of strong hydrophilicity and high porosity, and their flexibility comes from that water. Uses: controlled drug delivery (porous, biocompatible; tunable physical properties and controllable degradability protect labile drugs and control release); industrial lubricants (reduce friction and wear, control viscosity, improve thermal stability; non-flowable, so no lubrication failure; used for underwater and bio-lubrication). They have evaporative/radiative cooling properties for passive cooling and can act as desiccants to control humidity, but are not currently used in mobile air-conditioning.
  • PFAS molecules have chains of linked carbon and fluorine atoms; PFAS leak over time into soil, water and air, people are exposed by drinking contaminated water or food, products and air, and take in more than they excrete — bioaccumulation (found in blood of people and animals worldwide).
  • BPA has been used in food packaging since the 1960s; it leaches from epoxy coatings of canned foods, bottle tops and water supply pipes and from polycarbonate tableware and baby bottles.

Harmful effects of burning fuel

  • Incomplete combustion releases soot / black carbon (respiratory disease) and poisonous carbon monoxide — never sleep in a closed room with a coal fire. CO forms an irreversible complex with haemoglobin (carboxyhaemoglobin).
  • CO₂ → global warming/greenhouse effect; SO₂ from sulfur-containing fuels and oxides of nitrogen from petrol/diesel engines → acid rain (wet deposition harms crops, buildings, soil). Coal burning causes the maximum indoor chemical pollution.
  • CNG replaces petrol/diesel: lower emissions, burns without smoke — clean fuel. Wood: lots of smoke, deforestation. LPG beats wood: higher calorific value, no solid residue (wood leaves ash).
  • Coal ash contains arsenic, lead, mercury, cadmium, chromium, selenium; coal plants emit SO₂, SO₃, NOₓ, particulate matter; Indian coal ash is 25–45% vs 10–20% for imported coal (drift theory). In India some coastal coal plants (e.g. Mundra) use seawater, ≈ 48% of coal plants are in water-scarce districts (CSE 2021), and many (e.g. Adani Power) are privately owned.
  • Copper smelting releases SO₂ and particulate matter; copper slag leaches heavy metals (arsenic, lead, cadmium, nickel); it does not release lethal quantities of CO.
  • Furnace oil = residue of crude distillation; used in boilers of power plants, ships, industry; causes sulfur oxide emissions.

03Fossil Fuels — Coal, Coke, Coal Tar, Coal Gas, Petroleum

  • Fossil fuels = buried organic deposits (decayed plants and animals) turned by heat and pressure over millions of years into crude oil, coal, natural gas, heavy oils; non-renewable. Petroleum, coal and natural gas are fossil fuels (alcohol, ether, water gas are not); petroleum is the "natural fuel" — coke, tar come from destructive distillation, coal gas is made artificially. Natural gas is the cleanest fossil fuel.

Coal

  • Carbon-rich sedimentary rock (also H, S, O, N); brown or black; stratified deposits; burning gives CO₂, SO₂, NOₓ. Products: coke, coal tar, coal gas. Four types: peat → lignite → bituminous → anthracite.
  • Peat: excess moisture and impurities, precursor of coal. Lignite (brown coal): soft, brown, from compressed peat. Bituminous (soft/black coal): contains bitumen, better than lignite, poorer than anthracite; formed by coalification under heat and pressure. Anthracite (hard coal): hard, compact, submetallic lustre; highest carbon, fewest impurities, highest rank, low sulfur — also called black gold.
  • Coke: few impurities, high carbon; solid, hard, porous, grey; destructive distillation of low-ash, low-sulfur bituminous coal without oxygen; used in steel and metal extraction. In a blast furnace coke is the fuel (heat) and reducing agent (not oxidising agent, not silica remover).
  • Coal tar: thick dark smelly liquid, by-product of coke and coal gas; medical — psoriasis, seborrheic dermatitis; industrial — synthetic colours, explosives, plastics, photographic material, road materials.
  • Coal gas: made by strongly heating coal without air; hydrogen + methane + carbon monoxide; fuel for industries near coal plants.
  • Coal gasification → syngas → ethanol and ammonia → urea; nitroglycerine is made by nitrating glycerin, not by gasification.

Petroleum

  • Complex mixture of hydrocarbons (liquid = crude oil, also gas/solid); Latin petra (rock) + oleum (oil); conventional oil. Fractions by distillation: natural gas, gasoline, naphtha, kerosene, diesel, heating oil, bitumen, lubricating oils, paraffin wax, asphalt.
  • Asphalt / bitumen = by-product of petroleum refining — road binder, roofing felt, waterproofing. Petrochemicals (acetylene, benzene, ethane) → pesticides, plastics, synthetic fibres, paint, medicines. Kerosene contains no oxygen (glass, soil, cement do).

05Octane, Cetane, Engines, Catalytic Converter, New Fuels

  • Octane number measures petrol's antiknock quality (vs iso-octane); higher = withstands more compression before detonating. Tetraethyl lead (TEL), (CH₃CH₂)₄Pb was the antiknock agent (forms PbO) — no longer added to motor petrol, replaced by unleaded boosters. Fuel from plastic waste has high octane rating, no lead ("green fuel"). Cetane number measures diesel quality.
  • Diesel engine: air compressed until hot enough to ignite the fuel — no spark, no carburettor (uses fuel injection); high power and economy for heavy vehicles. Carburettor is used only in petrol engines.
  • Antifreeze for automobile engines = ethylene glycol, (CH₂OH)₂.
  • Catalytic converter: platinum, palladium, rhodium catalyse redox reaction turning hazardous exhaust gases to less harmful ones. Features making cars fuel-efficient: radial tyres, multipoint fuel injection, catalytic converter (key answer).
  • Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) feedstocks: corn grain, agricultural residues, wastewater sludge, wood mill waste (all four); also oil seeds, algae, municipal waste, manure.
  • Fuel cell electric vehicles powered by hydrogen emit only water vapour. Hydrogen can be stored by absorption in a hydride (palladium hydride) and released by exhaust heat.
  • Hydrocarbon Vision 2025 — storage of petroleum products and long-term energy security through indigenous production and equity oil abroad.
  • Cooking gas / biogas fuel gas = methane; methane is also the main component of natural gas.
  • Inside the hot cylinder the bonds between lead and ethyl groups in TEL break; the lead then forms lead oxide (PbO), which stops fractions of the fuel burning too quickly and thus prevents knocking.

06Food Preservation, Preservatives, Sugars

  • Food preservation = treating food to stop/slow spoilage and foodborne illness while keeping nutrition, texture, flavour.

Principles and methods

  • Control microbial growth: remove air/water, lower or raise temperature, raise salt, sugar or acid concentration (dry green leafy vegetables so microbes cannot thrive). Inactivate enzymes (blanching peas in boiling water before freezing).
  • Pasteurization: ≈ 63 °C for 30 min or 72 °C for 15 s. Freezing / cold storage / refrigeration: slows deterioration and biochemical reactions; fruits in a cold chamber last longer because respiration (and ethylene production) decreases. Salt in strong concentration inhibits bacteria.
  • Canning: food cooked under pressure at about 100–121 °C to destroy endospores. Drying: removes water, stops bacteria, yeasts, moulds. Lower oxygen around fruits/vegetables slows respiration and ageing — potato chips are packed in nitrogen (prevents oxidation, microbial growth, keeps chips intact). Wax emulsion on fruits/vegetables extends storage life (10–12 days) — carrier for sprout inhibitors, growth regulators, preservatives.

08Soap and Detergent

  • Soap = sodium/potassium salts of fatty acids (aliphatic monocarboxylic, e.g. palmitic C₁₅H₃₁COOH, stearic C₁₇H₃₅COOH; mixture C₁₂–C₁₈) made by saponification: fat/oil + alkali (heat) → soap + glycerol. Saturated fats → hard soap; unsaturated oils → soft soap. Raw materials: vegetable oils (peanut, mahua, castor, coconut), caustic soda (NaOH) or caustic potash (KOH); modern makers use fatty acids, giving water as by-product.
  • Fillers: sodium rosinate (foaming, laundry soap); glycerol (prevents shaving soaps drying); sodium silicate (durability, hardness); sulfur (anti-acne soap).
  • Soap action: emulsification — hydrocarbon end (C₁₇H₃₅) dissolves in grease, polar end (COO⁻Na⁺) in water; grease suspended as droplets. Soap solution is alkaline (hydrolysis) — harmful for delicate fabrics; best in soft water.
  • Detergent = water-soluble surfactant (or mixture) with long carbon chain (12–18 C; e.g. sodium lauryl sulphate, 12 C, anionic) — ionic, non-ionic, zwitterionic; removes oily dirt by trapping it in micelles. Clothes detergents are usually alkylbenzene sulfonates. It lowers (does not raise) surface tension of water, forms soluble Ca/Mg salts so no scum in hard water and more lather there; neutral solution → suits delicate fibres.
  • Washing powders contain sodium silicate and sodium sulphate — keep powder dry and maintain basicity (key answer).

09Fertilizers — Types, Nutrient Content, Urea

  • Fertilizer = natural or synthetic material applied to soil/plant tissue to supply nutrients. Manure: organic (animal faeces/urine with litter); gives humus, improves water holding, reduces erosion; less nutrient-rich than synthetics (1 tonne gives only ≈ 1.5–3 kg phosphorus) so needed in large amounts; N and K lost by leaching if rained on. Compost: managed aerobic decomposition of manure. Humus = organic colloids.
  • Green manure: crop ploughed in green or soon after flowering — leguminous (pea family) crops with nitrogen-fixing root-nodule bacteria: clover, vetch, sunhemp, dhaincha, pillipesara, clusterbean, Sesbania rostrata. Vermicompost: easily absorbed nutrients, retained by worm mucus. Biofertilizers: living bacteria, fungi, algae — fix nitrogen, solubilise phosphorus.
  • Continuous chemical fertilizer use destroys soil fertility (no humus, fewer microorganisms, less vitamins/nutrients in food); ammonium fertilizers acidify soil — ammonium sulphate leaves maximum acidity. Excess nitrogen fertilizer: soil acidity, nitrate leaching to groundwater, eutrophication — not proliferation of nitrogen-fixers.
  • Plants need ≥ 16 elements; C, H, O come from CO₂ and water, so hydrogen is the element not supplied by fertilizers. Roles: N — growth; P — roots (absorbed as phosphate); K — stomatal opening/closing, drought and disease resistance; B (micronutrient) — disease resistance, root nodules. Wheat needs nitrogen.

  • 2NaHCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O + 2CO₂
  • NaHCO₃ = the "soda"; H₂SO₄ = the "acid"
  • MCQ answer: dilute H₂SO₄ + NaHCO₃ solution (not carbonate, not aluminium sulphate)
MCQ TrapSource theory writes conc. H₂SO₄ for this extinguisher; the MCQ answer is dilute H₂SO₄.

Photocell Fire Alarm

  • Photocell = application of the photoelectric effect
  • A resistor whose resistance changes with incident light
  • Semiconductor photoconductivity: photons free electrons → resistance decreases
  • Device projects a focused light beam onto a mirror
  • Smoke entering the chamber refracts the light slightly → alarm sounds
  • Not water, not a solar cell

Fireproof Clothing

  • Aluminium sulphate is used to prepare fireproof clothes
  • Boric acid is also a flame retardant
  • HFCs have minor use in fire protection

Red Ink, Eosin & Chromate

Titanium

Haemoglobin, Lead Battery, Copper

Dry Cleaning & Phenyl

06Dry Cell, Car Battery, Ni-Cd, Magnetron

Dry Cell (Leclanché)

  • Zinc–carbon cell; no free liquid — works in many orientations (torch, calculator)
  • Electrolyte: ammonium chloride (sal ammoniac); sometimes zinc chloride (higher voltage)
  • Zinc pot holds manganese dioxide, ammonium chloride, carbon
  • Cannot be charged — reaction is not reversible (A true, R false)
  • A primary cell: converts chemical energy to electrical energy

Car Battery (Lead-Acid)

  • Electrolyte: dilute sulfuric acid — a strong acid
  • Not hydrochloric, nitric or acetic acid
  • Electrodes: lead and lead peroxide; high current in short time

Nickel–Cadmium Cell

  • Rechargeable — torches, electric shavers, mobiles
  • Nickel hydroxide cathode, cadmium anode
  • Potassium hydroxide electrolyte

Magnetron Tube

  • High-powered vacuum tube producing microwaves — used in microwave ovens
  • Works on motion of electrons under combined electric and magnetic fields
  • Electrons flow from cathode to anode
  • Not klystron or travelling-wave tube

07Dating Methods, Antimatter, BRIT, Fusion vs Fission

Carbon Dating

  • Determines age of objects with organic material — wood, bones, shells, firewood, fossils, trees
  • Based on decay of carbon-14 (₆C¹⁴) after death
  • Half-life ≈ 5,730 ± 40 years
  • Not used for rocks or the age of the Earth

Uranium / Radioactive Dating

  • Uranium–lead dating: oldest, most refined method for age of Earth and rocks
  • Clair Cameron Patterson — American geochemist; pioneered U-Pb dating; early accurate Earth age
  • Radioactive dating = principal source of absolute age of rocks and geological features

BRIT

  • Board of Radiation and Isotope Technology
  • Industrial unit of the Department of Atomic Energy
  • Radiation/isotope services: healthcare, agriculture, research, industry
  • HQ: Vashi, Navi Mumbai

Antimatter (Antihelium-4)

  • 2011: Brookhaven National Laboratory (with Indian scientists) made heaviest antimatter
  • Antihelium-4 = 2 antiprotons + 2 antineutrons
  • Antimatter partner of the alpha particle; found on its 100th discovery anniversary
  • Helps probe antimatter stars/galaxies; helps understand universe's evolution
  • Does NOT make mineral prospecting or oil exploration easier

Fusion vs Fission

  • Fusion: atoms combine into one more massive atom (mass slightly less than sum)
  • Fission: large nucleus breaks into smaller nuclei, releasing great energy
  • "Breaking the nucleus is fusion" = the incorrect statement
  • Dry ice = solid CO₂; Darwin — evolution; Graham Bell — telephone

Inventor Matches

  • Rutherford — atom bomb (key); Alfred Nobel — dynamite
  • Cartwright — powerloom; Graham Bell — telephone

Thermal Pollution

  • Caused by nuclear (and thermal) power plants
  • Coolant water returned warmer → less oxygen, changed ecosystem composition

08Ozone, CFC/HFC, Greenhouse Gases, Climate Facts

Ozone Layer & CFCs

  • Destroyed by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) — chlorine and bromine atoms hit ozone in stratosphere
  • One chlorine atom destroys over 100,000 ozone molecules
  • Ozone destroyed faster than naturally created
  • Ozone depletion → skin cancer; skin cancer ↔ ultraviolet light

Ozone as Germicide

  • O₃ readily gives up one oxygen atom → powerful oxidiser
  • Toxic to bacteria, mould/yeast spores, viruses, cyst-forming protozoans
  • Ozone is not radioactive — but that is not why it kills germs

Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)

  • Entirely man-made gases
  • Uses: refrigeration, air-conditioning, insulating foams (foam agents), aerosol propellants
  • Minor use: solvents, fire protection
  • Not used to make lubricants → used in 3 of the 4 options

  • Adds to stability/instability of air; NOT maximum at the poles

India's CO₂ Emissions

  • ≈ 2 t per capita (IEA 2023: 1.922 t) — far above 0.5 t
  • Second in Asia-Pacific (after China) from fuel combustion
  • Electricity and heat producers = largest source (~54%, 2023)

Wet-Bulb Temperature

  • Index combining air temperature and humidity — true heat stress
  • 35 °C = upper tolerable human limit (sweat cannot evaporate)
  • World Bank, Nov 2022: India may be among first to exceed it
  • Projected under IPCC RCP8.5 by end of 21st century: Ganges valley, NE India, east coast, Chota Nagpur Plateau
  • Implication: survival of humans/animals (body-heat loss) — not floods/cyclones/droughts

Geo-engineering

  • Inject sulphate aerosols into stratosphere — reflect sunlight; 1% reflected sunlight offsets major warming
  • Cirrus cloud thinning: cirrus ice-crystal clouds pass sunlight but trap outgoing infrared
  • Thinning them lets Earth's heat escape → cooling

Carbon Credit & DAC

  • Carbon credit = permit to emit 1 metric tonne (1,000 kg) CO₂ or equivalent; from Kyoto Protocol 1997
  • Direct Air Capture: removes CO₂ from ambient air using liquid solvents / solid sorbents
  • Stored underground (sequestration) or used in synthetic jet fuel (with hydrogen), plastics, concrete, food/beverages

Smog

  • Smog = smoke + fog; visible air pollution
  • Contains nitrogen oxides, sulfur oxides, ozone, smoke, particulates
  • Sources: coal combustion, vehicles, industry, forest/agricultural fires

Photochemical Smog

  • Brownish-gray haze: solar UV acts on air with hydrocarbons + nitrogen oxides (internal combustion engines)
  • Trapped by temperature inversion
  • Always contains ozone (principal); also nitric acid, aldehydes, PANs
  • NO₂ splits to free O atoms → combine with O₂ → O₃

09Air/Water Pollution, Monitoring, Treatment

Inorganic Gaseous Pollutants

  • Hydrogen cyanide and hydrogen sulphide; also CO₂, CO, SO₂, N₂O, NO₂
  • Phosgene and methane are not (key)

Hydrogen Sulphide (H₂S)

  • Colourless, rotten-egg odour
  • Chief source: decaying vegetation/animal matter, bacterial decomposition of wastes (sewage, landfills), geothermal fields
  • Industrial: oil/gas extraction, petrochemicals, coke ovens, kraft paper mills

Incomplete Combustion

  • Poor air supply → CO (poisonous) and soot (carbon) + water
  • Complete combustion → CO₂ + water
  • Metropolitan pollutants: CO, CO₂, NOₓ, SO₂, lead, ozone (key *)

Particulate Matter (PM)

  • Complex mixture of tiny particles and liquid droplets in air — affects heart and lungs
  • RSPM: part of total suspended particulates that is readily inhaled
  • Key: size less than 5 µm; explanation: diameter less than 2.5 µm (PM₂.₅)

NAMP Air Monitoring

  • National Air Quality Monitoring Programme, run by CPCB
  • Monitored everywhere: SO₂, NO₂, PM₁₀; PM₂.₅ at 611 stations in 277 cities
  • Nov 2024: 966 stations, 419 cities
  • Aims: status/trends, standard violations, non-attainment cities, preventive measures, natural cleansing

Magnetite Pollution

  • Magnetite = iron oxide (Fe²⁺ + Fe³⁺); toxic to brain; makes reactive oxygen species (Alzheimer's link)
  • Sources: vehicle brakes and engines, power plants, open fires
  • No clear evidence from microwave ovens or telephone lines

Mercury Pollution Sources

  • Pesticides
  • Dental amalgam fillings
  • Fluorescent lamps
  • Coal-based thermal power plants — all four

Green Muffler

  • 4–5 rows of dense trees around noisy places, roadsides, industries
  • Filter and block noise pollution

Eutrophication

  • Nutrient (nitrate, phosphate, silicate) enrichment of a water body
  • From runoff, detergents, fertilizers, sewage
  • Dense algal growth/blooms → depletes dissolved oxygen
  • Natural, but aggravated by humans

BOD & Effluents

  • BOD = oxygen used by microbes decomposing organic matter aerobically
  • Standard criterion for pollution assay in aquatic ecosystems
  • High BOD = poor water quality, low dissolved oxygen
  • Effluents (sewage, industrial outflow) change water's physico-chemical characteristics

Membrane Bioreactor (MBR)

  • Wastewater-treatment equipment: membrane separation + conventional treatment
  • Membranes keep biomass in the bioreactor
  • Replace bulky clarifiers of conventional activated sludge process

Activated Carbon

  • Porous adsorbent — large surface area, oxygen-containing functional groups
  • Removes heavy metals (Pb, Cd, Ni, Cu) by adsorption + surface complexation
  • Cheap; easily made from carbon-rich environmental wastes
  • Statements II and III both explain Statement I

Pyrolysis & Plasma Gasification

  • Thermal waste-to-energy technologies using little/no oxygen
  • Plasma gasification disposes of organic waste safely; minimises inorganic waste to slag
  • Gases formed: methane, CO, hydrogen, CO₂, water, carbonaceous matter — calorific value
  • Assessed by NITI Aayog (Action Agenda 2017–20)

Desalination in India

  • First plant: 2005, Kavaratti, Lakshadweep
  • LTTD (Low Temperature Thermal Desalination); 1 lakh litres/day
  • Developed by NIOT; Chennai also has plants

11Cloud Seeding, Soil, Cryogenics, Miscellaneous

Cloud Seeding

  • Dry ice or (more commonly) silver iodide aerosols spread in upper clouds → induce rain/snow
  • Sprayed from planes; or from ground generators / anti-aircraft guns
  • Potassium iodide auxiliary; sodium chloride, dry ice also used
  • Particles attract water droplets until drops are heavy enough to fall
  • Silver chloride = horn silver; silver bromide = photography (not rain)
  • Amount of rain depends on atmospheric humidity

Alkali Soil & Gypsum

  • Alkali soil: saline, excess sodium carbonate + sodium chloride
  • Hard crust with cubical salt crystals; smaller leaves, superficial roots, browned stems
  • Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) reclaims it — no direct pH effect; calcium replaces sodium

Edaphic Factors

  • Edaphic = related to soil (drainage, texture, pH)
  • Not climatic, biotic or topography

Cryogenics

  • Study/use of materials at very low temperature
  • Liquid nitrogen, hydrogen, oxygen used
  • Liquid hydrogen = rocket fuel; liquid oxygen (LOX) = oxidiser

Urinal Smell

  • Pungent smell = ammonia
  • Urine: 91–96% water; urea ≈ 2.6%; phosphate 0.6%; sodium 0.4%; ammonia 0.05%; creatinine, ions
