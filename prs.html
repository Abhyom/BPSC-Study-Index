01 Historical Roots & Nomenclature

  • In 1830, Sir Charles Metcalfe, then acting Governor General of India, described the village community as "a little republic," nearly self-sufficient and almost independent of foreign relations — the earliest articulation of India's self-reliant rural system.
  • Lord Ripon, appointed Viceroy of India in 1880, is regarded as the "Father of Local Self-Government in India"; he laid the foundation of representative institutions and municipal bodies in the country.
  • Mahatma Gandhi suggested the nomenclature "Panchayati Raj" for rural local governments, envisioning each village as a self-sufficient "little republic" enjoying maximum freedom in local affairs.
  • The Panchayati Raj System is associated with the theme of "Grass-Root Democracy" — decentralization within a democratic setup.
  • Balwant Rai Mehta described the system as one of "Democratic Decentralization."
  • Local self-government is best explained as an exercise in democratic decentralisation — not federalism, administrative delegation, or direct democracy.
  • The principal aim of Panchayati Raj is the decentralization of power to villagers, enabling people's participation in developmental administration — not primarily political accountability or financial mobilisation.
  • Attributes of Local Government: statutory status, participation of local community, power to raise finances by taxation — central control is NOT an attribute of local government.
  • Characteristics of decentralization: autonomy, people's participation, empowering local communities — instilling non-confidence among local communities is NOT a characteristic.
  • Full functioning of Panchayati Raj requires the cooperation of the local public above all.
  • Panchayati Raj in India represents decentralisation of power, participation of people, and community development — all together.

02 Constitutional Basis & the 73rd Amendment

  • Article 40 (Directive Principles of State Policy) directs the State to organise Village Panchayats and endow them with powers/authority to function as units of self-governance.
  • Panchayati Raj as a unit of self-government is formulated under the 73rd Amendment (1992), which gave it Constitutional status.
  • The third tier was added to the Indian federal system in 1993 (73rd Amendment in force 24 April 1993, though passed by Parliament in 1992).
  • The 64th Constitutional Amendment Bill (introduced July 1989) was the first attempt after Independence to confer constitutional status on Panchayati Raj Institutions — the legislative ancestor of the 73rd Amendment.
  • The 73rd Amendment Act, 1992 inserted Part IX ("The Panchayats") and the 11th Schedule into the Constitution, covering Articles 243 to 243-O.
  • The 73rd and 74th Amendments were passed in 1992 during the prime-ministership of P.V. Narasimha Rao.
  • The 73rd Amendment gave practical shape to Article 40 of the Constitution.
  • Madhya Pradesh was the first State to implement the 73rd Amendment in true spirit.
  • National Panchayati Raj Day is celebrated every year on 24 April (first celebrated in 2010).
  • The main national event for National Panchayati Raj Day 2025 (32nd anniversary) was held at Lohna Uttar Gram Panchayat, Madhubani district, Bihar.
  • New provisions of the 73rd Amendment (1992): collective responsibilities in agriculture, rural development, primary education, social forestry; mandatory elections for vacant posts; statutory one-third representation for women — regular remuneration to panchayat members was NOT one of these provisions.
  • The 73rd Amendment made Panchayat elections mandatory, placing them on a footing as serious as Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabha elections (via Article 243K / State Election Commission).
  • Despite the 73rd/74th Amendments, local government continues to be a State subject under the Seventh Schedule — separate constitutional provisions for local governance nonetheless exist under Article 40, Part IX, and Part IX-A.

⚠ Trap — "copied from Australia"

One source question states that the three-tier governance system introduced by the 73rd/74th Amendments was "copied from the Constitution of Australia" and marks this as correct. This is inconsistent with the mainstream account (the system traces to the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee, 1957, and indigenous experiments in Rajasthan/Andhra Pradesh) — flagged here as a contradiction in the source rather than resolved silently.

03 Three-Tier Structure

  • The Panchayati Raj set-up is a three-tier structure: village level (Gram Panchayat), intermediate/block level (Panchayat Samiti), and district level (Zila Parishad) — governed by Article 243B.
  • A Panchayat at the intermediate level may NOT be constituted in a State having a population not exceeding 20 lakh — Article 243B(2). This makes the intermediate tier optional, not mandatory, for such States.
  • The 73rd Amendment covers Gram Panchayat, Kshetra/Block Panchayat (Panchayat Samiti), and Zila Parishad — Nagar Panchayat is NOT covered by the 73rd Amendment (it falls under the 74th Amendment/Municipalities).
  • Panchayat Samiti (Block level): members are directly elected by the public; it functions as an administrative authority, linking Gram Panchayats and Zila Parishad, handling both co-ordination and execution of plans.
  • Zila Parishad (District level) functions as a supervisory and co-ordinating authority; it is NOT composed entirely of directly elected members — it also includes ex-officio heads of district departments, chairpersons of all Panchayat Samitis in the district, MPs, MLAs, MLCs from the district, and representatives of SC/ST/women/co-operative societies.
  • Territory of a Kshetra Panchayat (intermediate level) is determined by the State Government (Article 243C(2): each Panchayat area divided into territorial constituencies so the population-to-seat ratio is uniform).
  • Main characteristics of the Panchayati Raj System: three tiers of government; aims at social and economic justice to villages; some MPs sit as representatives in the Zila Parishad.
  • Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) recommended the original three-tier structure: Gram Panchayat (village), Panchayat Samiti (block), Zila Parishad (district) — "Nyaya Panchayat" is NOT one of these three tiers (it was proposed later, by the Ashok Mehta Committee, as a separate judicial-type entity).

04 Gram Sabha

  • "Gram Sabha" is defined under Article 243(b) as a body of persons registered in the electoral rolls relating to a village within a Panchayat area at village level — NOT simply "all persons living in the village" (only registered electors qualify).
  • The Gram Sabha exercises such powers/functions at village level as the State Legislature may, by law, provide (Article 243A) — its powers are NOT determined by the Central Government.
  • The Gram Sabha functions at village level analogous to how the State Legislature functions at the State level; it is a permanent body and the largest/foundational institution of the Panchayati Raj system.
  • All other PRI bodies (Gram Panchayat, Panchayat Samiti, Zila Parishad) are constituted of elected representatives, whereas the Gram Sabha itself is the sabha of the electorate directly.
  • A decision taken by the Gram Sabha cannot be annulled by any other body — only the Gram Sabha itself can annul its own decision.
  • A Gram Panchayat can cover more than one village; Gram Sabha meetings are held in each village (by rotation), while Gram Panchayat meetings are held at the Gram Panchayat headquarters.
  • Under the Chhattisgarh Panchayati Raj Act, 1993 (amended 2004): Gram Sabha must meet once every three months; quorum = 1/10th of total members, of whom 1/3rd must be women; the Sarpanch and Panchs are responsible for quorum; resolutions on the annual working plan, beneficiary selection, budget, audit report, and administrative report can only pass with quorum present (not in an adjourned meeting lacking quorum); if quorum is absent in five subsequent meetings, removal proceedings against the Sarpanch begin.
  • In Madhya Pradesh, if both Sarpanch and Up-Sarpanch are absent, the Gram Sabha meeting is presided over by a Panch elected by members present (M.P. Panchayati Raj Avam Gram Swaraj Adhiniyam, 1993).
  • Under PESA rules, in Scheduled Areas the Gram Sabha is presided over by a Scheduled Tribe member selected by consensus for one year; without consensus, the oldest ST woman present becomes Chairperson.

05 Composition, Qualification & Reservation

Composition

  • The Legislature of a State — not the Governor, Parliament, or President — is authorised to make provisions regarding the composition of Panchayats, under Article 243-C(1).
  • Each State must constitute Panchayats at the village, intermediate, and district levels (Article 243B(1)).

Reservation of Seats

  • Article 243D provides for reservation of seats for SCs and STs in Panchayats.
  • Article 243D(2): not less than one-third of SC/ST-reserved seats shall be reserved for SC/ST women — NOT 50 percent and NOT one-fourth.
  • Article 243D(3): not less than one-third of total seats filled by direct election shall be reserved for women (inclusive of SC/ST women's seats); such seats may be allotted by rotation to different constituencies.
  • Article 243D guarantees the one-third representation of women in Panchayats.
  • Article 334 (Lok Sabha/Assembly reservation) has no bearing on Panchayat reservation — women's reservation in Panchayats does NOT cease per the period specified in Article 334.
  • Reservation of seats for women, at the time of these provisions, was specific to Panchayati Raj Institutions — NOT the result of any prior public demand by rural women (a top-down constitutional provision).
  • The Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023 ("Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam") separately provides one-third reservation for women in the Lok Sabha, State Assemblies, and Delhi Assembly (including SC/ST-reserved seats), effective after the first delimitation following the first census post-commencement.
  • The Uttar Pradesh Panchayat Laws (Amendment) Act, 1994 (in force 22 April 1994) provides one-third reservation for women in UP's PRIs.
  • SC reservation in Panchayat elections does NOT apply to Arunachal Pradesh (no SC population per Census 2011).
  • By c.2010–2011, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, and Himachal Pradesh had 50 percent women's reservation in Panchayats; this list later expanded to include Andhra Pradesh, Kerala, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, Tripura, Uttarakhand, Assam, Gujarat, Karnataka, Sikkim, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, West Bengal, and Punjab.

⚠ Trap — common distractors

"30% seats reserved for women" and "disqualification for having more than two children" are frequently offered as distractors — neither was actually provided for by the 73rd Amendment. The correct figure is one-third (not 30%), and no two-child disqualification clause exists.

Qualification / Minimum Age

  • Article 243-F(1): minimum age to contest/be a member of a Panchayat (all three levels) is 21 years — NOT 18, 25, or 30 years.

06 Duration, Dissolution & Elections

  • Article 243E: every Panchayat, unless sooner dissolved, continues for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting — not from election notification, result declaration, or oath of office.
  • If dissolved before completing its term, elections must be completed within six months of dissolution — NOT one month, three months, or one year (exception: if remaining term is under 6 months, re-election isn't necessary).
  • A Panchayat reconstituted after premature dissolution continues only for the remainder of the original period — it does NOT receive a fresh full five-year term.
  • Panchayat elections are, by design, held every five years (subject to the six-month re-election rule on dissolution).
  • The State Government decides to conduct Panchayat elections (a State List subject); the State Election Commission (Article 243K) holds constitutional control over the actual conduct of elections and preparation of electoral rolls.
  • Under the Chhattisgarh Panchayat Nirvachan Niyam, 1995 (Sec 46): if a candidate dies before polling and more than one candidate remains, polling is NOT postponed; if only one candidate remains, polling IS postponed.
  • A Panchayat election has one District Election Officer and one or more Returning Officers; the SEC appoints the Returning Officer (or the District Election Officer may, with SEC consent); a Naib Tehsildar may serve as Returning Officer for the Janpad or Gram Panchayat.
  • Panchayati Raj is a State List subject (Seventh Schedule, 5th entry).

Rule — worked dissolution example

If a Gram Panchayat elected 20 Jan 2014 held its first meeting on 10 Feb 2014, and was dissolved on 20 Feb 2016, the new Panchayat (elected 15 May 2016, first meeting 25 May 2016) inherits only the remainder of the ORIGINAL five-year term — it runs until 10 February 2019, five years from the original first-meeting date, not its own.

07 Powers, Functions & the 11th Schedule

  • Article 243G empowers the State Legislature to devolve powers to Panchayats for: (a) preparation of plans for economic development and social justice, and (b) implementation of schemes for economic development and social justice, including 11th Schedule matters.
  • The 11th Schedule (added by the 73rd Amendment) lists 29 subjects/functions devolved to Panchayats — including Libraries, Fuel and Fodder, and Technical Training — "Rural Sports" is NOT part of it.
  • Functions assigned by the 73rd Amendment are listed in the Eleventh Schedule — not the 10th, 12th, or 13th Schedule (12th is for Municipalities, under the 74th Amendment).
  • The 73rd Amendment provided for: regular elections (Art. 243K), reservation for women at all levels (Art. 243D), mandatory fund transfer per SFC recommendation (Art. 243I), and mandatory transfer of powers on 11th Schedule subjects — all four together.
  • Functions that ARE the concern of Local Self-Government: Public Health, Sanitation, Public Utility Services, Roads and Bridges, Developmental Schemes — "Maintenance of Public Order" is NOT one (belongs to the police/state administration).
  • Judicial Review is NOT a power of Panchayati Raj Institutions — it belongs to the Judiciary.
  • PRI powers include implementation of land reforms and poverty-alleviation programmes.
  • Under the Chhattisgarh Panchayati Raj Act, 1993, Zila Panchayats hold powers to: plan minor water bodies; control institutions in all social sectors; control tribal sub-plans; and exercise other functions conferred by the State Government.
  • On Nyaya Panchayats: under the Bihar Panchayati Raj Act, 1947 & 1993, village courts could impose imprisonment; the Patna High Court (1996) declared this illegal; under the current Bihar Panchayati Raj Act, 2006, no village court may sentence any imprisonment.

08 Finance — State Finance Commission & Accounts

  • Article 243-I: the Governor must constitute a State Finance Commission within one year of the 73rd Amendment's commencement, and thereafter every five years, to review Panchayats' finances and recommend distribution principles for tax/duty/toll/fee proceeds.
  • The State Finance Commission is responsible for the distribution of revenue between the State Government and Local Governments — not the Chief Minister or Governor personally.
  • Article 243J allows the State Legislature — not Parliament, District Collector, or the Finance Commission — to make provisions on maintenance and audit of Panchayat accounts.
  • The Union Finance Commission (Article 280) also recommends measures to augment the State's Consolidated Fund to supplement Panchayat/Municipality resources, based on State Finance Commission recommendations — it is incorrect to say the Union Finance Commission has NO role at all.
  • State Finance Commission (243I), State Election Commission (243K), and District Panchayat (243B) are all Constitutional authorities — a State Electoral Officer/Chief Electoral Officer is NOT.
  • The Madhya Pradesh SEC was constituted 1 February 1994 under Articles 243K and 243ZA.
  • The Rajasthan SEC was constituted July 1994 under Article 243K — a single-member body whose Secretary also serves as Chief Electoral Officer.

09 State Election Commission

  • Article 243K vests the State Election Commission (SEC) with superintendence, direction, and control over electoral rolls and the conduct of all Panchayat elections.
  • Article 243ZA gives the SEC identical powers over Municipal elections.
  • The State Election Commissioner is appointed by the Governor — not by the President of India or the Chief Election Commissioner of India.
  • The SEC is a Constitutional authority in its own right; the Election Commission of India has NO role in overseeing local-body elections — SEC and ECI are entirely separate bodies.
  • SEC functions: prepares electoral rolls for Panchayat/Municipality elections; conducts those elections; may conduct elections to other bodies on the Governor's direction — it does NOT act "as assigned by the Election Commission of India" (functionally independent of the ECI).
  • All Panchayat elections are conducted by the State Election Commission — not the ECI, the State Legislature, or any "Panchayati Raj Commission."

11 PESA Act, 1996

  • The Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act was passed by Parliament in 1996, on the recommendations of the Bhuria Committee.
  • PESA applies to ten States with Fifth Schedule Areas: Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, Telangana.
  • Objectives: self-governance, recognising traditional rights, and freeing tribal peoples from exploitation — creating autonomous regions is NOT an objective.
  • In PESA areas, the Gram Sabha can stop land transfer in Scheduled Areas and holds ownership of minor forest produce — its recommendation is NOT mandatorily required for every mineral's mining lease (consent applies specifically to minor minerals).
  • PESA is NOT enforceable outside the ten notified Fifth Schedule States (e.g., not in Assam–Meghalaya–Tamil Nadu).

12 States Without Panchayati Raj / Special Areas

  • Per Article 243M(2), Panchayat provisions do NOT apply to the hill areas of Nagaland, Meghalaya, and Mizoram, or to Manipur, where District Councils exist instead.
  • At present there is no Panchayati Raj Institution in Mizoram, Nagaland, and Meghalaya.

13 State-Specific Provisions

Chhattisgarh

  • Chhattisgarh Panchayati Raj Act, 1993: each Gram Panchayat divided into 10–20 wards, each electing one Panch (all elected, none nominated); Gram Panchayat = elected Panchs + elected Sarpanch.
  • Section 127: the Governor may, by notification, change Block headquarters or alter Block/Zila Panchayat limits.
  • Section 39(3): if a Sarpanch/President is suspended, members elect a temporary officiating Sarpanch/President.
  • Chhattisgarh Municipalities Act, 1961, Sec 79(2): disputes between Panchayat and Cantonment Board decided by State Government, subject to Central Government concurrence.
  • Same Act, Sec 34: Municipal Council President candidate must be ≥25 years; Councillor candidate must be ≥21 years; cannot contest two wards simultaneously; if elected to both offices, must resign one within 7 days.
  • Same Act, Sec 47: recalling a Municipal President needs a 3/4-elected-Councillor signed proposal, then a secret-ballot recall vote needing a majority of over half of total voters casting a vote; cannot be initiated within 2 years of election; may be initiated only once per full term.
  • A councillor acting as a legal practitioner against the Municipal Council is removed from office by the Collector.
  • A woman Sarpanch cannot legally nominate her husband to attend Gram/Janpad Panchayat meetings — though an ex-officio MP/MLA/MLC member CAN legally nominate a representative.

Madhya Pradesh

  • MP Zila Panchayat Standing Committees: General Administration, Agriculture, and Education (optionally also Communication/Works and Co-operation/Industries).
  • MP State Election Commission constituted 1 February 1994.

Uttar Pradesh

  • UP Kshetra Panchayats and Zila Panchayat Act, 1961, Sec 19: Zila Panchayat Chairman is elected by its own elected members from amongst themselves (not directly by voters); Sec 19-A reserves the office for SC/ST/Backward Classes.
  • The Chairman of a UP Municipality is elected by all voters on the electoral list of the municipal area, including ward electors — NOT by all adults, and NOT solely by elected municipal members.
  • UP's District Plan includes Nagar Panchayat, Gram Panchayat, and Kshetra Panchayat — all three.

Rajasthan

  • Rajasthan Panchayati Raj Act, 1994, Sec 89, amended to replace "Gram Vikas Adhikari" with "Gram Sevak."
  • Rajasthan's SEC constituted July 1994 — a single-member body whose Secretary also serves as Chief Electoral Officer.
  • Rajasthan Municipal Act, 2009: State Government may nominate max 8 persons to a Municipal Council (max 6 to a Board, max 12 to a Corporation).
  • Mayor election security deposit: Rs 30,000 (general), Rs 15,000 (women/SC/ST/OBC).
  • Rajasthan's District Planning Committee: 20 elected + 5 ex-officio members (Sec 121 of the 1994 Act); chairperson is the Zila Pramukh (Zila Parishad head) — this figure varies by state (34 in Bihar).
  • Rajasthan was the first State to establish the Panchayati Raj system, on 2 October 1959 in Nagaur district.

14 74th Amendment — Municipalities

  • The 74th Amendment Act, 1992 gave constitutional status to urban local self-government (Municipalities) — NOT to Panchayats, which the 73rd Amendment addressed instead.
  • It inserted Part IX-A ("The Municipalities"), comprising Articles 243P–243ZG, and added the 12th Schedule listing 18 functional items.
  • It came into force on 1 June 1993.
  • Article 243Q envisages THREE types of Municipalities — Nagar Panchayat (transitional area), Municipal Council (smaller urban area), and Municipal Corporation (larger urban area) — NOT two types.
  • The 74th Amendment is applicable in ALL States — not just specified States.
  • Municipalities have a fixed five-year term from the first meeting (Article 243U) — counted from the first meeting, NOT the election-result date; if dissolved early, a reconstituted Corporation continues only for the remainder of the original period.
  • Article 243T(3): at least one-third of directly-elected seats reserved for women in every Municipality (inclusive of SC/ST women); many States raise this up to 50%.
  • Article 243T(1) provides reservation of seats for Scheduled Tribes in Municipalities.
  • Minimum age to be a Municipality member is 21 years (Article 243V) — NOT 25 years.
  • Territorial area of a Municipality is notified by the Governor (Article 243P(d)).
  • Under Article 243S, Ward Committees are constituted for wards with population 3 lakh or more; ward councillors are elected; two persons are nominated by the Mayor from ward residents; term is five years.
  • Municipal Corporations are formed for big cities with a definite area and population, because urban administration in big cities is very complex (Article 243Q).
  • Census urban-area criteria: Municipality/Corporation/Cantonment/Notified Town Area Committee, OR all of: min. population 5,000, at least 75% of male workforce in non-agricultural pursuits (NOT 60%), and density ≥400/sq km.
  • Mayor's tenure is generally 5 years (Article 243U) — but Delhi and Bengaluru: 1 year; Mumbai: 2.5 years.
  • Example matches: Ghaziabad has a Municipal Corporation (Nagar Nigam), not a Nagarpalika Parishad; Lucknow has a Municipal Corporation plus a Cantonment Board; Kakori is a Nagar Panchayat.

15 District & Metropolitan Planning Committees

  • The District Planning Committee (DPC) is constituted under Article 243ZD, added by the 74th Amendment (though it consolidates plans of BOTH Panchayats and Municipalities in the district).
  • The DPC consolidates plans from Panchayats and Municipalities and prepares a draft development plan for the district as a whole.
  • DPC membership varies by State: 20 elected + 5 ex-officio in Rajasthan; 34 in Bihar; 20 in Chhattisgarh.
  • In Rajasthan, the DPC chairperson is the Zila Pramukh (Sec 121, Rajasthan Panchayati Raj Act, 1994).
  • The Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC) is constituted under Article 243ZE for every metropolitan area, and prepares a draft development plan for it — it does NOT have sole responsibility for implementing government-sponsored schemes (limited to sending draft plans to the State Government).
  • 73rd and 74th Amendments together created: State Election Commission (243K), State Finance Commission (243I), SEC for Municipalities (243ZA), State Finance Commission for Municipalities (243Y), and District Planning Committee (243ZD) — a "State Property Tax Valuation Board" was NOT created.
  • The 73rd Amendment specifically provides for conducting Panchayat elections via SECs and establishing State Finance Commissions — constituting District Planning Committees is a 74th Amendment provision (Art. 243ZD), NOT a 73rd Amendment provision.

16 Rural Employment Schemes

  • The National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (NREGA) was passed in August 2005, in force from 2 February 2006; renamed MNREGA on 2 October 2009.
  • First launched/piloted in Anantapur district, Andhra Pradesh.
  • MNREGA guarantees at least 100 days of employment per year to adult members of ANY household — not restricted to SC/ST, BPL, or backward-community households only.
  • The Gram Panchayat (after considering the Gram Sabha's recommendation) is responsible for planning, execution, and implementation of MNREGA works — not the Gram Sabha itself, the State Government, or the DRDA directly.
  • The Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin), "VB-GRAMG Act, 2025", received Presidential assent on 20 December 2025, and replaces MGNREGA under a new statutory framework aligned with Viksit Bharat 2047; it raises the employment guarantee to 125 days per rural household annually and strengthens income security (effective date to be notified).
