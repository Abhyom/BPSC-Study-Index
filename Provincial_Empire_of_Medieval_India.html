Delhi Sultanate’s blow to the four kingdoms

  • Under Alauddin Khalji, these States were defeated by General Malik Kafur in 1304 and 1311.
  • Muhammad Tughluq (1325–51) tried to shift the capital to Devagiri (Daulatabad) — the experiment failed; when he returned to Delhi, the southern subordinates sought independence.
  • This power vacuum produced two new powers: Vijayanagara (1336) and Bahmani (1345/1347).

Kakatiya highlight — Rudrama Devi

  • Kakatiya king Ganapati Deva (Ganapti) had no son — after his death his daughter Rudrama Devi became ruler (Uttarakhand PCS 2021).
  • The sovereign Kakatiya empire emerged under Rudradeva / Pratap Rudradev I; then came Mahadeva and Ganapati Deva; Rudrama was the fourth independent ruler of Warangal’s Kakatiya line.
  • She defeated the Pandyas of south Tamil Nadu, the Eastern Ganga rulers of Orissa and the Seuna (Yadava) rulers of Devagiri (U.P. RO/ARO 2023 — both statements correct).
  • The Kakatiyas built the original mud fort of Golkonda (see Ch. 3).

Hoysala highlight — Halebidu & Belur

  • Hoysala monuments are at Halebidu and Belur (I.A.S. 2001).
  • Hoysaleshwara temple, Halebidu — dedicated to Shiva; 12th–13th century; built in the reign of Narasimha I by architect Kedroz under the supervision of Ketamal, Chief Officer of the Public Works Department; famous for heritage architecture.
  • Chennakeshava temple, Belur — Vishnu temple built by Hoysala king Vishnuvardhana.
Context — how the southern map was redrawn
  • The Tughluq experiment at Daulatabad was the last time a Delhi ruler tried to command the peninsula from a southern centre; when it collapsed, provincial governors and Hindu feudatories both moved to independence.
  • The Hindu answer was Vijayanagara (south of the Tungabhadra); the Muslim answer was Bahmani (Gulbarga) — the two spent the next two centuries fighting over the Raichur doab and the horse trade.

Bahmani Kingdom — Foundation, Rulers & Mohammad Gawan

Coin titleStyled himself ‘Second Alexander’ on his coins after successful campaigns.

Administration under Alauddin Bahman Shah

  • Adopted the Delhi Sultanate’s structure; divided the realm into four Tarafs (provinces) — Gulbarga, Daulatabad, Berar, Bidar (both the text and PYQ list these four).
  • Each Taraf was governed by a Governor (Tarafdar) responsible for administration, revenue collection and military command.

Mohammad Gawan — the great Wazir

  • Won wars using gunpowder in the Belgaum battle against the Vijayanagar kings.
  • Administrative reforms: divided the existing four provinces into eight Tarafs; each governed by a Tarafdar.
  • Royal officers were paid by land assignments and were held accountable for their income and expenditure.
  • Reforms intensified the conflict between Deccani Muslims and Pardesi (foreigner) Muslims.
  • Granted the title Malikut-Tujjar (Chief of the Merchants) by Humayun Shah.
Trap — the book repeats Gawan’s title under Ibrahim Adil Shah II
  • The theory text lists ‘Malikut-Tujjar’ under Ibrahim Adil Shah II as well — but it is Mohammad Gawan’s title from Bahmani ruler Humayun Shah; Ibrahim II’s title is Jagadguru.

Context — sequence of break-up
  • The Bahmani Sultanate broke into four independent kingdoms — Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Berar, Golkonda; the Bahmani Sultan at Bidar ruled as a puppet, and Bidar became the fifth.
  • Timeline (theory): 1490 — emergence of Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Berar; 1518 — collapse of the Bahmani kingdom and emergence of Golconda.
  • Ahmadnagar and Golkonda first acted independently, then united with Bijapur against Vijayanagar and won at the Battle of Talikota (Rakshasi-Tangadi), 1565 (66th B.P.S.C. Re-Exam 2020). Within about a century the Mughals subjugated the Sultanates.

Ibrahim Adil Shah II of Bijapur (1580–1627)

  • Good administrator, artist, poet and patron of arts; extended the frontier south up to Mysore; reverted to the Sunni sect but stayed tolerant of other religions.
  • Given the title Jagadguru / Jagatguru by his Muslim subjects for his secular/broad-minded outlook (I.A.S. 2000). Also called Ablababa (‘Friend of the Poor’).
  • Author of Kitab-i-Nauras (Book of Nine Rasas / music collection) in Dakhani (Deccani) (U.P.P.C.S. 2020). He issued a coin named Nun-i-Nauras.
  • Well aware of the Indian aesthetic concept of Rasa (essence).
  • Founded a new capital/township, Naurasapur, to realise his idea of a musical city.
  • In his era Firishta completed the historical work Tarikh-i-Firishta.

Gol Gumbaz — Bijapur

  • Mausoleum of Muhammad Adil Shah (1626–1656), the seventh Sultan of the Adil Shahi dynasty; in Bijapur district, Karnataka.
  • Its dome is said to be the largest in the world / one of the largest — I.A.S. 1995.
  • Match (Uttarakhand PCS 2012): Double Dome — Sikandar Lodi; Octagonal Tomb — Sher Shah; True-Arch Tomb — Balban; Gol Gumbad — Muhammad Adil Shah.

Golkonda Fort (Hyderabad)

  • Built first as a mud fort by a ruler of the Kakatiya dynasty.
  • In 1495–96 it was handed to Sultan Quli Qutub Khan as a jagir; he rebuilt it in granite, named the place Muhammed Nagar. The Qutb Shahi dynasty then made Golkonda its capital; Mohammad Quli Qutub Shah enhanced its grandeur.
  • The Fort houses the tombs of the Qutb Shahis; famous for acoustic architecture; highest point — Bala Hissar; entrance — Fateh Darwaza (Victory Gate).
  • By the 17th century Golkonda was famous for the Kohinoor diamond; ‘Golconda is now Hyderabad’ and ‘famous for diamonds’ (M.P. P.C.S. 1990).
  • Aurangzeb’s 1687 siege lasted eight months and fell through treachery; Aurangzeb annexed it, ending the Qutb Shahi dynasty.

Fall of Ahmadnagar (I.A.S. 2004)

  • Ahmadnagar was conquered and annexed by Shah Jahan (1632); Mahabat Khan was sent to take Daulatabad fort; the fort’s holder Fateh Khan was bribed with 10.5 lakh.
  • Last Nizam Shah, Hussain Nizam Shah III, was imprisoned for life in Gwalior Fort.

Trap — I.A.S. 2004 four-statement question (answer: all four correct)
  • Narasimha Saluva ended the Sangama dynasty and started the Saluva; Vira Narasimha deposed the last Saluva ruler and seized the throne; Vira Narasimha was succeeded by his younger brother Krishnadeva Raya; Krishnadeva Raya was succeeded by his half-brother Achyuta Raya.
  • Dynasty association: Sangama, Saluva, Tuluva → Vijayanagara (Chhattisgarh PCS 2023).

Capitals and the city

  • Capitals in sequence: Anegundi → Vijayanagara (Hampi) → Penukonda (Penugonda) → Chandragiri → later Vellore.
  • Initially the capital was near Anegondi (north bank of Tungabhadra); later shifted to the Hoysala town Hosapattana (near Hampi; south bank) and renamed Vijayanagara — ‘city of victory’.
  • Hampi takes its name from the local mother goddess Pampadevi.
  • Hampi was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986; the ruins lie in present central Karnataka (Vijayanagara district) — U.P.P.C.S. Mains 2007/2008 & 63rd B.P.S.C. 2017.
  • The ruins were brought to light in 1800 by Colonel Colin Mackenzie, who in 1815 became the first Surveyor General of India.
  • The Virupaksha temple in Hampi is a Vijayanagara-period temple (the Ghatnachakra text says ‘7th century CE’ — an evident misprint; Vijayanagara began in 1336).

Titles, symbols and traditions

  • Popular tradition: Deccan Sultans = Ashvapati (lord of horses); the Rayas = Narapati (lord of men).
  • Rulers claimed a strong link with deities through the title ‘Hindu Suratrana’ — a Sanskritisation of the Arabic term Sultan.
  • Adopted the Chalukya emblem, the Boar (Varaha), as royal insignia.
  • Harihara II assumed the title Maharajadhiraja; Krishnadeva Raya took Andhra Bhoj and the title ‘establisher of the Yavana kingdom’.

Territorial expansion

  • The entire area of the Hoysala kingdom in Karnataka.
  • Coastal Andhra — power struggle with the Gajapati kingdom of Orissa.
  • Tondai-mandalam (northern Tamil Nadu and southern Andhra) — won by Prince Kumara Kampana, son of Bukka I; he also defeated the Madurai Sultan, ending that Sultanate about 1370 (the conquest of the Madurai region was completed by about 1370 after the army’s 1365 campaign) as told in Madhura Vijayam by his wife Gangadevi (U.P.P.C.S. 2000).
  • Until 1500 the Pandya region including Madurai was independent of Vijayanagara; only the northern and central Tamil areas up to the Kaveri delta were governed by Sangama and Saluva.
  • The Konkan — the narrow strip between the Western Ghats and the sea.
  • At its peak the empire stretched from the River Krishna in the north to the extreme south of the peninsula.

Saluva & Tuluva Dynasties — Krishnadeva Raya, the Peak

1485–1542 · Saluva Narasimha → Vira Narasimha → Krishnadeva Raya → Achyuta

Saluva Narasimha & Vira Narasimha

  • Saluva Narasimha founded the short-lived Saluva dynasty in 1485; Saluvas were military commanders. Literary source for his wars: Saluvabhyudayam.
  • His elder son Viranarsimha started the Tuluva dynasty in 1505 after removing Immadi Narasimha.
Krishnadeva Raya (1509–29) — identity card
AspectDetail
StatusGreatest of all Vijayanagara rulers; younger brother of Vira Narasimha, built on the military foundation laid by his father and elder brother; titles Andhra Bhoj (Jharkhand PCS 2013; U.P. UDA/LDA 2001; R.A.S. 2010) and ‘establisher of the Yavana kingdom’. Babur’s memoirs called him the most powerful ruler of India.
Two-front warFought the Bahmani Sultans (traditional enemy) and the Gajapati rulers of Orissa. Acquired the Raichur Doab (1512), subdued Orissa (1514), defeated the Sultan of Bijapur (1520). Inscriptions describe seizure of Gajapati forts such as Udayagiri; put up a pillar of victory at Simhachalam (Visakhapatnam).
Battle of GolcondaFought Quli Qutb Shah of Golconda (43rd B.P.S.C. 1999). Quli, who founded the Qutb Shahi dynasty, had captured forts such as Warangal and Kondaveedu while Krishnadeva was busy with Odisha; he was finally defeated and the Qutb Shahi army lost.
PortugueseFriendly relations with Albuquerque (Portuguese governor); his ambassador Friar Luis resided at Vijayanagara. Obtained guns and Arabian horses; on Albuquerque’s request permitted a fort at Bhatkal (Uttara Kannada) (I.A.S. 2024). With Portuguese help he established authority along the Malabar and Konkan coasts.
Religious patronageGenerous gifts to temples — Srisailam, Tirupati, Kalahasti, Kanchipuram, Tiruvannamalai, Chidambaram — and added towering gopurams. In spite of religious differences he supported some claimants in the Sultanates.
Foreign praiseDomingo Paes, Fernao Nuniz, Duarte Barbosa praised his character and the opulence of the city.

Achyuta Deva Raya

  • Krishnadeva Raya’s brother (half-brother per I.A.S. 2004) who took the throne; the support of powerful Chellappa (Saluva Nayak, a Brahmana) helped him keep a dominant position.
  • Fernao Nuniz visited during his reign; by 1542 Sadasivaraya succeeded him.

Sadasiva Raya, Rama Raya, Battle of Talikota & Aravidu Dynasty

1543–1650/72 · Talikota 1565

Sadasiva Raya (1543–67) & Rama Raya

  • Real power lay with minister Rama Raya, who gained support from kinsmen of the Aravidu clan by appointing them Nayaks of strategic localities.
  • Rama Raya was a great warrior and strategist: played the Bahmani Muslim powers against one another; entered a commercial treaty with the Portuguese that stopped the supply of horses to the Bijapur ruler; fought Bijapur but later allied with Bijapur against Golconda and Ahmadnagar — a chain of events that led to Talikota.
Battle of Talikota (Rakshasi-Tangadi), 1565
AspectDetail
DateJanuary 1565 A.D. (U.P.P.C.S. 1993; M.P.P.C.S. 1997; 66th B.P.S.C. Re-Exam 2020).
CombatantsVijayanagara vs the combined armies of Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda (theory & U.P.P.C.S. Mains 2014) — Ghatnachakra adds Bidar (four Deccan sultanates: Bijapur, Bidar, Ahmadnagar, Golconda); Berar was not included.
PlaceCalled Talikota after Firishta, but the actual battlefield lay between the villages Rakshasi and Tangadi. Talikota is near Vijayapura district, Karnataka.
Commander & fateRuler was Sadasiva Raya but real power was with Rama Raya; he personally commanded, lost and was killed/executed — Hussain Nizam Shah killed Rama Raya.
ResultFirst time the victorious armies entered Vijayanagara city; they ransacked it for months. Regarded as the signal for the end of Vijayanagara — a watershed that weakened ‘one of the greatest Indian empires originating from southern India before the Maratha Empire’.

After Talikota — the Aravidu dynasty

  • Sadasiva escaped to Penugonda and later ruled from Chandragiri (near Tirupati).
  • Tirumala, brother of Rama Raya, dethroned Sadasiva at Penukonda and declared himself king in 1570; he founded the Aravidu dynasty (last dynasty of great Vijayanagara rulers). Real power was held by Nayak chiefs.
  • His eldest son Shri Ranga I succeeded; then Venkata II, who made Chandragiri his headquarters — one of the greatest rulers of Aravidu.
  • Venkata II was a contemporary of Raja Wodeyar, who founded the Kingdom of Mysore in 1612 (I.A.S. 2006).
Context — the fall of the empire
  • After Talikota the Nayak chiefs became almost independent of the Vijayanagara king — Madurai, Tanjavur and Ikkeri grew into powerful states (see Ch. 9).

Vijayanagara Administration — Provinces, Nayaka, Amara-Nayaka, Ayagar

Central · Provincial · Military · Village

Central government

  • The king was the ultimate authority and supreme commander of the army, assisted by high-ranking officers.
  • Mahapradhani (Chief Minister) led lower-ranking officers: Dalavay (commander), Vassal (palace guard), Rayasam (secretary/accountant), Adaippam (personal attendant), Kariya-karta (executive agents).
  • Central Department of Revenue: Athavane (Asthvan/Athvan).

Nayaka system
FeatureDetail
Meaning‘Nayak’ = military chiefs in Telugu and Kannada regions (from the 13th century); usually controlled forts and had armed supporters.
Origin parallelRevenue assignment for military service existed in the Kakatiya kingdom (13th c.) — similar to the Iqta of the Delhi Sultanate.
ObligationsPer Nuniz each Nayak maintained a fixed number of horses and foot soldiers for the king (matches Rayavachakamu); paid a portion of revenue to the king at the Mahanavami festival.
ControlOften rebelled and had to be subdued; the king transferred them from place to place.
EvidenceVamsavalis (family histories) of the Palayagars, heirs of old Nayak families, confirm the system was refined under Krishnadevaraya.
Social originsBoth Brahmana and non-Brahmana: warriors, pastoral clans (Yadava, Billama), peasants (Reddi), merchants (Balija); prominent Brahmana Nayak — Chellappa under Krishnadevaraya.
PeriodEvident around 1500, solidified under Krishnadevaraya and Achyuta Devaraya; terms: Nayakkattanam (Tamil), Nayaktanam (Kannada), Nayankaramu (Telugu).

PolygarsPalaiyakkarar / Polygar = feudal title of a class of territorial administrative and military governors appointed by the Nayaka rulers of South India in the 16th–18th century (U.P.P.C.S. Re-Exam 2015).
Amara-Nayaka system
FeatureDetail
WhatA major political innovation of Vijayanagara: Amara-nayakas were military commanders given territories to govern by the Raya.
DutiesCollected taxes from peasants, craftspersons and traders; kept part for personal use and to maintain a stipulated contingent of horses and elephants; part spent on temples and irrigation works.
Economic roleNayaktanam chiefs managed production through commercial centres (pettai), tax incentives, settlers and irrigation. Many started as commanders, governors, accountants — the king’s agents.

Ayagar system (village level)

  • A group of 12 village functionaries (officials) constituted by the centre — hereditary families called Ayagars.
  • Paid by tax-free plots/shares of the village produce; land in the village could not be sold or bought without their permission.

Society, Economy, Taxation, Trade & Coinage

Land revenue · Commerce · Portuguese · Varaha

Society

  • Foreign visitors noted the opulent lifestyle of rulers, officials and elites in cities like Vijayanagar and Bijapur, contrasted with widespread poverty and slavery — a widening gap between rulers and ruled.
  • Continuous warfare caused displacement and migration.
  • Women (per Nuniz): wrestlers, astrologers, accountants, soothsayers, also dancers, domestic servants, palanquin bearers (I.A.S. 2021).
  • Social conditions and caste system described in Manucharitram.
Taxation & revenue
PointDetail
Main sourceLand revenue (39th B.P.S.C. 1994). Land was surveyed and classified into four categories: wetland, dry land, orchards and woods.
RateNormally 1/3 to 1/6 of the produce, varying with type of crop, quality of soil, method of irrigation etc.
Chief taxShist (Rai-rekha) — the main source of state income; revenue department = Athavane.
Other taxesProperty (wealth) tax, tax on sale of produce (trade/commercial tax), profession tax, industry tax, tax on marriage, social & community tax, fines on crime, military contribution in times of distress.
Krishnadeva’s systemLand rate by land quality; private workshop owners paid industry tax (I.A.S. 2016).

Economic activity and trade

  • Initially agrarian; became a commercial economy from the 14th century — many commercial and weaving centres in northern Tamil Nadu, Rayalaseema and coastal Andhra; artisans (weavers, smiths, masons) gained importance.
  • Non-agrarian groups: Pattadaior (workshop people) and Kasayavargam (cash-paying group). In the 16th century Nayakas promoted crafts like weaving with occasional tax incentives.
  • Inland, coastal and overseas trade brought prosperity; markets for spices, textiles, precious stones.
  • Horse trade: initially controlled by Arab traders importing horses from Arabia and Central Asia; local merchants Kudirai Chettis (horse merchants) also took part.
  • From 1498 the Portuguese sought trade and military posts on the west coast; textiles were their main attraction in the early 16th century; superior military technology such as muskets raised their importance.

Hampi — City Planning, Water Works & Temples

Sacred Centre · Royal Centre · Fortifications

City plan and fortification

  • Vijayanagara City was divided into the Sacred Centre and the Royal Centre.
  • Abdur Razzaq mentioned seven lines of forts enclosing not just the city but its agricultural hinterland and forests; no mortar or cementing agent was used; great fortress walls also enclosed agricultural tracts; the arch and dome on the gateway show Indo-Islamic features.
  • Often called the Dravida style, but it had distinct features — the book calls it Provida style (large numbers of pillars and piers).
  • Urban core: tombs and mosques with distinctive functions, yet resembling temple mandapas; the area was dotted with shrines supported by different communities.
  • Water resources: Kamalapuram tank, Hiriya canal (from the Tungabhadra) and an elaborate canal system drawing water from the river.
