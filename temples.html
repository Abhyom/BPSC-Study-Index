01 Styles & Classification of Temple Architecture

Draws on Q12, Q13, Q14, Q27, Q95, Q96.

Nagara, Dravida and Vesara are the three main styles of Indian temple architecture — not racial groups, linguistic divisions, or musical gharanas.

The three styles at a glance

  • Nagara — prevalent throughout North India; the temples of Odisha are purely of this style.
  • Dravida (Dravidian) — developed and spread in South India, especially between the Krishna River and Cape Comorin (Tamil Nadu).
  • Vesara — grew from a mixture of Nagara and Dravidian styles; also called the "Chalukya style" because it was used by the later Chalukya rulers. The Hoysaleshwara Temple at Halebidu (Karnataka), built in dark schist stone by the Hoysala king, is a leading Vesara/hybrid example — neither purely Dravida nor Nagara, identifiable by star-shaped ground plans and dense decorative carving.

Some monuments blend styles directly: the Teli Temple of Gwalior fuses both — its Shikhar (pinnacle) is Dravidian in style while its ornamentation follows Nagara conventions.

A Panchayatan temple has a central shrine surrounded by four subsidiary shrines (Sanskrit: pancha = five, ayatana = containing). Examples include the Kandariya Mahadeo Temple (Khajuraho), the Brahmeshwar and Lingaraja Temples (Bhubaneswar), and the Dashavatara Temple (Deogarh, UP) — the last being the earliest known Panchayatan temple in North India.

Origins of freestanding temple architecture

Freestanding (mortar-and-stone) temples are believed to have originated in the Gupta period. Ladkhan, one of the earliest such temples, belongs to the Chalukyas of Badami. At Khajuraho, all compartments of a temple are connected both internally and externally. The Kailashnath Temple at Kanchi — though a fine early Dravida temple built by Narasimhavarman-II (Rajasimha) and completed by his son Mahendravarman-II — is NOT the earliest freestanding Dravida-style temple.

Badami's caves are NOT the oldest surviving rock-cut caves in India — that distinction belongs to the Bhimbetka rock shelters.

02 Khajuraho & Chandela Architecture

Draws on the main theory block plus Q1–Q11.

The Khajuraho temples evidence the excellence of Chandela architecture, located in Chhatarpur district, Madhya Pradesh, built between 950–1050 AD. Historical record describes the construction of 85 temples at the site, dedicated to Shiva, Vishnu, Shakta, Surya and the Jain patriarchs — i.e., a mix of Hindu and Jain temples (no Buddhist dedication).

Named temples at Khajuraho

  • Kandariya Mahadev Temple — attributed to King Vidyadhar (11th century); the largest of the Khajuraho temples; built in Nagara style.
  • Matangeshvara Temple — dedicated to Shiva; built during King Dhanga's reign; Nagara style.
  • Parshvanath Temple — the most famous Jain temple at the site.
  • Others: Chausath Yogini, Brahma, Lalguan Mahadev, Lakshmana, Vishvanath temples.

⚠ Trap — what is NOT at Khajuraho

The Dashavatara Temple is NOT at Khajuraho — it is located at Deogarh (Lalitpur), Uttar Pradesh, and belongs to the Gupta period.

Khajuraho group of monuments, Bhimbetka rock shelters, and Sanchi Stupa are all listed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites in India. Mandu Fort is NOT a World Heritage Site.

Chausath Yogini Temple (near Morena — often studied alongside Khajuraho)

This circular temple was built during the reign of the Kachchhapaghata dynasty, per an inscription dated 1323 CE (Vikram Samvat 1383), under king Devapala. Its circular form is popularly (though not confirmedly) believed to have inspired the design of the Indian Parliament building — the shape is thought to represent a Sri Yantra, with the Supreme Yogini/Maha Sakti residing at the circular central shrine. It is NOT the only circular temple in India, and it was NOT meant to promote the Vaishnava cult.

03 Gupta-Period Temples & Cave Painting

Draws on Q10, Q23, Q44–Q51.

Cave painting

Ajanta and Bagh caves are the only two known examples of cave paintings from the Gupta period. Ajanta lies in Chhatrapati Sambhaji Nagar district, Maharashtra, in the gorge/valley of the Waghora river; an earlier group of caves was built during the Satavahana period, with a major later portion made during the Vakataka period — the art of Ajanta was mainly patronised by the Vakatakas. Its murals and sculptures are considered masterpieces of religious art. Cave 16 carries an important painted theme, and Cave 17 preserves Vakataka-age painting including a huge "Wheel of Life."

The most famous mural at Ajanta — in Cave 1 — is the painting of Bodhisattva Padmapani ("one who holds the lotus"): shown amid a crowd of devotees (both princesses and commoners), with long dark hair flowing beneath a jewelled crown, holding a blue lotus flower in his right hand.

Ajanta is linked to the Jataka stories — its paintings concentrate on tales of the Buddha's previous life as a king. Bagh caves are the other surviving Gupta-period cave painting site, and reflect scenes of ordinary public life. (For contrast: Lomas Rishi caves belong to the Mauryan period, located in the Barabar hills, Jahanabad district, Bihar; Nasik caves belong to the Satavahana period.)

Mural-painting evidence is clearly visible at both Ajanta and the Lepakshi temple — but not at Sanchi Stupa, whose core is simply a hemispherical brick structure crowned by a Chhatra (parasol) honouring the Buddha's relics. Also, Buddhist caves are NOT found at Sanchi or Bulsar (Ajanta and Bagh do carry Buddhist art).

Gupta temples

The Deogarh temple near Jhansi (Lalitpur) and the sculptures at the Garhwa temple complex (Shankargarh, Prayagraj) are important remains of Gupta art. The Bhitargaon temple, Kanpur (UP) is a terraced brick building fronted with a terracotta panel, constructed during the 5th–6th century AD, and is one of the oldest brick temples of India — built on a square plan with double-recessed corners, facing east, with a tall pyramidal spire over the garbhagriha, in Nagara style.

04 Rock-cut Cave Architecture

Draws on Q15, Q18–Q43.

The classic rock-cut caves of western India are located at Nasik, Ellora and Ajanta. Ajanta and Ellora both sit in the Chhatrapati Sambhaji Nagar (formerly Aurangabad) district, Maharashtra. Nasik's caves are also known as "Pandavleni" or Trirashmi caves, representing Buddhist devotional practice from the Satavahana period. Kanheri caves, north of Borivali on Mumbai's western outskirts, form a group of rock-cut monuments built during the 1st century BC, demonstrating Buddhist influence, and constitute a centre with more than 100 Buddha caves. In the Barabar Hills, Ashoka donated caves to the Ajivikas — four caves named Karan Chaupar, Lomas Rishi, Sudama and Vishva Zopari.

Ellora

Ellora (locally "Verul Leni") is world-famous for rock-cut cave temples: a total of 34 caves, built across different periods — caves 1–12 Buddhist, 13–29 Hindu, and 30–34 Jain. Buddhist, Hindu and Jain rock-cut caves thus coexist at a single site. The Kailasa Temple (Cave 16) is the standout example of rock-cut architecture — built by the Rashtrakuta ruler Krishna-I in Dravidian style, designed to recall Mount Kailasa, the abode of Shiva, and admired for its striking proportion, elaborate workmanship and sculptural ornamentation.

Rashtrakuta kings patronised followers of the Shaiva, Vaishnava and Shakta faiths, but several were inclined toward Jainism — many court scholars wrote in Sanskrit, Kannada, and some in Apabhramsha and Prakrit, and were Jains. King Amoghavarsha-I was himself a disciple of the Jain Acharya Jinasena, author of the Adipurana.

Elephanta

Elephanta caves, believed built by the Rashtrakuta dynasty, sit on an island on an arm of the Arabian Sea, comprising five large Hindu caves and two smaller Buddhist caves, dated to the 5th–6th century AD. The Hindu caves, dedicated to Shiva (Shaiva sect), house the famous Trimurti (Mahesa-murti) statue — the three faces of Shiva echoing the Brahma-Vishnu-Mahesh trinity, which also served as the royal insignia of the Rashtrakutas. Both Ellora and Elephanta belong to the Rashtrakuta period.

05 Pallava Architecture

Draws on Q54, Q72–Q79, Q84.

Narasimhavarman-I ("Mahamalla") (630–668 AD) was among the most celebrated Pallava kings; the Mamalla style developed under his reign, and the city of Mamallapuram was named after his title ("great wrestler"). He built the Seven Ratha Temple, adorned with seven rock-cut pagodas.

Mahabalipuram (Mamallapuram) lies on the seashore about 55 km south of Chennai (Chengalpattu district, Tamil Nadu) and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site; it is also called the "Mandapas" Rock-Cut Caves and was established across the 7th–10th centuries of the Pallava dynasty. It was a pilgrimage site even before the Pallava period, and became a major centre of Pallava art. Its famous monuments include the Shore Temple, Rathas carved from single rocks, Arjuna's Penance, and other cave temples.

⚠ Trap — Draupadi Rath's ornamentation

One passage says Draupadi Rath is the smallest rath (Mamalla-style, monolithic) and is "adorned with carvings, including those of Goddess Durga." An MCQ explanation elsewhere in the same source says of the same rath: "there is no ornamentation found." Both statements appear verbatim — for MCQ purposes, its defining, unambiguous fact is simply that it is the smallest of the Pancha Rathas. The Panch Rathas as a set are accompanied by statues of an elephant, a lion, and a Nandi bull.

The Kailasanathar Temple, Kanchipuram was built by the Pallava king Narasimhavarman-II, in the Rajsimha architectural style — marked by intricate carvings, large Gopurams, and finely detailed stone sculpture; its main deity is Lord Shiva.

The Sittanavasal cave temple was built by the Pallava king Mahendravarman-I for Jainism.

Chronology check

Correct construction order: Sapt Pagoda (Mamalla style, 640–674 AD) → Shore Temple of Mahabalipuram (Rajsimha style, 674–800 AD) → Brihadeeswara Temple (Rajaraja-I period) → Gangaikonda Cholapuram Temple (Rajendra-I period).

Ajanta and Mahabalipuram share one feature and differ on two: both are rock-cut monuments, but they were NOT built in the same period (Ajanta ranges 2nd century BC–7th century AD; Mahabalipuram is 7th–10th century AD Pallava work) and belong to different religious traditions (Mahabalipuram: Shiva/Vishnu; Ajanta: Buddhist).

06 Chola Temple Architecture

The Korangnath Temple of Srinivasanallur was built during the reign of Chola ruler Parantaka-I. The Rajarajeshwara (Brihadeeswara) Temple at Thanjavur was constructed under Rajaraja-I, using granite stone — and, unlike the Ellora Kailasa temple, it is not an example of rock-cut architecture. During the reign of Rajaraja's son Rajendra Chola, the temple at Gangaikonda Cholapuram was built.

The Gopuram — the ornamental, multi-storied gateway structure of Dravidian-style temples — traces its origin to early Pallava structures, and by the 12th century became a dominant feature under the Pandya rulers, its scale eventually overshadowing the inner sanctuary itself, which became hidden from outside view. Temples built in Dravidian style under the Pandyas were themselves small, but wrapped in elaborate ramparts and richly ornamented Gopuram gateways.

07 Odisha (and related Eastern) Temples

Draws on Q56–Q66, Q86.

The Konark Sun Temple is a 13th-century temple built by King Narasimhadeva-I of the Eastern Ganga Dynasty. It was conceived as a giant solar chariot with twelve pairs of ornamental wheels, dragged by seven rearing horses. European sailors called it the "Black Pagoda" — the Jagannath Temple at Puri, by contrast, was called the "White Pagoda."

The Lingaraja Temple, Bhubaneswar, is dedicated to Lord Shiva (worshipped here as Lingaraj, considered a form of Harihara) and is one of the oldest temples in the city. It stands roughly 180 ft tall and enshrines a 'Swayambhu' (self-originated) Shivling, built in Nagara style — and is the largest and tallest surviving temple in Odisha (ahead of the Brahmeshwara, Jagannath and Rajarani temples).

The Jagannath Temple, Puri, is also in Nagara style. The idols of Jagannath, Subhadra and Balbhadra in its sanctum sanctorum are carved from neem wood — chosen because the Bhavishya Purana names it the most auspicious wood for Vishnu images. The wood image is repainted yearly before the Ratha-yatra, and replaced with a freshly carved image roughly every 12–19 years, timed to years when the lunar month of Asadha occurs twice under the Luni-solar Hindu calendar.

The temples of both Bhubaneswar and Puri were built in Nagara style.

The Arasavalli Sun Temple, a prominent 7th-century sun temple in Andhra Pradesh, is believed to have been built by King Devendra Varma of the Eastern Ganga (Kalinga) Dynasty. By contrast, Amarkantak and Omkareshwar (MP) are NOT sun temples — Omkareshwar (Khandwa district, on an island in the Narmada) is one of the 12 Jyotirlinga shrines of Shiva, and Amarkantak is the source of the Narmada, Son and Johila rivers, home to the Jaleswara Mahadev Shiva temple.

08 Jain Temples

The Vimal Vasahi temple, the most famous of the Dilwara Jain temples at Mount Abu (Aravalli hills, Rajasthan), was built by Vimal Shah, minister/feudatory of the Chalukya (Solanki) ruler Bheemdev-I (Bhima-I). It is famed for extraordinary architecture and marvellous marble stone carving.

The Palitana temples of Jainism sit on Shatrunjaya hill near Palitana, Bhavnagar district, Gujarat, dedicated to the first Jain Tirthankara, Adinatha.

Sonagiri, about 15 km from Datia (MP), is a sacred site for Digambar Jain devotees and ascetics practising self-discipline, austerity and the pursuit of Nirvana. It has 103 Jain temples with sky-high spires; Temple No. 57, dedicated to Lord Chandraprabhu, is the principal one.

09 Other Regional & South-East Asian Sites

The Sun Temple of Modhera (Patan, Gujarat) was built by King Bhimadeva-I of the Solanki dynasty.

The Virupaksha Temple is located at Hampi, Karnataka, dedicated to Lord Shiva, worshipped here as Virupaksha.

Angkor Wat, in present-day Cambodia (Kampuchea), was built by the Khmer king Suryavarman-II at the start of the 12th century in his capital Yashodhara Pura (present-day Angkor). Dedicated to Lord Vishnu, it is the world's largest religious structure, built in Khmer-style architecture.

The famous Borobudur stupa is on Java island, Indonesia — a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Folk paintings (frequently paired with architecture questions)

Style ↔ origin/technique

  • Pattachitra — belongs to Odisha; themes drawn from the Jagannath and Vaishnav cults.
  • Manjusha painting — a folk art of Bhagalpur, Bihar, one of India's oldest painting traditions; executed on temple-shaped boxes of jute and paper with eight pillars, using only three colours — pink, green and yellow; used in Bishahari Puja.
  • Pithora painting — a ritualistic painting tradition of tribal communities in Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh.

⚠ Trap — the Varaha Avatar sculpture's location

A famous huge rock-carved Varaha Avatar (boar incarnation) of Vishnu, shown rescuing Goddess Earth, is sometimes paired with "Mount Abu" in matching questions — this pairing is incorrect. The sculpture is actually at the Udayagiri caves, Madhya Pradesh. Two other pairs in the same matching set ARE correct: a grand image of the Buddha's Mahaparinirvana with celestial musicians above and sorrowful followers below is at Ajanta (Cave 26); "Arjuna's Penance"/"Descent of the Ganga", carved on huge boulders, is at Mamallapuram.

Odds & ends

Naimisharanya, in Sitapur district, Uttar Pradesh, is where Sage Dadhichi is believed to have given up his bones to the Gods; the bones were used to make the weapon Vajra, which Lord Indra then used to defeat the Asuras.

Takshashila, established around the 6th–7th century BC, was located between the rivers Jhelum and Indus (present-day Rawalpindi district, Punjab, Pakistan).

Chitrakoot is a Hindu pilgrimage site straddling both Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh — its main holy place, Kamadgiri (associated with Lord Kamata Nath), itself has portions in both states; BPSC's re-exam accepted Madhya Pradesh as the answer despite the ambiguity.

Sanchi Stupa lies west of the Betwa river in Raisen district, MP (built by Ashoka) — not in a Chambal river gorge. Pandu-lena cave shrines (Nashik) lie in the Godavari valley. Amaravati Stupa stands on the banks of the Krishna river, Palnadu district, Andhra Pradesh.
