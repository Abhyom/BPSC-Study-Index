Formation of the Swaraj Party (1923)

From council boycott to "enter and wreck"
Swaraj Party — full picture
Aspect Detail
Background The Central Legislature (Council of States & Legislative Assembly) and Provincial Legislative Councils, set up by the Government of India Act, 1919, were boycotted by Congress on Gandhiji's directions — Congress did not contest the 1920 elections. After the withdrawal of the Non-Cooperation Movement and Gandhi's arrest, an atmosphere of hopelessness set in.
New ideology C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru proposed that Congress should fight elections, enter the councils, become part of Government, and continue the struggle from within — this plan was rejected by the majority of Congressmen.
Split & founding Suspension of the NCM led to a split within Congress at the Gaya session, December 1922. C.R. Das, then President of Congress, resigned the presidency and, with Motilal Nehru, organised a party within Congress in January 1923, named the Swaraj Party (also called Swaraj Dal).
Mandate / strategy Enter the Councils and hinder/wreck their working through agitation from within — slogan "end or mend"; the aim was to expose the councils as "sham parliaments."
Leadership C.R. Das — President of the Party; Motilal Nehru — General Secretary; S. Srinivasa Iyengar (founder of the Swaraj Party at Madras) and N.C. Kelkar were the other main leaders.
Elections contested Legislative Council elections held November 1923. The Swarajists won 42 out of 104 elected seats and a clear majority in the provincial assemblies of the Central Provinces; in the Central Legislature they joined hands with liberals and independents like Jinnah and Malviya.
Biggest achievement Election of Vithalbhai Patel as Speaker/President of the (Central) Legislative Assembly in 1925 — he was the first Indian Speaker of the Central Legislative Assembly, and was also a co-founder of the Swaraj Party.
Legislative actions Swarajists disrupted the councils, passed resolutions for self-government, demanded civil liberties, and exposed British repressive policies.
Decline Faced internal divisions including communal tensions; declined after the death of C.R. Das in 1925.
Demand Setting up of responsible Government in India with necessary changes to the GoI Act, 1919.

Other 1922–23 political splits and fallout

  • Madan Mohan Malviya — founder member of the Hindu Mahasabha; also founded Benaras Hindu University in 1916.
  • On 16 December 1922, Madan Mohan Malviya and Motilal Nehru decided to launch the Independent Party (UP PCS Mains 2006 — code pairs 2 & 4; Lala Har Dayal and M.A. Jinnah were not part of this decision).
  • When Congress condemned the Montagu-Chelmsford Report, many moderates left the party to form the Indian Liberal Federation (IAS Pre 2003) — Surendranath Banerjee and other Liberal leaders had welcomed the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms and disassociated from Congress.
  • When the Montford Reforms report was made public in 1918, Congress split — moderates accepted it, extremists opposed it; the moderate remnants (Sapru, Jayakar, Chintamani) formed the Indian National Liberal Federation in 1918 (68th BPSC 2022), founded by Surendra Nath Banerjee; T.B. Sapru, V.S. Srinivas Sastri and M.R. Jayakar were prominent leaders.
MCQ Trap · who was / wasn't in the Swaraj Party
  • Not associated with formation of Swaraj Party: Subhash Chandra Bose and Jawaharlal Nehru (only C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru are correct — UP PCS Pre 1998).
  • Not a member of the Swaraj Party: C. Rajagopalachari (UP PCS 1993/1991) and Rajendra Prasad (UP PCS Mains 2014) — both were "No-Changers," not Swarajists.
  • Party name formed by Motilal Nehru & C.R. Das in 1923 is the Swaraj Party — not the Independence Party, Ghadar Party, or Indian National Party (UP PCS Pre 2016).
Context — Pro-changers vs No-changers After the Non-Cooperation Movement, Congress divided into two groups. The pro-changers wanted to enter the legislatures — led by C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru and Vithalbhai Patel; they formed the Swaraj Party in 1923. The no-changers wanted to boycott the legislatures — led by Vallabhbhai Patel, C. Rajagopalachari and Rajendra Prasad. This is why Rajagopalachari and Rajendra Prasad are the classic "was NOT part of Swaraj Party" trap answers.

Swaraj Dal in Bihar & chronology check

  • A branch of Swaraj Dal was formed in Bihar, led by Shri Krishna Singh (65th BPSC 2019).
  • Correct chronological order (R.A.S./R.T.S. 2018): Lucknow Pact (1916) → Jallianwala Massacre (1919) → Death of Bal Gangadhar Tilak (1920) → Formation of Swaraj Party (1923).
  • Who supported the Swaraj Party in the Central Legislative Assembly: M.A. Jinnah (UP PCS 2017) — the Swarajists, having won 42/104 seats in the Nov 1923 elections, joined hands with liberals and independents like Jinnah and Malviya.

Emergence of Swarajists and Socialist Ideas
Swarajists vs No-Changers
Aspect Swarajists No-Changers
Key Leaders C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru (66th BPSC) C. Rajagopalachari, Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad, M.A. Ansari

Simon Commission (1927)

The "White Commission" and "Simon Go Back"
Simon Commission — setup and composition
Aspect Detail
Statutory basis The Government of India Act, 1919 had a provision that a commission would be appointed after ten years to study the progress of the government scheme and suggest new steps — it was based on the recommendations of Lord Chelmsford and Samuel Montagu to introduce self-governing institutions gradually.
Established November 1927 (specifically 8 November 1927) by the British Conservative Government under PM Stanley Baldwin, to report on the working of the Indian Constitution under the GoI Act, 1919.
Composition An all-White, seven-member Indian Statutory Commission chaired by Sir John Simon (Liberal Party). Members: Sir John Simon, Clement Attlee (Labour Party — later UK PM during India's independence), Sir Harry Levy-Lawson, Sir Edward Cecil George Cadogan, Vernon Hartshorn, George Richard Lane-Fox, Donald Sterling Palmer Howard. No Indian member — on the suggestion of Lord Irwin.
Objective Assess whether India was ready for further constitutional reforms and recommend changes to the British Government.
Political context The Conservative Government aimed to act before a possible Labour Party victory in Britain.
Indian response Opposed and boycotted by the Indian National Congress and a section of the Muslim League (headed by Muhammad Ali Jinnah); termed the "White Commission" by Indians, purely on the ground that it had no Indian member. Congress's Madras session, December 1927, led by M.A. Ansari and Jawaharlal Nehru, decided to boycott the Commission and demand complete independence.
Arrival & protests Reached Bombay on 3 February 1928, sparking countrywide hartals, rallies and protests across Calcutta, Lahore, Madras, Lucknow, with the slogan "Simon, Go Back!"
Police violence Lala Lajpat Rai, leading the protest in Lahore, was severely injured in a lathi charge in October 1928 and succumbed to his injuries on 17 November 1928. He was a major political leader of Punjab known as 'Punjab Kesari'. This directly led to Bhagat Singh's assassination of Saunders in retaliation.
Dr. B.R. Ambedkar Advocated before the Commission for Universal Adult Franchise and protections for the depressed classes.
Visits to India Visited India twice: first visit 3 February – 31 March 1928; second visit 11 October 1928 – 13 April 1929 (UP RO/ARO Pre 2023 — a statement claiming the second visit ran Oct 1929–Apr 1930 is incorrect).
Simon Commission Recommendations (1930)
Area Recommendation
Provincial reforms Abolish dyarchy, increase legislative councils, grant provincial autonomy, governors given discretionary powers on security — i.e. recommended replacement of diarchy with responsible government in the provinces (IAS Pre 2010).
Central governance Rejected parliamentary responsibility at the centre; strengthened the Governor-General's authority; retained central control over High Courts.
Communal electorates Extended separate communal electorates.
Federalism Gradual federalism; a Consultative Council of Greater India; reconstitution of the Central legislature with a federal (not unitary) character, members elected indirectly from provincial legislatures.
Local legislatures For NWFP and Baluchistan, represented at the centre.
Separation Separate Sindh from Bombay; Burma from India.
Military Indianization of the army, while retaining British forces.
Context — the composite MCQ statement A frequently tested compound statement (UP PCS Mains 2005) holds all of the following as true: (1) the Commission was appointed to inquire into the working of the 1919 Act; (2) it was headed by Sir John Simon; (3) it recommended a Federal form of Government; (4) it was opposed by Indian leaders. All four check out — remember this as one bundle.
MCQ Trap · why was it boycotted The boycott was only because it had no Indian member — not because Indians opposed reviewing the 1919 Act, not because it recommended abolishing dyarchy, and not because of any partition suggestion. Assertion–Reason questions on this (UP PCS 2010, UDA/LDA Spl 2010, Mains 2010) confirm: both statements true, and "no Indian member" correctly explains "Congress boycotted it."

Nehru Report (1928)

India's own answer to the Simon Commission
Nehru Report — origin and recommendations
Aspect Detail
Trigger Lord Birkenhead (Secretary of State for India) challenged Indians to produce a unified constitutional proposal if they rejected the Simon Commission.
Initiative February 1928: the All Parties Conference (held in Delhi, presided over by Dr. M.A. Ansari, attended by representatives of 29 organisations — Hindu Mahasabha, Muslim League, Khilafat Committee etc.) appointed a subcommittee led by Motilal Nehru to draft a constitution.
Committee Chairman: Motilal Nehru; Secretary: Jawaharlal Nehru. Ten members including two Muslim members. Named members: Tej Bahadur Sapru, Subhas Chandra Bose, M.S. Aney, Mangal Singh, Ali Imam, Shuaib Qureshi, G.R. Pradhan.
Finalised August 1928.
Dominion Status Proposed Dominion Status akin to self-governing dominions within the British Empire — not complete independence.
Electoral system Rejected separate electorates; advocated joint/mixed electorates with reserved constituencies for minorities (excluding Punjab and Bengal, where minorities were actually a majority).
Provincial reforms Suggested establishment of linguistic provinces.
Fundamental rights Proposed 19 fundamental rights, including equal rights for women, right to form unions, universal adult suffrage, right of free expression of opinion, right to assemble peaceably and without arms, right to form associations/unions.
Governance structure Indian Parliament: House of Representatives (500 members, elected by adult suffrage, 5-year term); Senate (200 members, elected by provincial councils, 7-year term). Governor-General: appointed by the British Government, paid from Indian revenues, worked with a central executive council responsible to Parliament. Provincial Councils: headed by a governor acting on the advice of provincial executive councils.
Muslim interests Ensured full protection of Muslim cultural and religious interests.
Secular state Advocated complete separation of state from religion.

Aftermath

  • Congress set a two-year deadline for Dominion Status, later reduced to one year, threatening civil disobedience if unmet.
  • Younger nationalists — Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhash Chandra Bose — were dissatisfied with the Dominion Status demand and founded the Independence for India League in opposition to the Nehru Report itself (IAS Pre 1995), demanding Complete Independence instead.
  • December 1929, Lahore Session of Congress adopted the Complete Independence resolution; Nehru's Tricolour hoisting symbolised this commitment at midnight, 31 December 1929.
  • Despite its limitations, the Report laid the groundwork for future discussions on Indian governance and constitutional reform.
MCQ Trap · what the Nehru Report actually recommended
  • It recommended Dominion Status, not Complete Independence (IAS Pre 2011) — statement "it recommended Complete Independence" is false.
  • It recommended joint electorates with reservation of seats for minorities and provision of fundamental rights — both true.
  • First to demand Dominion Status for India, per one PYQ's coded answer: Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru and Jaykar (UP PCS 2013) — the option pairing Motilal Nehru with Govind Ballabh Pant is the trap, since Pant was not connected to that 1926 demand.
  • Responsible for founding the Independence for India League (1928): Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhash Chandra Bose only — not Acharya Narendra Dev or Jaya Prakash Narayan (UP Lower Spl 2008).

Communal Responses — Delhi Proposals & Jinnah's Fourteen Points

Muslim League and Hindu Mahasabha reactions to the Nehru Report
Delhi Proposals (1927) vs Jinnah's Fourteen Points (March 1929)
Delhi Proposals (Dec 1927, Muslim League) Jinnah's Fourteen Points (March 1929)
Replace separate electorates with joint electorates, reserving seats for Muslims. Reversed course: separate electorates for Muslims restored as a demand.
One-third representation for Muslims in the Central Assembly. One-third representation for Muslims in the central legislature and any cabinet.
Proportional representation in Punjab and Bengal. Territorial redistribution to protect Muslim majorities in Punjab, Bengal and NWFP; ensuring no Muslim-majority province turned into a minority.
Creation of Sindh, Balochistan, and NWFP as Muslim-majority provinces. Separation of Sindh from Bombay; constitutional reforms in NWFP and Balochistan.
— Federal constitution with provincial residual powers; provincial autonomy, no constitutional amendments without state concurrence.
— Veto power for minorities — no bill passed if three-fourths of a minority opposed it.
— Muslim representation in services and self-governing bodies; religious freedom guaranteed to all communities; protection of Muslim rights in religion, culture, education, language.

Amendments proposed to the Nehru Report (1928)

  • One-third representation for Muslims in the central legislature.
  • Reservations for Muslims in Bengal and Punjab legislatures based on population, until adult suffrage.
  • Residual powers for provinces.
Context — Hindu Mahasabha's response & the Report's fate The Hindu Mahasabha opposed the creation of new Muslim-majority provinces and reserved seats in Punjab and Bengal, advocating instead for a unitary political structure — complicating communal negotiations. The Nehru Report was rejected by the Muslim League, the Hindu Mahasabha, Sikh communalists, and younger Congress members (all dissatisfied with Dominion Status for different reasons) — this three-way rejection directly contributed to Nehru and Bose forming the Independence for India League.
MCQ Trap · which session, which year Jinnah put forth his Fourteen Points proposal in the 1929 session of the Muslim League (UP PCS Mains 2015) — not 1927, 1928, or 1930. The Fourteen Points were a reply to (and rejection of) the Nehru Report's terms.

Independence for India League & the Rise of Youth Activism

1928–1929

Independence for India League (1928)

  • Founded in 1928 by Subhas Chandra Bose and Jawaharlal Nehru to demand Complete Independence instead of Dominion Status.
  • Formed in direct opposition to the Nehru Report, whose Dominion Status demand had disappointed the Congress's radical/youth wing.
  • Played an important role in uniting progressive powers against British rule.

Emergence of Youth Activism in the Freedom Struggle

  • Youth brought energy and vision that shaped the trajectory of the independence movement.
  • Organisational platforms: in the late 1920s and 1930s, youths established student leagues and political wings for idea exchange and strategising.
  • Significance of 1928: Jawaharlal Nehru's presidency at the All Bengal Students' Conference symbolised the union of seasoned wisdom with youthful vigour.
  • Active participation: young activists engaged in boycotts, protests, and non-violent resistance, reaching into rural areas.
  • Literary influence: pamphlets and underground newsletters were produced and circulated, envisioning a liberated India.
  • Colonial repression: the British used arrests and expulsions to stifle youth influence — but this only fuelled resistance.

The Left Movement — CPI, CSP, RSP & M.N. Roy

Emergence of new forces in the 1920s
Left parties of the freedom movement
Party Founded Key facts
Communist Party of India (CPI) 1920 / formally 1925 M.N. Roy founded the CPI in 1920; it was formally established at the Indian Communist Conference, Kanpur, in 1925. Faced government crackdowns (e.g. Meerut Conspiracy Case 1929); aligned with Congress, promoting Marxist ideas and strengthening its left-wing presence. Later split into factions such as CPI(M).
Congress Socialist Party (CSP) 17 May 1934, Patna President: Acharya Narendra Dev (66th BPSC Re-Exam). Supported cooperative economies, independent farmers, local governance; opposed the dictatorship of the proletariat. Pushed for radical measures within Congress — removal of middlemen, cancellation of peasants' debts. After independence, struggled to redefine its role and eventually dissolved as Congress adopted a socialist agenda under Nehru.
Revolutionary Socialist Party (RSP) 1940, Bengali socialist movement Criticised both Soviet-style socialism and the Gandhian approach; active primarily in West Bengal and Kerala; focused on working-class rights, opposing both capitalist and Stalinist models. Limited electoral success but influenced leftist policy debates.
Radical Democratic Party 1940 Established by M.N. Roy (UP PCS Mains 2007).
Revolutionary Communist Party of India (RCPI) 1934 Founded by Saumendra Nath Tagore, breaking away from the CPI (UP PCS Re-Exam 2015).

Origins of the Left movement — driving factors

  • Development of Indian Industries: economic advancements prompted the rise of new political ideas.
  • Economic stress from World War I: the war exacerbated existing economic hardships.
  • Success of the Bolshevik Revolution: inspired young nationalists to seek radical solutions to India's socio-political challenges.

Key figures

  • M.N. Roy — pivotal figure introducing communist ideas to India; founded the CPI in 1920. Visited Russia on the invitation of Lenin and joined the Communist International (founded March 1919 by Lenin and the Bolshevik Party of Russia) — the first Indian to do so. 17 October 1920: Roy, along with Abani Mukherjee, Mohd. Ali, Mohd. Shafique, Evelyn Roy Trent, Rosa Fitingov and M.P.T. Acharya, founded the Indian Communist Party at Tashkent.
  • Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhash Chandra Bose — influential leaders within Congress's left wing, shaped by socialist thought.
  • S.A. Dange — involved in the Kanpur Bolshevik Conspiracy Case of 1924, contributing to the spread of communist ideas.

Revolutionary Underground — HRA → HSRA, Kakori to the Assembly Bombing

Punjab, UP & Bihar, 1924–1931

Major influences driving revolutionary activity

  • Impact of the Russian Revolution (1917): the establishment of the Soviet state inspired Indian revolutionaries, infusing Marxist and socialist ideologies into their struggles.
  • Emergence of Communist Groups: new communist organisations promoted Marxism and emphasised the role of the proletariat in revolution.
  • Role of publications: journals such as Atmasakti, Sarathi, and Bijoli, along with banned literature like Bandi Jiwan by Sachin Sanyal and Pather Dabi by Sharatchandra Chatterjee, glorified revolutionary sacrifices and spread socialist ideas.
HRA / HSRA and the Kakori–Assembly Bombing chain
Case / Organisation (Year, Place) Associated people & description
Hindustan Republican Association (HRA) / Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA)
(1924, Punjab, United Provinces, Bihar) 		Founded in Kanpur by Ramprasad Bismil, Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee, and Sachin Sanyal. Aim: overthrow the colonial government through armed revolution and establish a Federal Republic of the United States of India. Reorganised as HSRA in September 1928 under Chandrashekhar Azad, reflecting an ideological shift toward socialist ideas. Key participants: Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, Bhagwaticharan Vohra (Punjab), and Bejoy Kumar Sinha, Shiv Verma, Jaidev Kapur (United Provinces).
Kakori Robbery
(1925, Kakori, near Lucknow) 		HRA revolutionaries looted the 8-Down train at Kakori railway station near Lucknow. Led to arrest of several revolutionaries; key figures Bismil, Ashfaqullah, Roshan Singh, and Rajendra Lahiri were executed (65th BPSC).
Saunders' Murder
(December 1928, Lahore) 		Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad, and Rajguru shot dead Saunders, the police official responsible for the lathi-charge in Lahore that killed Lala Lajpat Rai — retaliation for his death during the anti-Simon-Commission protest. Bhagat Singh and Rajguru mistakenly assassinated John P. Saunders instead of their intended target, James Scott.
Bombing of Central Legislative Assembly
(April 1929, Delhi) (70th BPSC Re-exam) 		Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt bombed the Central Legislative Assembly in protest against the Public Safety Bill and the Trade Disputes Bill on 8 April 1929. The bombing was intended as a statement, not to cause harm; the duo aimed to use the trial to spread revolutionary ideology. Note: Bhagat Singh was born 26 September 1907, so at the time of the bombing he was a little over 21 years old (64th BPSC).
Lahore Conspiracy Case
(1929–30) 		Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru tried here. Imprisoned, they protested poor conditions through hunger strikes, demanding political-prisoner status. Jatin Das became the first martyr, arrested 14 June 1929 and dying on 13 September 1929 after a 63/64-day hunger strike. Chandrashekhar Azad died in a police encounter in Allahabad (February 1931). On 23 March 1931 (67th BPSC Re-exam), Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru were executed by hanging.

Other Conspiracy Cases & Revolutionary Bengal

Ghadar to Chittagong, and the women of the underground
Other revolutionary conspiracy cases
Case (Year, Place) Details
Ghadar Conspiracy
(1915, Worldwide/India)		 Led by Har Dayal, Raghubir Singh and others. Conspired to initiate an armed revolt. Many conspirators executed or imprisoned. Mobilised the Indian diaspora, underscoring the international aspect of the freedom movement.
Berar Conspiracy Case
(1916, Berar)		 R.S. Nimbkar and others involved in inciting an armed rebellion. Accused received various sentences, broadening the reach of the freedom movement.
Bombay Conspiracy Case
(1917, Bombay)		 Key figures: V.N. Chatterjee, Joseph Baptista and others. Involved sedition and incitement to warfare. Imprisonment led to increased urban discontent, highlighting the role of educated Indians in the freedom struggle.
Peshawar Conspiracy Cases
(1922–1927, Peshawar)		 Various Pashtun leaders involved in anti-British activities/conspiracies. Harsh sentences (including transportation for life) galvanised support for non-cooperation, especially in the North-West Frontier Province.
Chittagong Armoury Raid
(April 1930, Bengal)		 Organised by Surya Sen with associates Anant Singh, Ganesh Ghosh, and Lokenath Baul (60th–62nd BPSC). Aimed to show it was possible to challenge the armed might of the British Empire. Did not achieve immediate goals but became a powerful symbol of resistance, demonstrating a commitment to social justice alongside political freedom.
Murder of Michael O'Dwyer
(13 March 1940, Caxton Hall, London) (60th–62nd BPSC) 		Udham Singh (67th BPSC Re-exam) assassinated Michael O'Dwyer, former Lieutenant Governor of Punjab, at Caxton Hall in London. O'Dwyer was responsible for the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (1919), which Udham Singh had survived.

Revolutionary Activities in Bengal (1920s–1930s)

  • Revolutionary groups reorganised underground networks while some operated under Congress, gaining access to the masses.
  • Collaboration with C.R. Das strengthened the Swarajist agenda.
  • Post-Das's death ( 1925), Bengal Congress split into the J.M. Sengupta faction (backed by Anushilan) and the Subhash Bose faction (supported by Yugantar).
  • Role of Surya Sen: also a poet and admirer of Tagore and Qazi Nazrul Islam; seized Chittagong's main armouries; disrupted railway, telephone and telegraph systems, and proclaimed a provisional revolutionary government; execution of the raid was conducted by 65 members under the Indian Republican Army – Chittagong Branch; Surya Sen was arrested 16 February 1933 and hanged 12 January 1934.
  • Inclusivity: the movement abandoned Hindu rituals, facilitating Muslim participation ( Satar, Mir Ahmed, Fakir Ahmed Mian, Tunu Mian).
  • Drawbacks: retained some conservative elements and failed to develop broader socio-economic goals; the Swarajists did not support the Muslim peasantry against zamindars in Bengal.
  • Group action: targeted colonial structures rather than individuals, inspiring youth and demoralising the British bureaucracy.

Growth of Trade Unionism

Madras Labour Union to the AITUC split
Early trade unions
Union Founded Detail
Madras Labour Union 1918 Under B.P. Wadia and V. Kalyanasundaram — India's first modern labour union (UP PCS Mains 2007). Members included Buckingham and Carnatic Mills labourers. Soon after, two unions formed in Bombay, one in Calcutta (the Indian Seamen's Union), and four more in Madras.
Ahmedabad Textile Labour Association 25 February 1920 Established by Anasuya Sarabhai (became its President) and Mahatma Gandhi (IAS Pre 2009), following the 1918 Ahmedabad mill strike.
All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) 31 October 1920, Bombay Founded by N.M. Joshi. Lala Lajpat Rai — first President; Joseph Baptista — Vice President; Dewan/Diwan Chaman Lal — General Secretary (all founding members). Motive: the establishment of the International Labour Organization in 1919 prompted Indian labourers to organise as labour unions.

Notable strikes and agitations

  • Kharagpur Railway Workshops: early strike against poor working conditions and unfair wages.
  • Tata Iron and Steel Works: workers protested discriminatory wages and working conditions.
  • Bombay Textile Mills: major unrest, demanding better wages, working hours, and safe environments.
  • Milestone — May Day: the first May Day was celebrated in Madras in 1923, symbolising international worker solidarity.
Context — the 1929 AITUC split at Nagpur AITUC's first split occurred in 1929 at Nagpur, with Jawaharlal Nehru presiding at that session — the session displayed a "trial of strength" between leftists and rightists/moderates. The moderate section, under N.M. Joshi, V.V. Giri and B. Shiva Rao, broke off to form the Indian Trade Union Federation — not founded by S.A. Dange, a common MCQ trap (Uttarakhand PCS 2025).

Workers and Peasants Party (WPP)

  • Labour Swaraj Party formed in Bengal in 1925 under Congress, transformed into the Workers and Peasants Party of Bengal in 1926.
  • 1927: Workers and Peasants Party of Bombay Province formed from the Congress Labour Party (which had emerged within Congress in Bombay Province in 1926).
  • 1928: Workers and Peasants Party formed in the United Provinces, and in Punjab under the name Kirti Kisan Party.
  • December 1928: at a conference of these provincial parties held in Calcutta, the All India Workers and Peasants Party was formed as a pan-India organisation ( not in 1927, a common trap — UP PCS 2024).
  • Purpose: to work within Congress to make it a more revolution-inclined party and an organisation of the common people.
MCQ Trap · Radical Democratic Party & RCPI founders
  • M.N. Roy founded the Radical Democratic Party in 1940 ( not Somendranath Tagore or Shachindranath Sanyal) — note "Bandi Jeevan" was written by Shachindranath Sanyal, a separate fact often confused with the RDP founder.
  • Saumyandranath Tagore founded the Revolutionary Communist Party of India in 1934, breaking away from the CPI.

State People's Conference / Praja Mandal Movements (68th BPSC)

  • Emerged in the early 20th century as a significant aspect of the national movement within the princely states.
  • Following the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920, facilitated establishment of people's organisations advocating for democratic rights and addressing grievances of subjects under feudal rule.
  • Anti-Feudal and Anti-Colonial: aimed to combat both feudal princes and the British administration, demanding democratic rights for subjects of princely states.
  • Focus: the primary demand was establishment of democratic rights and governance.

Key organisations

  • Hitvardhak Sabha — founded May 1921 in Poona; addressed issues facing the people of the southern princely states.
  • Akhil Bhor Sansthan Praja Sabha — founded November 1921 by Wamanrao Patwardhan in the Bhor region; tackled local problems affecting the Bhor populace.
  • All India Association of the People's Council — the Bombay session in 1927 transformed the princely states' national movement into a nationwide cause, seeking responsible governance and rights for citizens of princely states.
  • Congress's role: INC supported the movement, endorsing its demands during the Madras Session and integrating it with the national struggle against British rule.
Context — Congress's contribution & later development The Congress Party's policies and programmes, particularly those of the Gandhian Movement, boosted the Praja Mandal movement. In Orissa, Congress leaders organised a State People's Conference in 1937 to address conditions in princely states, which was reconstituted in 1938 under H.K. Mahatab. The establishment of a Congress ministry in Orissa in July 1937 inspired further activism against despotic rulers.

Peasant Movements & the Kisan Sabha

From Bijolia (1897) to Bhoodan (1951)
Major peasant movements — chronological
Movement Period / Place Leadership & details
Bijolia Movement 1897 onward, Mewar (Rajasthan) — ~44 years, the first peasant movement of India (R.A.S./R.T.S. 1992). First phase led by Sadhu Sitaram Das (from 1913); second phase by Vijay Singh Pathik (from 1915), who organised farmers against excessive taxes imposed by the rulers. Newspapers Prayag's Abhyudaya and Calcutta's Bharat Mitra regularly published news on it (Rajasthan PCS 2024) — it is not true that Pathik went to meet Gandhi to inform him of the movement.
UP / Oudh Kisan Sabha UP Kisan Sabha: February 1918. Oudh Kisan Sabha: 1920. Workers of the Home Rule League were active in Awadh and organised peasants. UP Kisan Sabha formed by Indra Narayan Dwivedi and Gauri Shankar Mishra, with support of Madan Mohan Malaviya ( not Jawaharlal Nehru — IAS 2005). A huge number of farmers from UP participated in the 1918 Delhi Session of Congress. End of 1919: 'Nai-Dhobi Bandh' social boycott started in the Pratapgarh district of Oudh against exploiting landlords (39th BPSC 1994) — led initially by Jhinguri Singh and Durgapal (Singh); Baba Ramchandra then emerged (mid-1920) as a peasants' leader in Awadh/Oudh, forming the Oudh Kisan Sabha in 1920. Baba Ramchandra was a Sanyasi who had earlier been an indentured labourer in Fiji, carrying a copy of the Tulsidas Ramayana to recite verses to rural audiences (68th BPSC 2022).
Eka Movement 1921–22, Hardoi, Barabanki, Bahraich, Sitapur (UP). Grassroots leadership by Madari Pasi (lower caste) and other low-caste leaders and small Zamindars. Issues: rent 50% higher than recorded rates; oppression by Thikedars collecting revenue; practice of share-rents. Aim: transformation of rent into cash (39th BPSC 1994) — peasants did not refuse to pay rent, but insisted on paying in cash due to high inflation. Symbolic ritual vows: pay only recorded rent, but on time; not leave when evicted; refuse forced labour; give no help to criminals; abide by Panchayat decisions. Ended by March 1922 due to severe government repression.
Bardoli Satyagraha 1928, Bardoli taluka, Surat district (Gujarat). Led by Vallabhbhai Patel (sustained campaign since 1922 had been run by Gandhi's followers, the Mehta brothers). With its success, the women of Bardoli gave Patel the title 'Sardar' (on behalf of the women, conferred by Gandhi).
Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha (BPKS) / AIKS BPKS: 1929. All India Kisan Congress: 11 April 1936, Lucknow. Founded by Swami Sahajanand Saraswati (also called 'Kisan-Pran'); programme aimed at eradication of the Zamindari system and granting ownership rights to farmers. Sahajanand became first President of the All India Kisan Congress; N.G. Ranga first General Secretary. Later renamed the All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS); held its session alongside the Congress session at Faizpur, chaired by N.G. Ranga. Sahajanand helped by Karyanand Sharma, Rahul Sankrityayan, Panchanan Sharma, Yadunandan Sharma and other communist leaders at village level.
Tebhaga Movement September–November 1946, North Bengal. Bengal Provincial Kisan Council decided in September 1946 to launch it for the harvest season of November 1946. Sharecroppers ( bargadars) demanded the Zamindars' share be reduced from one-half to one-third of produce, with two-thirds going to the sharecropper — implementing the Flood Commission's recommendation; the Bargadari Act was passed as a result. Led by the Kisan Sabha.
Bhoodan Movement 18 April 1951, Pochampalli / Bhudan Pochampally (now Yadadri Bhuvanagiri district, Telangana — carved from Nalgonda). Started by Acharya Vinoba Bhave in relation to a Sarvodaya conference; the district was then a centre of communist activities. Zamindar Ramachandra Reddy donated 100 acres of land to 40 Harijan families. In October 1951, Vinoba set a target of 50 million acres by 1957; the movement continued beyond 1957 but began to wane later. Jaya Prakash Narayan left active politics and joined the Bhoodan Movement in 1954. Bhoodan was most successful in Orissa.

Sahajanand Saraswati — publications & associations

  • Published "Lok Sangraha", a newspaper highlighting peasant hardships (70th BPSC 2024).
  • Ran the Hindi weekly "Hunkar" from Patna (63rd BPSC 2017) — Sahajanand (1889–1950) was a Sanyasi, prominent farmers'-rights activist, Indian freedom fighter, and forceful writer.
  • Just before his death, formed the All India United Kisan Sabha with the demand for 'nationalization of land and waterways' and all sources of energy/wealth (56th–59th BPSC 2015).
  • Jawaharlal Nehru addressed and participated in the first meeting of the AIKS (April 1936, Lucknow), where Sahajanand was chosen President and N.G. Ranga secretary (UP PCS Mains 2017).
  • Rajendra Prasad was associated with the Kisan Movement in Bihar (44th BPSC 2000) — he later became President of the Constituent Assembly and independent India's first President.
MCQ Trap · easily mixed-up Kisan Sabha facts
  • First All India Kisan Maha Sabha was formed at Lucknow, not Allahabad, Kolkata or Patna.
  • The Akhil Bhartiya Kisan Congress was founded in 1936, not 1939/1942/1945.
  • General Secretary of the All India Kisan Congress: N.G. Ranga — not E.M.S. Namboodiripad or Acharya Narendra Dev, both common distractors.
  • Tebhaga demanded reducing the landlord's share from one-half to one-third — not full ownership transfer, abolition of zamindari, or debt-writing-off (those are separate, unrelated demands from other movements).
  • Bhoodan Movement began in Andhra Pradesh (present-day Telangana) — the specific starting village is Pochampalli, not Udaygiri, Raipur, or Venkatagiri.
Context — Vinoba Bhave's own trajectory Vinoba Bhave was one of the great spiritual leaders and reformers of Modern India. He was the first person to be elected for Individual Satyagraha in 1940. After Independence, he organised the Sarvodaya Samaj to bring about non-violent social transformation through land reforms, persuading large landlords to gift at least one-sixth of their land — leading into the Bhoodan Movement, whose main objective was to distribute land to landless farmers.
