Part A
Establishment, Composition & Appointment

Origin of the Court, strength, qualifications, appointment, collegium and special categories of judges.

3

Origin, Establishment & Constitutional Basis

Integrated Judiciary & Establishment

  • An integrated judicial system has been set up under the Indian Constitution, with the Supreme Court at the top and the various High Courts below it.
  • This single system of judiciary was adopted from the Government of India Act, 1935.
  • The Supreme Court succeeded the Federal Court of India, which had been established under the Government of India Act, 1935.
  • The Supreme Court was inaugurated on 28 January 1950.
  • It was established by the Indian Constitution itself — not by an Act of Parliament, and not by the Indian Independence Act 1947.
  • Constitutional location
    • Articles 124 to 147 in Part V of the Constitution deal with the Supreme Court.
    • It was established under Part V, Chapter IV, Article 124.
    • The Supreme Court is the highest judicial forum and the final Court of Appeal.
    • Establishment, composition and jurisdiction are all laid down in Arts 124–147.
MCQ TrapOptions "Act of Parliament in 1950", "Indian Independence Act 1947" and "Indian Government Act 1953" are all wrong (no Indian Government Act of 1953 exists); the Court is a constitutional body, and the year-date options 27, 29 and 30 January are wrong.

Pre-Constitution Background — Sapru Committee & the Ad hoc Committee on the Supreme Court

  • The provisions on the appointment of Supreme Court and High Court Judges were incorporated on the basis of recommendations published by the Sapru Committee (1945).
  • The committee was led by Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru.
  • It was appointed by the Non-Party Conference in November 1944.
  • It was formed to address issues relating to minorities in India; its report contained 21 recommendations on constitutional questions.
  • Constituent Assembly's Ad hoc Committee on the Supreme Court
    • The Constituent Assembly appointed a separate Ad hoc Committee on the Supreme Court for recommendations on the judiciary.
    • Members: S. Varadachariar (former Judge of the Federal Court), Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, B.L. Mitter and K.M. Munshi (eminent advocates), and B.N. Rau (Constitutional Adviser and former high-ranking judicial officer).
    • Its report was submitted on 21 May 1947; it addressed the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and proposed two alternative methods for appointment of its judges.

Strength of the Court — Number of Judges

Constitutional Position

  • Article 124(1): there shall be a Supreme Court consisting of a Chief Justice of India and, until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number, not more than 33 other Judges (originally 7).
  • Present strength = 34 judges including the CJI (33 + 1).
  • Earlier the strength was 30 + CJI = 31; the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019 raised it from 31 (30+1) to 34 (33+1).
  • The power to increase the number of Judges is vested in Parliament — not the President, the CJI or the Law Commission.
  • The original Constitution of 1950 envisaged a CJI and 7 puisne judges, leaving it to Parliament to increase the number.
  • A February 2008 Cabinet decision to enlarge the Court meant a strength of the CJI plus 30 other Judges — realised through the 2009 Amendment Act that took the Court from 26 to 31 including the CJI.
  • In the early years all the Judges sat together to hear cases; as work grew and arrears accumulated, Parliament raised the number repeatedly.
  • There is no provision for additional judges in the Supreme Court.

Qualifications & Appointment of Judges

Who Appoints & On What Consultation

  • Every Judge of the Supreme Court is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal (Art 124(2)) — not by the PM, the CJI or the Ombudsman.
  • The President acts after consultation with such Judges of the Supreme Court and of the High Courts as he deems necessary.
  • In the case of a Judge other than the Chief Justice, the CJI must always be consulted.
  • The Chief Justice is appointed after consultation with the other Judges of the Supreme Court and the Judges of the High Courts.
  • Consultation with the CJI curtails the absolute discretion of the Executive and ensures judicial appointments are not made on political or practical considerations — one safeguard of the Court's autonomy.
  • Consultation with the CJI was made obligatory by the 1993 decision of the Supreme Court (see the Collegium chapter).
  • Judges are not appointed on the recommendation of Rajya Sabha, the advice of Lok Sabha, or the advice of the PM.
  • Qualifications — Art 124(3)
    • Must be a citizen of India.
    • Has been a Judge of a High Court (or two or more such courts in succession) for at least 5 years; or
    • Has been an advocate of a High Court (or two or more in succession) for at least 10 years; or
    • Is, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist.
    • No minimum age is prescribed for appointment.

Collegium System & NJAC

Collegium System

  • The Collegium is the system of appointment and transfer of judges that has evolved through judgments of the Supreme Court — it is not created by an Act of Parliament or by a provision of the Constitution; Parliament did not establish it.
  • It arose because the Constitution requires consultation with the CJI before any judicial appointment.
  • It was adopted by the Supreme Court in 1993; its genesis lies in the three Judges Cases.
  • In the 1993 case the Court ruled that "consultation" means concurrence, binding the President to the CJI's consultation.
  • Initially the collegium was the CJI + two senior-most judges of the Supreme Court.
  • In 1998 the Court clarified that consultation with the CJI means consultation of the collegium, comprising the CJI and four other senior-most Judges.
  • Present composition: a five-member body headed by the CJI with four senior-most Judges.
  • It decides elevation of Judges to the Supreme Court and the elevation of High Court Judges to Chief Justice; when there is disagreement the majority opinion prevails.
  • Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts are appointed through the collegium system.
  • [Outside source] The three cases: First Judges Case (S.P. Gupta, 1981), Second Judges Case (SC Advocates-on-Record Association, 1993) and the Third Judges Case (1998 Presidential Reference under Art 143) — the 1998 expansion to five judges came through a reference on the process of appointment and transfer of judges.
MCQ TrapThe claim that the collegium was introduced in 1993 by Justice P.N. Bhagwati in the First Judges Case is false — the 1993 ruling is the Second Judges Case. Also "evolved by Parliament by law" is false.

National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)

  • Introduced by the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 to replace the Collegium System for appointing judges to the Supreme Court and High Courts.
  • Struck down by the Supreme Court in 2015 as unconstitutional; currently India has no NJAC and the Collegium continues.

Ad hoc, Acting & Retired Judges

Acting Chief Justice — Art 126

  • The President appoints an Acting Chief Justice when the office of CJI is vacant or the CJI is unable to discharge his duties.
  • Not appointed by the CJI, the PM, the Law Minister, or the CJI with the President's consent.
  • There is no provision for additional judges in the Supreme Court.

Ad hoc Judges — Art 127

  • Ad hoc judges can be appointed in the Supreme Court only — not in High Courts or District/Sessions Courts (and additional judges cannot be).
  • Purpose: when there is no quorum of Judges to hold or continue a session of the Court — not for leave, lack of permanent appointees or case pendency.
  • The CJI, with the previous consent of the President and after consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court concerned, requests in writing the attendance of a High Court Judge duly qualified for Supreme Court appointment, designated by the CJI, for as long as necessary.

Attendance of Retired Judges — Art 128

  • The CJI may at any time, with the previous consent of the President, request a person who has held office as a Supreme Court Judge, a Federal Court Judge, or a High Court Judge duly qualified for Supreme Court appointment, to sit and act as a Supreme Court Judge.
  • This is why a retired Supreme Court judge can be called back to sit and act by the CJI with the President's permission.
Part B
Tenure, Service Conditions & Independence

Oath, retirement, resignation, removal, salary, post-retirement bar and safeguards of judicial autonomy.

8

Oath, Tenure, Resignation & Retirement

Oath & Tenure

  • A person appointed as a Supreme Court Judge makes and subscribes an oath or affirmation before the President.
  • The Third Schedule contains the forms of oaths for Union and State Ministers, MPs, State Legislators, Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, and the CAG — 10 forms in all.
  • The form beginning "true faith and allegiance to the Constitution ... uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India ... perform the duties of my office" is prescribed for the Chief Justice of India (and the CAG); not the President, an MP or the Governor.
  • A Judge holds office until the age of 65 years (Art 124(2)) — the High Court retirement age is 62 years; options 60, 62, 63, 64 and 70 are wrong.

Resignation

  • A Judge may resign by writing under his hand addressed to the President (proviso (a) to Art 124(2)).
  • Not addressed to the PM, the Law Minister, the Attorney General, the CJI or the senior-most Judge.
9

Removal of Judges

Procedure under Art 124(4)

  • A Judge cannot be removed except by an order of the President passed after an address by each House of Parliament.
  • The address must be supported by a majority of the total membership of that House and a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • It must be presented to the President in the same session.
  • Grounds: proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • Judges cannot be removed by the will of the CJI, by the President alone, or by the President on the CJI's recommendation; nor does a CBI enquiry, a CJI enquiry or a Bar Council report remove a Judge.
  • Though an impeachment-like process, the word "impeachment" is used in the Constitution only for removing the President; for Supreme Court and High Court Judges the word used is "remove".
  • The Constitution gives the grounds but does not define "proved misbehaviour" or "incapacity".
  • Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968
    • The details of the process of removal are given in the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968.
    • The Speaker of the Lok Sabha can reject a motion to remove a Judge under this Act.
    • [Outside source] A notice is signed by 100 Lok Sabha / 50 Rajya Sabha members and, if admitted, a three-member committee inquires.
MCQ TrapThe theory line saying removals "can be declared unconstitutional and illegal in the state of being extraordinary" is garbled in the source; its exact meaning is unclear and it is not elaborated further here.

Salary, Expenses, Post-Retirement Bar & Independence

Salary & Administrative Expenses

  • Salaries and allowances are determined by Parliament by law (Art 125(1)); until then, as specified in the Second Schedule. Not fixed by a Pay Commission, Law Commission or Council of Ministers.
  • Salaries of Judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India — the Legislature does not vote on them.
  • All administrative expenses of the Supreme Court, including salaries, allowances and pensions of its officers and servants, are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India; a High Court's expenses are charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State.
  • The CJI appoints the officers and servants of the Supreme Court without executive interference and can prescribe their conditions of service.

Post-Retirement Bar

  • Art 124(7): no person who has held office as a Supreme Court Judge shall plead or act in any court or before any authority within India.
  • A former Judge is therefore debarred from practising in any court of law or before any other authority.
  • Art 220: a permanent High Court Judge after commencement of the Constitution may not plead or act in any court or authority in India except the Supreme Court and the other High Courts.

Safeguards of the Court's Autonomy

  • Consultation of the CJI in appointments; removal only by special majority of Parliament; salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund; the CJI's control over staff appointments.
  • The Judges cannot be removed by the CJI alone, and the Government alone does not appoint staff.
  • Parliament cannot enlarge jurisdiction beyond what the Constitution provides (see Art 138, Union List only).
Part C
Jurisdiction & Powers

Seat, benches, court of record, original, appellate, advisory, writ and special powers.

11

Seat, Language, Benches & Constitution Bench

Seat and Language

  • The Court sits in New Delhi, but may sit elsewhere as the CJI, with the approval of the President, appoints (Art 130).
  • All proceedings in the Supreme Court and every High Court are in English — Art 348(1)(a), which also covers the language of Acts, Bills etc.

Benches

  • A bench hearing a case on the interpretation of the Constitution (a substantial question of law) must have at least 5 Judges — the Constitution Bench; options 7, 9, 10, 11 and 13 are wrong.
  • Benches generally have an odd number of Judges (3, 5, 7, 9, 11) to avoid a clash of votes or views.
  • The largest bench (13 judges) sat in Keshavananda Bharati (1973); the second largest (11 judges) in Golaknath v State of Punjab (1967).
MCQ Trap[Outside source] Golaknath, the Bank Nationalisation case and T.M.A. Pai were all 11-judge benches; the source key accepts Golaknath as the answer to "second largest bench".

Court of Record & Contempt

Court of Record

  • Art 129: the Supreme Court is a Court of Record with all the powers of such a court, including the power to punish for contempt of itself.
  • Meaning: its proceedings and judgments are written and enrolled for perpetual memory, available as evidence of fact, with evidentiary value, and cannot be questioned in any court.
  • Art 215 makes High Courts courts of record too — so the Supreme Court and High Courts are both courts of record, not District Courts. A High Court as a court of record can review and correct its own judgments, as the Supreme Court can.
  • Art 124 deals with the establishment and constitution of the Court, not court of record status.
MCQ TrapOptions "decisions have evidentiary value" and "power to punish for contempt" are both correct; the source flags that the state commission chose the evidentiary-value option.

Contempt of Court

  • The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 followed the H.N. Sanyal Committee (1961).
  • The Constitution empowers the Supreme Court and High Courts to punish for contempt of themselves but does not define civil or criminal contempt — they are defined in the Act.
  • Parliament has the power to make laws on contempt of court.
  • Sec 2(b) of the Act: wilful disobedience of a judgment, decree, direction, order or writ is civil contempt.
  • In M.L. Sachdev v Union of India (an Art 32 case) wilful non-compliance with court orders was held to be contempt; the party was held guilty.
13

Original Jurisdiction — Art 131

Disputes Covered

  • The Supreme Court has exclusive original jurisdiction in the Centre–State and inter-State disputes.
  • Covered: dispute (a) between the Government of India and one or more States; (b) between the Government of India and one or more States on one side and one or more States on the other; (c) between two or more States.
  • Applies only if the dispute involves a question (of law or fact) on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.
  • Proviso: it does not extend to a dispute arising out of a pre-Constitution treaty, agreement, covenant, engagement or similar instrument that continues in operation, or which provides that this jurisdiction shall not extend.
  • As a federal court the Supreme Court decides disputes between units of the federation.
  • Not covered: election disputes of Parliament / State Legislature, and disputes between the Government of India and a Union Territory.

Three Jurisdictions of the Court

  • It has original, appellate and advisory jurisdiction: Art 131; Arts 132–136; Art 143.
  • Writ power to protect Fundamental Rights (Art 32) is not exclusive to it (see Writ chapter).
14

Appellate Jurisdiction — Arts 132–136

Heads of Appeal

  • Cases on the interpretation of the Constitution reach the Court under its appellate jurisdiction (not original or advisory).
  • Art 132: appeal from High Courts against any judgment, decree or final order in civil, criminal or other proceedings involving a substantial question of law on interpretation of the Constitution.
  • Art 133: civil matters (whether or not constitutional questions).
  • Art 134: criminal matters (whether or not constitutional questions).
  • Art 134A: certificate for appeal; in constitutional cases Art 132 read with Art 134A creates the appellate jurisdiction.
  • Art 136: special leave to appeal.

Special Leave — Art 136

  • Under this extraordinary power the Court can quash the decision of a quasi-judicial tribunal that exceeded its jurisdiction or approached the question in a manner likely to result in injustice.
  • The Supreme Court is the highest Court of Appeal of all courts.

Tribunals & Court Martial

  • The Court hears appeals from any High Court or Tribunal including Court Martial, since the Armed Forces Tribunal Act, 2007 allows appeal to the Supreme Court.
  • TDSAT (Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal) was set up in 2000 by amending the TRAI Act, 1997; for telecom, broadcasting and airport tariff matters appeals go to the Supreme Court only on substantial questions of law; for cyber issues, to the High Court.
15

Advisory Jurisdiction — Art 143

Scope

  • Art 143: the President may seek the Court's opinion on a question of law or fact of public importance, or on a dispute arising out of a pre-Constitution treaty, agreement, covenant or similar instrument.
  • In the first case the Court may refuse; in the second it is bound to give its opinion.
  • Only the President can refer — not the PM or a High Court; the Court cannot advise suo motu, only on Fundamental Rights, or to the Government in general.
  • Hearing is by a bench of at least 5 Judges and the advice is not binding on the Government.
  • Also used for the question of the process of appointment and transfer of Judges.

2002 Gujarat Reference

  • In August 2002 President A.P.J. Abdul Kalam sought the Court's opinion under Art 143 on the dispute between the Election Commission and the Government over elections in Gujarat.
  • Issues: the limits on the EC's powers under Art 324, the impact of Art 174 on its jurisdiction, and whether the EC could recommend President's Rule in a State.
16

Writ Jurisdiction — Supreme Court vs High Courts

Arts 32, 226 & 227

  • Art 32: Right to Constitutional Remedies — the Supreme Court can issue directions, orders or writs to enforce Fundamental Rights (Part III).
  • The High Courts (under Art 226) can also issue writs; both courts share jurisdiction over enforcement of FRs.
  • Art 226 is broader: High Courts can issue writs for FRs, other constitutional rights and other purposes.
  • Art 227: High Court superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territory.
  • The Supreme Court therefore has no exclusive jurisdiction over the validity of Central laws.
17

Review, Curative Petition & Special Powers

Powers

  • Art 137: the Supreme Court (not the President, the Cabinet or the Law Ministry) can review and change its own judgment or order, subject to Parliamentary law or rules under Art 145.
  • Curative Petition: evolved in Rupa Ashok Hurra v Ashok Hurra (2002); filed under Art 142.
  • Art 142: the Court may pass any decree or order necessary for complete justice, enforceable across India as prescribed by Parliament's law, or by Presidential order until then; ordinary law limits do not restrict it.
  • Art 138: Parliament can enlarge the jurisdiction on Union List matters by making a law, not by resolution or Presidential order.
  • Art 139A: transfer of cases — where the same questions of law are pending before the SC and a High Court or two High Courts, the SC may withdraw and decide them itself (suo motu or on an application from the Attorney General or a party).
  • Art 141: its decision binds all courts in India.
Part D
Judicial Review, Basic Structure & Guardianship

The power to test laws against the Constitution and the doctrine that limits even constitutional amendments.

18

Judicial Review

Meaning & Basis

  • Judicial Review = the power of the Judiciary to pronounce on the constitutionality of laws and executive orders and to declare any law or order of the Legislature or Executive void if it conflicts with the Constitution — not the power to question the wisdom of laws, review Bills before assent, or review its own judgments.
  • It originated and developed in the USA, first propounded in Marbury v Madison (1803) by Chief Justice John Marshall.
  • Art 13 provides for it: 13(1) — pre-Constitution laws inconsistent with Part III are void to the extent of the inconsistency; 13(2) — the State shall not make a law abridging Part III rights, and a contravening law is void to that extent.
  • The Constitution establishes it through Arts 13, 32, 136, 137, 226 and 227.
  • Both the Supreme Court and the High Courts enjoy the power; the Supreme Court declared it a part of the basic structure.
  • India's judicial review is based on procedure established by law, not US-style "due process" — and the concept rests on the supremacy of the Constitution.
  • Judicial review exists in India and USA; in the UK Parliament is supreme and its laws cannot be challenged in court.
  • Its scope is limited in India — the Court reviews legislation especially if it affects the basic structure, has defined sovereign power as divided between the three branches, and the huge size of the Constitution further limits it; the "bag of borrowings" remark is true but does not explain the limit.
  • Amendments & the High Courts
    • The 42nd Amendment (1976) curtailed High Court review by barring them from deciding the validity of Central laws; the 43rd Amendment (1977) restored it.
    • A High Court can invalidate a Central law; and a constitutional amendment against the basic structure can be questioned by the Supreme Court.
    • An amendment removing Equality before law from Part III would be unconstitutional (Art 13(2)).

Due Process vs Procedure Established by Law

  • Due Process of Law in essence = the principle of natural justice — fairness, reasonableness, equity and equality, free from arbitrariness.
  • Two principles: Nemo judex in causa sua (no one a judge in his own cause — rule against bias) and Audi alteram partem (no one condemned unheard).
  • Natural justice is grounded in Arts 14 and 21; the Court recognised due process in Maneka Gandhi to remove arbitrariness.
  • Judicial process in India rests on procedure established by law, the Constitution, conventions and routine due process; Art 14 — equality before law (British) and equal protection of laws (American).
19

Doctrine of Basic Structure

Origin & Content

  • Propounded in the Keshavananda Bharati v State of Kerala case (1973) by a 13-judge bench by a 7:6 majority — Parliament may amend (Art 368) only so far as it does not "damage or destroy" the basic structure.
  • The Court may abrogate, change or alter nothing of the basic framework; amendments are subject to judicial review — a substantive non-legislative check on Parliament.
  • Its source is judicial interpretation; the term is not in the Constitution (the Constitution does not define it in terms of federalism, secularism etc.).
  • Later approved in Minerva Mills (1980).
  • Articles declared basic structure: Arts 32, 226 and 227 — judicial review under Art 32 (SC) and Art 226 (HC) is integral, and 227 gives HC superintendence; Art 245 is not.
MCQ TrapOther case options (Golaknath, Gopalan, Maneka Gandhi, Indira Sawhney, Shankari Prasad, Rudal Shah) are not the source of the doctrine.
20

Guardian, Custodian & Final Interpreter

Role

  • The Supreme Court is the last authority to interpret the Constitution — the Guardian / Custodian of the Constitution (not the President, Parliament, Council of Ministers or Lok Sabha Secretariat).
  • It is a federal court, protector of human rights, and final interpreter — all true.
  • The Constitution is the supreme law and a living document, laying down the framework of institutions and the Fundamental Rights, DPSP and Duties.

Public Interest Litigation & Judicial Activism

Public Interest Litigation (PIL)

  • PIL = Public Interest Litigation; it is a judicially developed process (judicial initiative, not Act or amendment).
  • Concept originated in the USA: since the 19th century the legal-aid movement fed public interest law; first legal aid office in New York, 1876.
  • Before the 1980s only the aggrieved party could approach courts; after the Emergency the Court devised a way for any member of public or NGO to approach it.
  • Justices P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer were among the first to admit PILs; Krishna Iyer (1915–2014) was a Supreme Court judge, not CJI.
  • Aligned with Art 39A; can be filed in High Courts and the Supreme Court.
  • Expanded locus standi so socially and economically weaker sections have access to justice; any citizen can go to court for the welfare of another person or group.
  • PIL strengthens but is not indispensable for the rule of law, which rests on the basic structure, Art 14, Art 32, Art 226 and judicial review.
  • First PIL: Hussainara Khatoon v State of Bihar (1979) on a petition by Kapila Hingorani, accepted by Bhagwati; the CJI at the time was Y.V. Chandrachud; Bhagwati became the 17th CJI (12 July 1985 – 20 Dec 1986).

Judicial Activism

  • Related to PIL; began in the mid-1970s on the Supreme Court's initiative.
  • Justice A.S. Anand: judicial activism should not become "judicial adventurism"; activism arose from failure of Executive and Legislature, adventurism being an extreme form.

3

Constitutional Basis & Establishment

Constitutional Position

  • The High Court occupies the top position in the judicial administration of the State.
  • Articles 214 to 231 in Part VI (Chapter V) deal with the organisation, independence, jurisdiction, powers and procedures of High Courts.
  • Art 214: every State will have a High Court. Art 215: every High Court is a Court of Record.
  • Art 216: a High Court consists of a Chief Justice and such other Judges as the President may from time to time deem necessary to appoint.
  • The 7th Constitution Amendment Act, 1956 authorised Parliament to establish a common High Court for two or more States, or two or more States and a Union Territory.
  • Parliament can also extend the jurisdiction of a High Court.
  • High Courts are instituted as Constitutional Courts.

Court of Record

  • Judgments, proceedings and acts are recorded for perpetual memory and testimony; the records have evidentiary value and cannot be questioned before any subordinate court.
  • They are recognised as legal precedents and references, and the court can punish for contempt of itself.

Origin

  • The institution originated in India in 1862, with High Courts set up at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras.
  • In 1866 the High Court of Judicature for the North-Western Provinces came into existence at Agra; its seat shifted to Allahabad in 1869.
4

Number & Territorial Jurisdiction of High Courts

Number

  • At present there are 25 High Courts, 7 of which have jurisdiction over more than one State / Union Territory.
  • Delhi has its own High Court; Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh share a common High Court; Delhi is the only UT with a separate High Court (J&K and Ladakh share one).
  • Other Union Territories fall under State High Courts.
  • Punjab, Haryana and Chandigarh have a common High Court.
  • Three High Courts cover more than one State (excluding UTs): Gauhati (Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram), Bombay (Maharashtra, Goa) and Punjab & Haryana.
  • Bombay also covers the UTs of Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu.

Appointment, Transfer, Acting & Additional Judges

Appointment

  • High Court Judges are appointed by the President by warrant under hand and seal (Art 217(1)) — not the Governor.
  • The President consults the CJI, the Governor of the State and, for Judges other than the Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of the High Court.
  • Number of Judges is decided by the President — Art 216; the Judges appointed shall not exceed the maximum number he fixes by order.
  • Qualifications: citizen of India; has held judicial office in India for at least 10 years, or has been an advocate of a High Court(s) for at least 10 years.
  • Law Commission (8th) chairman H.R. Khanna, in its 80th report ("The Method of Appointment of Judges"), recommended that at least one-third of High Court Judges come from other States.

Transfer — Art 222

  • The President may transfer a Judge from one High Court to another after consulting the CJI.
  • In the Third Judges Case (1998) the Court held that the CJI must also consult the collegium of the four senior-most SC judges and the Chief Justices of both High Courts — the CJI's sole opinion is not consultation.

Acting Chief Justice, Additional & Acting Judges

  • Art 223: the President can appoint a High Court Judge as Acting Chief Justice when the office is vacant, or the Chief Justice is temporarily absent or unable to perform duties.
  • Art 224(1): Additional Judges for a period up to 2 years on temporary increase in business or arrears.
  • Art 224(2): an acting Judge may sit when a Judge (other than the Chief Justice) is absent or unable, or is acting as Chief Justice, until the permanent Judge resumes.
  • Art 224(3): no additional or acting Judge holds office beyond 62 years.
  • Additional and acting Judges exist in the High Court only (not the Supreme Court or District Courts).

Tenure, Resignation, Removal & Salary

Tenure & Retirement

  • A Judge holds office until the age of 62 years (maximum age 62; options 58, 60, 65 wrong) — fixed under Art 217(1) by the 15th Amendment, 1963; earlier it was 60.
  • The 114th Amendment Bill (2010) proposing to raise it from 62 to 65 could not pass.
  • Resignation: in writing to the President (Art 217(1)(a)) — not the CJI, the High Court Chief Justice or the Governor.

Removal

  • The President removes a Judge on an address by Parliament presented in the same session, passed by a special majority (majority of total membership and two-thirds present and voting in each House).
  • Grounds: proved misbehaviour or incapacity; the mode is the same as for an SC Judge — Art 217(1)(b) read with Art 124(4).
  • Justice Soumitra Sen (Calcutta HC) was charged with misappropriation of funds; the Rajya Sabha passed the motion in 2011; the Lok Sabha was to take it up on 5 Sept 2011 but he resigned on 1 Sept 2011.

Salary, Pension & Post-Retirement Bar

  • Salaries and allowances: charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State (Art 202(3)(d)); pension: charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (Art 112(3)(d)).
  • Art 220: a retired permanent Judge cannot plead or act in any court or before any authority except the Supreme Court and other High Courts — so the claim that he cannot plead anywhere is wrong.

Jurisdiction & Powers

Court of Record, administrative role and writ jurisdiction.

7

Jurisdiction, Contempt & Administrative Role

Jurisdictions

  • High Courts have original, appellate and supervisory jurisdiction; advisory jurisdiction belongs to the Supreme Court (Art 143), not the High Court.
  • Art 227: superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territory, except those relating to the Armed Forces.
  • Art 235: control over all subordinate courts in the State.
  • As a court of record, it can also review and correct its own judgments.

Chief Justice

  • The Chief Justice acts in a dual capacity — judicial and administrative; administratively he is subject to the writ jurisdiction of any other Judge of the High Court, not the CJI, Governor or Chief Minister.
  • He controls, observes, superintends and administers the lower courts.

Contempt & Activism

  • Wilful disobedience of court orders and derogatory language about judicial behaviour amount to contempt of court; the argument that activism needs punitive powers is true but not the explanation.
8

Writ Jurisdiction — Art 226

Scope

  • Art 226: High Courts can issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose (constitutional rights, statutory / legal rights).
  • The Supreme Court under Art 32 can issue writs only for Part III; so High Courts are in a better position to protect citizens' rights.
  • Five writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto.

District Judges & Subordinate Courts

District Judges

  • Art 233: appointment, posting and promotion of District Judges are made by the Governor in consultation with the High Court.
  • The District Judge is the highest judicial authority in the district, with judicial and administrative powers and supervisory powers over subordinate courts.
  • Art 236(a): includes judges of city civil court, additional, joint and assistant district judges, chief judge of small cause court, chief presidency magistrate, additional chief presidency magistrate, sessions judges, additional and assistant sessions judges — but not tribunal judges.
11

Advocates, Prisons & Custody

Advocates

  • Advocates Act, 1961: creates a single class of legal practitioners called advocates; establishes the Bar Council of India and State Bar Councils; the BCI lays down rules for legal education and recognition of law colleges.
MCQ TrapSource explanation says corporate lawyers and patent attorneys are recognised as advocates; hence the claim that they are excluded is wrong.

Prisons & Custody

  • Prisons are a State subject (Entry 4, State List); governed by the Prisons Act, 1894; the MHA has finalised a Model Prisons Act, 2023.
  • Police custody is in a lock-up; judicial custody is in jail under the magistrate; interrogation requires court approval (Gian Singh v State, 1981).
  • Preventive detention generally lasts a maximum of 3 months, longer under certain conditions.
12

Lok Adalat, Gram Nyayalaya & Mobile Courts

Lok Adalat

  • An alternative dispute resolution forum for pending or pre-litigation cases, civil (including family) and compoundable criminal cases; consent of both parties is required; presided by a sitting or retired judicial officer with usually a lawyer and a social worker.

Gram Nyayalaya

  • Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008: access to justice at doorstep; civil and criminal jurisdiction (Sec 11); panel of village-level social workers prepared by the district court (Sec 27).

Mobile Courts

  • Conceived by A.P.J. Abdul Kalam; the first mobile court was set up in August 2007 in Mewat, Haryana, inaugurated by CJI K.G. Balakrishnan.
