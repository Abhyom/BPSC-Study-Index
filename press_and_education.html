Company-Era Educational Initiatives (Before 1813)

Minimal interest, individual initiatives

The East India Company, focused on trade, showed minimal interest in education until 1813 — what little existed came from individual officers acting on personal scholarly or political motives.

2

Charter Act of 1813 & the Orientalist-Anglicist Controversy

  • The Charter Act, 1813 incorporated the principle of promoting knowledge of modern sciences, allocating ₹1 lakh annually for education — the first-ever statutory grant for Indian education (U.P.P.C.S. Mains 2009) — though actual implementation was delayed until 1823.
  • 1817: Raja Rammohan Roy influenced funding for Hindu College, Calcutta (founded with David Hare) — one of the first institutions to introduce Western education in India (I.A.S. 2009). The government separately established three Sanskrit colleges — Calcutta, Delhi, Agra.
  • 1823: a General Committee of Public Instruction was appointed to administer the ₹1-lakh grant — 10 members, evenly split (5 Anglicists, 5 Orientalists), which is why the medium-of-instruction dispute dragged on for 12 years without resolution by vote (I.A.S. 2018).

The Orientalist–Anglicist Controversy

  • Orientalists supported traditional Indian learning (Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic); Anglicists favoured Western-focused education; a secondary split among Anglicists themselves over medium — English vs. vernacular.
  • This 12-year deadlock was only broken by Lord Macaulay's Minute (Ch.3).
3

Lord Macaulay's Minute (1835) & the Downward Filtration Theory

  • Issued 2 February 1835 (70th BPSC Re-exam) — Lord William Bentinck had appointed Macaulay (Law Member of the Governor-General's Executive Council) to head the Committee of Public Instruction and resolve the medium dispute.
  • Macaulay's core claim: "Indian learning was inferior to European learning" — famously: "a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole literature of India and Arabia."
  • Settled the dispute in favour of Anglicists: English became the medium in schools/colleges; mass education and Sanskrit/Arabic study were deliberately neglected in favour of Western sciences/literature.
  • Bentinck accepted Macaulay's proposal, and on 7 March 1835, a government resolution declared: "the great object of the British Government ought to be the promotion of European literature and science amongst the natives of India... all funds appropriated for education would be best employed on English education alone."
  • This is regarded as the point at which English officially became India's education medium (I.A.S. 1993) — the foundation of the modern educational system in India, more so than the 1813 Charter Act, Wood's Despatch (1854), or the Hunter Commission (1882), all distractor options for this specific "foundation" question.

The Downward Filtration Theory

  • Plan: educate a small upper-middle-class section who would act as interpreters, becoming "Indian in blood and colour but English in tastes, in opinions, in morals and in intellect."
  • Premise: education would "trickle down" from this educated elite to the masses over time — hence "Downward Filtration."
  • This theory later shaped subsequent policy debate, notably informing critiques within the Hunter Commission era (Ch.6).

4

Charles Wood's Despatch (1854) — "Magna Carta of English Education"

  • Issued 19 July 1854 by Sir Charles Wood, President of the Board of Control; Governor-General at the time: Lord Dalhousie (69th BPSC Prelims) — the first comprehensive plan for the spread of education in India.
  • Key recommendations: emphasised European sciences/arts/philosophy/literature as core curriculum (liberal education); English as the primary medium for higher education, while stressing vernacular languages for reaching the masses (I.A.S. 2018 & 69th BPSC both confirm this dual emphasis — English for higher studies, vernacular/female education/vocational training also stressed, not "English at all levels," a common overreach trap).
  • Proposed: primary schools in villages, Anglo-Vernacular high schools at district level, affiliated colleges; three universities at Calcutta, Madras, Bombay, modelled on the University of London; a Grants-in-Aid system to support private educational initiatives, contingent on quality standards and qualified teachers; and a separate Education Department in every province.
  • Emphasised the need for greater state involvement in education generally.
5

Post-Wood's Despatch Developments (Late 19th Century)

  • 1857: the three universities — Calcutta, Bombay, Madras — established, per Wood's Despatch's own recommendation, modelled on London University (R.A.S./R.T.S. 2010). during Lord Caning Tenure
  • Overall dominance of Wood's Despatch ideals led to Westernization of the education system, with European-run institutions, plus gradually emerging missionary and private Indian efforts.
6

Hunter Commission (1882)

  • Appointed by Lord Ripon (66th BPSC Re-exam confirms Ripon, not Wedderburn/Curzon/Lytton); chaired by W.W. (William Wilson) Hunter.
  • Objective: review implementation of Wood's Despatch, assess elementary education specifically.
  • Key recommendations: a dual track in high-school education (vocational/commercial training alongside literary studies for university admission); strong emphasis on primary education in local languages with practical subjects (U.P.P.C.S. 2004 — primary education, not higher, girls', or technical education, is the settled correct answer); decentralisation — district and city boards to administer primary education; promoted private effort under public oversight.
  • Follow-on growth: teaching-cum-examining universities set up — Punjab University (1882) and Allahabad University (1887).
W.W. Hunter, the man
Background
Scottish historian, statistician, compiler, and member of the Indian Civil Service.
Best known for

Indian Universities Act (1904) & the Gokhale-Era Reforms

Raleigh Commission (1902) → Indian Universities Act, 1904

  • Constituted 1902 by Lord Curzon, chaired by Sir Thomas Raleigh; two Indian members: Syed Hussain Bilgrami and Justice Gurudas Banerjee (66th BPSC Re-exam )
    Members => B & B
  • Scope: examined conditions/prospects of universities, explicitly excluding primary or secondary education from its remit.
  • Provisions of the 1904 Act: universities instructed to prioritise study and research; reduced the number of university fellows while increasing government-nominated ones; government gained veto/amendment power over university regulations; stricter conditions for affiliating private colleges; sanctioned ₹5 lakh/year for improving higher education.
  • Curzon justified this as promoting quality/efficiency — nationalists saw it as an attempt to restrict education and discipline the educated toward loyalty, strengthening imperial control. Gokhale famously called it a "retrograde measure."

Location:
Established in the Samastipur district of Bihar along the Burhi Gandak River.

Officially opened on April 1, 1905, by Viceroy Lord Curzon.

Gokhale's Resolution (1910) → Government Resolution on Education Policy (1913)

  • Gokhale's Resolution, 1910: proposed compulsory primary education in areas where at least 35% of 6–7-year-old boys were already receiving instruction; allowed state/local authorities to determine cost-sharing; recommended a separate central Education Department and an Education Secretary. Gokhale ultimately withdrew the resolution based on government assurances of reform.
  • The 1913 Government Resolution (also influenced by Baroda's Compulsory Primary Education, 1906) followed: government declined direct responsibility for compulsory education, but committed to reducing illiteracy; encouraged provinces toward free elementary education for the poor/marginalised; supported private educational efforts and improving secondary-school quality; recommended a university in each province.
  • Women's education: recommended a practical, utility-focused curriculum for girls (minimising exam emphasis), and more female teachers/inspectors.
8

Sadler University Commission (1917–1919)

  • Constituted 1917 to study Calcutta University specifically, but its recommendations applied broadly.
  • Chairman: Dr. Michael Sadler, Vice-Chancellor of Leeds University ; two Indian members: Dr. Ashutosh Mukherjee and Dr. Ziauddin Ahmad
    Members => A to Z.
  • Core position: improving secondary education is a necessary precondition for improving university education; strongly criticised the University Act, 1904 for poor college-university coordination.
  • Key recommendations: a 12-year schooling system — students entering university after an intermediate stage (rather than matriculation) for a 3-year degree course, meant to better prepare students, relieve universities of below-standard entrants, and provide collegiate education for non-university-bound students; a separate board for secondary/intermediate education; university autonomy (less rigid regulation); extended female and technical/vocational education, improved teacher training.
Impact of the Sadler Commission (1916–1921)
New universities
Seven established: Mysore, Patna, Benaras, Aligarh, Dacca, Lucknow, Osmania.
Structural change
More teaching-and-residential universities (vs. purely examining bodies); Honours courses introduced; new Academic Councils created for internal governance (curriculum, exams, research).
Coordination body
Inter-University Board established 1925.
Student welfare
First time student welfare became an institutional concern — dedicated Students' Welfare Boards set up in each university.

Education under Dyarchy (from 1919)

Following the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms, responsibility for education was transferred to provincial ministries (a "transferred subject" under Dyarchy) — a significant decentralising shift in educational administration (cross-refer the Government of India Act, 1919 in the 1918–22 dossier).

9

Hartog Committee (1929), the Sargent Plan (1944) & the Wardha Scheme (1937)

Hartog Committee (1929)

  • Chair: Philip Hartog; appointed by the Simon Commission to assess deteriorating educational standards.
  • Primary education: government control/inspection of primary schools to fix stagnation and wastage; curriculum suited to local needs with practical value; refresher courses for teachers; primary schools doubling as community centres (adult education, medical relief, recreation).
  • Secondary education: criticised the exam-focused system, urged a more diversified curriculum (industrial/commercial subjects).
  • Higher education: recommended affiliated universities alongside unitary models, better libraries, honours courses; admission based on aptitude, not rote memorisation.
  • Women's education: equal importance to boys'/girls' education; more female teachers and primary schools for girls; secondary curriculum for girls to include hygiene, home science, music.

Wardha Scheme of Basic Education (1937)

  • Initiated by Congress, under the Zakir Hussain Committee; principle: Nai Talim ("learning through activity") — rooted in Gandhian philosophy, promoting practical skills through education.
  • Key features: free and compulsory education for the first 7 years of school; instruction in the mother tongue, with Hindi taught where it wasn't the local language; community engagement via service activities around schools; curriculum included mathematics, general science, social studies, painting, music, physical education — excluding religious education.
  • Limited long-term impact due to WWII and the resignation of Congress ministries in 1939.

Sargent Plan (1944)

  • Drafted by Sir John Sargent, the Government of India's Educational Advisor — aimed at improving Indian education post-WWII.
  • Recommendations: free pre-primary education (ages 3–6, trained female teachers); universal, compulsory, free education for ages 6–11 (junior basic) and further to age 14 (senior basic per one source's split), focused on learning-through-activity and physical training; six years of high school (ages 11–17) with academic and vocational streams, mother tongue as medium, English as compulsory second language; post-higher-secondary 3-year university degree; financial assistance for poor students; improved teacher competence/service conditions; high standards for postgraduate/applied research; an all-India coordinating body like England's University Grants Committee; plus liquidation of adult illiteracy, school medical check-ups, and special education for the physically handicapped.
  • Impact: laid the foundation for compulsory/free education and teacher-training programmes in independent India.

Part III
Development of the Indian Press

From Hickey's Bengal Gazette to the Press Enquiry Committee of 1947.

11

Origins of the Indian Press

  • Printing was introduced to India (and all of Asia) by the Portuguese — at Goa, 1556/1557 (Jharkhand PCS 2021 gives 1556 as the settled year), with Jesuits publishing the first book.
  • 1684: the East India Company established a press in Bombay — but no newspapers were published in Company territory for nearly a century, largely to suppress news of Company misconduct.
  • 1776: William Bolts, after being censured by the Company, tried to publish a newspaper exposing its wrongdoings — stopped by the authorities before it could appear.
  • 29 January 1780: James Augustus Hickey launched The Bengal Gazette (also called the Original Calcutta General Advertiser) — India's first newspaper (71st BPSC), published from Calcutta, spreading across Bengal, Bihar, Orissa; known for outspoken criticism of the Company. Hickey's press was seized in 1782 after his attacks on officials.
  • 1824: Raja Rammohan Roy protested against press restrictions — the earliest organised call for press freedom in India.

The Vernacular Press Act, 1878 — Full Detail

  • Passed under Lord Lytton (1876–1880) — the vernacular press had grown increasingly critical of British rule since the 1857 revolt, and public discontent from the 1876–77 famine plus Lytton's own lavish spending intensified this criticism, prompting the Act.
  • Provisions (69th & 70th BPSC): empowered District Magistrates to compel printers/publishers into bonds preventing "disaffection" or inter-community "antipathy"; decisions were final and unappealable in court; publishers could submit content to a government censor in advance to avoid punishment; repeated violations → confiscation of press equipment; security deposits could be forfeited on violation. It treated the vernacular press more harshly than the English press, earning the derogatory nickname "Gagging Act."
  • Proceedings were instituted against papers like Som Prakash, Bharat Mihir, Dacca Prakash, and Samachar.
  • Pre-censorship clause removed in 1878 itself, following objections from Lord Cranbrook; a Press Commissioner post was created to supply accurate news to the press.
  • Repealed 1882 under Lord Ripon, after widespread protest — its original justification no longer held (70th BPSC Re-exam confirms all three linked facts: Lytton enacted it, it was the "Gagging Act," Ripon repealed it — all correct together).

Aftermath (post-1882)

  • Surendranath Banerjea (1883) — first Indian journalist imprisoned for criticising a judge in The Bengalee (70th BPSC) — jailed 2 April 1883 for calling out a Calcutta High Court judge's insensitivity toward Bengali religious sentiment.
14

Bal Gangadhar Tilak & Press Repression in the Extremist Phase

  • Through Kesari (Marathi) and Mahratta (English), both launched 1881, Poona — plus the Ganapati and Shivaji festivals — Tilak promoted anti-imperialist sentiment; led the boycott of foreign cloth (1896) and a no-tax movement (1897) in Maharashtra.
  • Plague unrest (1897): Tilak supported government anti-plague measures broadly, but their harsh execution sparked discontent, leading the Chapekar brothers to assassinate British official Rand.
  • Sedition charges against Tilak: arrested for a poem and a speech justifying Shivaji's killing of Afzal Khan, published/delivered around the Chapekar affair — resulting in an 18-month prison sentence (though earlier notes tie the specific 1897 arrest itself to Section 124-A IPC directly for the Kesari articles). His imprisonment sparked nationwide protest, earning him the title Lokmanya ("Respected by the People," 64th BPSC).
  • Tilak was first Indian to go to jail specifically for journalism-related "duty" per one framing (though Surendranath Banerjea is separately credited as the very first journalist imprisoned, 1883 — the two claims apply to slightly different specific charges/contexts, so watch which exact phrasing a question uses).
  • He was later (1908) arrested under the Newspaper Act for continued Extremist-press activity — see Ch.12's table.

High-frequency matching traps
  • NOT associated with Gandhi: Indian Mirror (edited by Manmohan Ghosh/Debendranath Tagore) and Yugantar (Bhupendra Dutta/Barindra Ghosh) — Gandhi's own papers were only Indian Opinion, Young India, Navjeevan, Harijan.
  • The Pioneer is wrongly sometimes paired with Gandhi — it was George Allen's Allahabad paper, unconnected to Gandhi.
  • Independent is wrongly paired with Ambedkar in one distractor set — it belongs to Motilal Nehru.
  • Tahzeeb-ul-Akhlaq is wrongly paired with Jinnah in one distractor — it is Sir Syed Ahmed Khan's paper.
  • Editor sequence, Vande Mataram: Bipin Chandra Pal (Aug–Oct 1906) → Aurobindo Ghosh (Oct 1906–May 1907) → Paris edition under Hardayal (from Sept 1909), distributed by Bhikaji Cama — a four-stage sequence often flattened incorrectly to a single name in PYQ options.
  • Amrita Bazar Patrika founder is Shishir Kumar Ghosh (with Motilal Ghosh) — not Girish Chandra Ghosh, who founded the separate Bengalee and Hindoo Patriot.
