Case File · From Purna Swaraj to the Cripps Mission — the Road to Quit India

From the Lahore pledge to the salt on Dandi's shore — the thirteen years that made Poorna Swaraj non-negotiable.

Road to Purna Swaraj (1928–29)

Calcutta's ultimatum to Lahore's midnight flag
Background — 1921 origin of the idea At the Ahmedabad Session (1921) of Congress, Maulana Hasrat Mohani first proposed that Swaraj be defined as Complete Independence free from all foreign control, and that Congress adopt it as its goal (I.A.S. Pre 2004, 2001). C.R. Das was elected President of this session but was in jail, so Hakim Ajmal Khan presided as acting President.
TrapMazharul Haque, Hakim Ajmal Khan, and Abul Kalam Azad are wrong-answer options for "who first proposed complete-independence Swaraj" — it was Hasrat Mohani.
Calcutta Session (1928)
AspectDetail
What happenedCongress approved the Nehru Report but saw discontent among younger leaders — Jawaharlal Nehru, Subhash Bose, Satyamurthy — who demanded Purna Swaraj (complete independence).
Senior leaders' positionGandhi and Motilal Nehru favoured Dominion Status; initially proposed a two-year grace period, later reduced to one year under pressure from younger elements.
UltimatumCongress gave the British government an ultimatum: accept the Nehru Report within the year, or accept complete independence as the goal / face a mass movement.
Presided byMadras Session, 1927 (the year before) was presided by Dr. M.A. Ansari — passed a resolution against sending Indian troops to fight in China/Mesopotamia/Persia, plus Simon Commission rejection and Hindu-Muslim unity proposals; Purna Swaraj was NOT adopted in 1927 (Chhattisgarh PCS 2022 trap).

Political Activity in 1929

  • Gandhi's mobilisation: travelled extensively to prepare the public, encouraging youth involvement, organising village work, drawing inspiration from the Bardoli agitation (1928).
  • Foreign Cloth Boycott Campaign: initiated March 1929 in Calcutta; the Congress Working Committee (CWC) formed a Foreign Cloth Boycott Committee.
  • Tensions of 1929: the Meerut Conspiracy Case (March); Bhagat Singh and B.K. Dutt's bomb explosion in the Central Legislative Assembly (8 April 1929); formation of a minority Labour government in England (May 1929) under Ramsay MacDonald (PM) with Wedgewood Benn as Secretary of State for India.

Irwin's Declaration (31 October 1929)

  • Joint effort of the Labour government and the Conservative Viceroy, Lord Irwin, to restore faith in British policy.
  • Dominion Status stated as the eventual goal per the 1917 Declaration — but with no specific timeline.
  • Irwin committed to a Round Table Conference after submission of the Simon Commission's report; the declaration lacked any immediate change.

Delhi Manifesto (2 November 1929)

  • National leaders demanded: RTC to act as a constituent assembly to implement dominion status, majority representation for Congress, and amnesty for political prisoners.
  • December 1929: Gandhi, Motilal Nehru and others met Irwin, who rejected these demands — the conference's purpose, he said, was not to draft a constitutional scheme.
Lahore Congress Session — Purna Swaraj declared (December 1929)
AspectDetail
CauseInability of the government to act on the Nehru Report compelled Congress to announce Purna Swaraj as its goal — India's independence proposal was passed for the first time (M.P. PCS 1996).
PresidentJawaharlal Nehru became Congress President (67th BPSC) with Gandhi's support despite opposition — this was the first time Nehru presided over a Congress session (UPPCS Mains 2013; contrast with Calcutta 1928, Lucknow 1936, Ramgarh 1940).
Nehru's stanceDeclared an "open conspiracy" for independence; advocated a socialist, republican India, rejecting kings and industrial power structures; presidential address: "we have but one goal today, that of independence."
Resolution moverThe Poorna Swaraj Resolution itself was moved/prepared and presented by J.L. Nehru (UPPCS Mains 2009); the Nehru Report was rejected in this very session, since it had only sought Dominion Status.
Key decisions (31 Dec 1929)(1) No benefit from RTC; (2) renunciation of the Dominion Status plan in the Nehru Report; (3) word "Swaraj" means Complete Independence; (4) AICC/CWC authorised to start Civil Disobedience — including non-payment of taxes — when deemed necessary; legislators urged to resign.
Flag hoistingOn 31 December 1929, at the stroke of midnight, Nehru hoisted the tricolour flag of independence at the bank of the River Ravi, Lahore, with cries of "Inquilab Zindabad" (64th BPSC).
Independence Day fixedCWC meeting of 2 January 1930 decided to celebrate 26 January 1930 as the first "Purna Swaraj Day" / Independence (Swarajya) Day, and every subsequent 26 January as "Purna Swadhinta Divas" (66th BPSC).
Traps around the Lahore Session
  • Abolition of untouchability was NOT among the Lahore resolutions (UP UDA/LDA Mains 2010) — the four resolutions above are exhaustive; "declaration of foreign policy" was primarily an earlier (1921) Congress concern, not a Lahore 1929 resolution.
  • The rift between Moderates and Extremists was resolved at the Lucknow Session (1916), NOT at Lahore 1929; and no resolution rejecting the two-nation theory was passed at Lahore either — of the three common claims tested about Lahore's significance, only "complete independence resolution passed" is correct (I.A.S. Pre 2012).
  • Presidents of Lahore Session — correct answer is always Jawaharlal Nehru, not Vallabhbhai Patel, Motilal Nehru, Rajendra Prasad, or Abul Kalam Azad.

Independence Pledge & Gandhi's Eleven Demands

From the Swadhinta oath to the ultimatum the British ignored

Independence Pledge (26 January 1930)

Nationwide meetings were held and the Independence Pledge was recited nationwide, emphasising:

  • The inalienable right to freedom.
  • British exploitation caused economic, political, cultural and spiritual ruin.
  • Economic deterioration from heavy revenue demands, destruction of village industries, and British manipulation of currency and trade.
  • Denial of political rights and free association.
  • Spiritual emasculation of India through compulsory disarmament.
  • Commitment to civil disobedience and Purna Swaraj under Congress's guidance.
TrapBefore 1947, 26 January was called "Poorna Swaraj Day" (Jharkhand PCS 2021) — NOT Republic Day (that title only applied from 1950), Martyr's Day, or Constitution Day.
Gandhi's Eleven Demands (1930) — presented to the British government
CategoryDemands
General interest50% cut in Army and civil-service expenditure; total prohibition; CID reforms; amendment of the Arms Act for popular control over firearms licences.
Bourgeois-specificDecrease rupee–sterling exchange ratio to 1s 4d; introduce textile protection.
Peasant-specificSlash land revenue by 50%; abolish the salt tax and government salt monopoly; reserve coastal shipping for Indians; release political prisoners; approve the Postal Reservation Bill.
  • Deadline: 31 January 1930 — the British ignored the ultimatum, directly leading to the launch of the Civil Disobedience Movement.
  • Authorising context: the Lahore Congress (1929) empowered the CWC to initiate civil disobedience, focusing on non-payment of taxes, and urged legislature members to resign (I.A.S. Pre 2005 — the Lahore Session is the correct answer for "which session authorised the CWC to launch civil disobedience", not Bombay/Lucknow/Tripura).
  • Gandhi's authority: in February 1930, at a CWC meeting at Sabarmati Ashram, the CWC gave Gandhi full authority to launch the Civil Disobedience Movement whenever he deemed fit, trusting his judgement as an expert in mass struggle.

Civil Disobedience Movement & the Dandi March

Salt as symbol — the movement that spread to every province
Dandi March — full picture
AspectDetail
Started12 March 1930 from Sabarmati Ashram; broke the Salt Law at Dandi beach on 6 April 1930. Reached Dandi 5 April 1930.
Duration / distanceLasted 24 days (66th BPSC); distance given as 240 miles (theory) / 241 miles (PYQ sources) through Gujarat.
Number of marchersSources vary: 78 followers (theory dossier) vs 80 volunteers (PYQ Wallah compilation) — both figures appear in study material; treat as a known discrepancy, not a single fixed number.
Letter to Viceroy2 March 1930 — Gandhi wrote to Lord Irwin describing British rule as a curse that impoverished the masses, reduced Indians to political serfdom, sapped cultural foundations, and degraded spiritual well-being; announced defiance of the salt laws from 12 March.
Pre-march plan (outlined at Ashram)Initiate civil disobedience of the salt law; picket foreign liquor/cloth shops; refuse to pay taxes; lawyers to abandon practice; boycott law courts; government servants to resign — all to adhere to truth and non-violence.
At Dandi (5–6 April)Addressing reporters on 5 April: "I want world sympathy in this battle of Right against might" (UPPCS Mains 2013 — associated specifically with the Dandi March, not Non-Cooperation/Individual Satyagraha/Quit India). Breaking the Salt Law on 6 April officially launched the CDM.
"Sedition" quote"Sedition has become my religion" — said by Gandhi during the Salt Satyagraha (speech of 18 March 1930), as a proclamation of non-violent civil disobedience (I.A.S. Pre 2025).
ReactionsSubhash Chandra Bose likened it to Napoleon's return from Elba. British editor Brailsford mockingly termed it a "kindergarten stage of revolution" / asked whether "an Emperor be unseated by boiling sea water in a Kettle"; The Statesman sarcastically replied that Gandhi could boil seawater till they got Dominion Status. Despite scepticism, the movement's moral impact boosted Satyagraha volunteers to over 60,000.
American reporter presentWebb Miller stayed with Gandhi at Sabarmati Ashram during the march and later reported on the Dharasana raid — the correct answer whenever "which American journalist" is asked (traps: Brailsford, G. Slocombe, James Patterson, Francis Louis, Mark Tully, Philip Sprat).

Arrests & leadership after Gandhi

  • Nehru's arrest in April 1930 sparked demonstrations in Madras, Calcutta, Karachi.
  • Gandhi was arrested on 4/5 May 1930 (sources differ on the exact date) while planning a raid on the Dharasana Salt Works; sent to Yerwada Jail.
  • Leadership passed to Abbas Tayabji (until his arrest), then to Sarojini Naidu, who led the raid on the Dharasana Salt Depot/Works on 21 May 1930 alongside Manilal Gandhi and Imamsahib.
  • Congress sanctioned: non-payment of revenue in ryotwari areas; a no-chowkidari-tax campaign in zamindari areas; violation of forest laws in the Central Provinces.
  • The government declared the Indian National Congress illegal following the mass breaking of the salt law.
Regional spread of the Civil Disobedience Movement
RegionLeaders & events

Pre 2015). Picketing of foreign cloth, anti-liquor campaign in Coimbatore, Madura, Virudhunagar; police repression at Choolai Mills; riots in Bodinayakanur.

OrissaGopalbandhu Chaudhuri led effective salt satyagraha in Balasore, Cuttack, Puri.

BengalCongress split between Bose and J.M. Sengupta; unrest in Midnapur, Arambagh, Dacca, Kishoreganj; Surya Sen raided armouries (Chittagong) and declared a provisional government.
BiharSalt satyagraha in Champaran, Saran; symbolic salt-making in Patna under Ambika Kant Sinha; no-chaukidari-tax agitation — including in Begusarai (42nd BPSC). Peshawar / NWFPKhan Abdul Gaffar Khan ("Frontier Gandhi", 67th BPSC re-exam) founded the Khudai Khidmatgars ("Red Shirts") — for Pathan regional nationalist unity and anti-colonial struggle (I.A.S. Pre 2002 trap: not union with Afghanistan, not terrorism, not communism); also improved conditions of wage-earners. Garhwal Rifles under Chandra Singh Garhwali refused to fire on an unarmed crowd — the "Peshawar Incident" (1930).
SholapurFiercest response to Gandhi's arrest — textile workers struck from 7 May, burnt liquor shops, railway stations, police stations, municipal buildings, law courts; a virtual parallel government was established.
GujaratAnand, Borsad, Nadiad, Bardoli — no-tax campaign.
Maharashtra / Karnataka / Central ProvincesDefiance of forest laws on grazing, timber, forest produce.
United ProvincesNo-revenue, no-rent campaign against loyalist zamindars — active in Agra and Rae Bareli.
Manipur & NagalandRani Gaidinliu, a Naga tribal woman, initiated revolts and received life imprisonment; her movement is called the Jiatrang / Jiyatarang Movement (UPPCS 2008).

Forms of mobilisation & mass participation

  • Mobilisation methods: Prabhat Pheris (morning processions); Vanar Senas (Monkey Brigades) & Manjari Senas (Flower Brigades) engaged youth; pamphlets and magic-lantern shows.
  • Women: protested at liquor shops and foreign-cloth stores — women's participation is considered maximum in the Salt Satyagraha among major movements listed (UPPCS Mains 2016). Named participants: Sarojini Naidu, Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay — all three participated (60th–62nd BPSC 2016). Prabhavati Devi (a freedom fighter of Patna, 48th–52nd BPSC), along with Rajvanshi Devi, Suniti Devi, Radhika Devi, contributed to Bihar's freedom movement. Dr. Radhabai was arrested on 13 June 1932 leading a procession in the Second Civil Disobedience Movement.
  • Students & Youth: boycotted foreign goods. Muslims: varied engagement — NWFP saw high participation. Merchants & petty traders: very enthusiastic, especially in Tamil Nadu and Punjab. Tribals: active in Central Provinces, Maharashtra, Karnataka. Workers: participated in Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Sholapur. Peasants: active in UP, Bihar, Gujarat.
  • Government response: ambivalence — alternated between repression (lathi charges, restrictions) and concessions.
Salt Satyagraha — participation & purpose facts
Provincial satyagrahi count
Gujarat highest (32), then Maharashtra (13), UP (8), Kutch (6), Kerala (4), Punjab (3), Rajputana (3), Bombay (2: Daudbhai, Harilal Mahimtura), Sindh (1: Anand Hingorani), Nepal (2), Tamil Nadu (1: Tapan Nayar), Andhra (1: Subramanyam), Utkal (1: Motibasdas), Karnataka (1: Mahadev Martand), Bihar (1: Girivardhari Chaudhary), Bengal (1: Durgesh Chandra Das) — UPPCS Mains 2013.
Ultimate goal of Salt Satyagraha
More than one correct: repeal of salt laws, curtailment of government power, and economic relief to common people all apply — Purna Swaraj was NOT the specific aim of the Salt Satyagraha itself (63rd BPSC 2017).
Those convicted alongside Gandhi
Madan Mohan Malaviya, Devadas Gandhi, K.M. Munshi, B.G. Kher, Mahadev Desai, C. Rajagopalachari, Vithalbhai Patel were convicted for breaching the salt law; Gandhi himself was arrested separately after breaking the law at Dandi (UP RO/ARO Mains 2016).
Assorted CDM traps
  • Chronological order check (Uttarakhand Mains 2006): Arrival of Simon Commission (3 Feb 1928) → Dandi March start (12 Mar 1930) → Gandhi-Irwin Pact (5 Mar 1931) → Quit India Movement (9 Aug 1942).
  • Vinoba Bhave was arrested for the first time for taking part in the Civil Disobedience Movement (UP UDA/LDA 2001) — not Bardoli Movement, Champaran Satyagraha, or Non-Cooperation.
  • Assertion-Reason: "the salt agitation was launched by Gandhi in 1930" is true, but the reason "his object was to make salt available free to the poor" is false — the real object was breaking the discriminatory salt tax/monopoly (UP PCS 2003).

Gandhi-Irwin Pact & Karachi Session (1931)

"Two Mahatmas" and the Fundamental Rights resolution
Gandhi-Irwin Pact (Delhi Pact)
AspectDetail
Release & talksGandhi and all CWC members unconditionally released from prison — dated 25 January (theory) / 26 January 1931 (PYQ source); talks began mid-February 1931 through the efforts of Tej Bahadur Sapru and M.R. Jaykar (mediators).
Signed5 March 1931 (67th BPSC re-exam), also called the Delhi Pact; the CWC had empowered Gandhi to negotiate.
Irwin's concessionsImmediate release of non-violent political prisoners; remission of unpaid fines; return of unsold lands; lenient treatment for resigned government servants; right to make salt in coastal villages for personal consumption (not sale); permission for peaceful picketing; withdrawal of emergency ordinances related to the CDM.
Rejected by IrwinA public inquiry into police excesses; commutation of the death sentences of Bhagat Singh and comrades.
Gandhi's concessionsSuspend the CDM; participate in the Second Round Table Conference (federation and Indian responsibilities, minority safeguards, finance/defence/external affairs).
Contemporary receptionSarojini Naidu termed Irwin and Gandhi the "Two Mahatmas." Irwin's biographer Alan Campbell Johnson called Gandhi's gains "consolation prizes" and the pact "the only surrender" — Irwin agreeing merely to negotiate (UPPCS Pre 2014).
Public disappointmentThe pact did not secure commutation of the death sentences of Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru.
Karachi Congress Session (29–31 March 1931)
AspectDetail
ContextHeld to ratify/endorse the Gandhi-Irwin Pact; convened six days after Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were executed (23 March 1931) — sparking protests over Gandhi's failure to secure clemency.
PresidentVallabhbhai Patel.
Tribute to martyrsCongress condemned political violence but honoured Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, Rajguru's bravery and sacrifice.
Fundamental Rights ResolutionPassed for the first time at any INC session (69th BPSC 2023 — trap: not Surat 1907 or Gaya 1922); guaranteed free speech/press, right to form associations and assemble peacefully, universal adult franchise, equal legal rights irrespective of caste/creed/gender, state neutrality in religion, free compulsory primary education, protection of minority culture/language/script. Drafted by Jawaharlal Nehru with the help of M.N. Roy.
National Economic Programme ResolutionRent/revenue reduction for peasants; exemption for uneconomic landholdings; relief from agricultural debt; control over usury; improved working conditions; union rights for workers/peasants; state control over key industries, mines, transport — also drafted by Nehru with M.N. Roy.
ReaffirmationPurna Swaraj reasserted as Congress's ultimate aim; this was also considered the first session where "Poorna Swaraj" was formally defined.
Gandhi's remarkFaced with black-flag protests against him at the session, Gandhi said: "They might kill me but they cannot kill Gandhism."
Bose's verdictSubhas Chandra Bose termed this session the "pinnacle of Mahatma Gandhi's popularity and prestige."
Chronology trapCorrect order: Gandhi-Irwin Pact (5 Mar 1931) → Execution of Bhagat Singh/Rajguru/Sukhdev (23 Mar 1931) → Karachi Session (29–31 Mar 1931) → Second RTC (7 Sep–1 Dec 1931) → Poona Pact (24 Sep 1932). Multiple PYQs test this exact sequence in scrambled form.

The Round Table Conferences (1930–32)

Three sessions of one conference — Congress attended only the middle one
Background The Simon Commission's two-volume report was published on 10 & 24 June 1930; political associations rejected it, and Congress's main leaders were in jail. The British organised the First RTC in an atmosphere of dissatisfaction.
First RTC — London, Nov 1930 – Jan 1931
AspectDetail
Opened / chairedOpened by King George V, chaired by Ramsay MacDonald.
CongressBoycotted (63rd BPSC) — 89 Indian delegates attended without Congress.
ParticipantsPrincely states (Maharajas of Alwar, Baroda, Patiala); Muslim League — Aga Khan III, Jinnah, Maulana Mohammad Ali, Muhammad Shafi, Fazlul Haq, Homi Mody; Hindu Mahasabha — B.S. Moonje, M.R. Jayakar; Sikhs/Parsis/Women — Sardar Ujjal Singh, Phiroze Sethna, Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz; Depressed Classes — B.R. Ambedkar, Rettamalai Srinivasan; Labour — N.M. Joshi, B. Shiva Rao; Indian Christians — K.T. Paul; Liberals — Tej Bahadur Sapru, Srinivasa Sastri; Government of India — Narendra Nath Law, Bhupendra Nath Mitra; also Sundar Singh Majithia.
OutcomeIndia to develop into a federation, with safeguards on defence/finance; limited achievement, no major implementation — CDM continued, underlining the need for Congress's participation in future talks. Ambedkar demanded a separate electorate for the depressed classes here.
Second RTC — London, 7 Sept – 1 Dec 1931
AspectDetail
Path to itIndian Liberal Party members (Tej Bahadur Sapru, C.Y. Chintamani) urged Gandhi to negotiate with the Viceroy → led directly to the Gandhi-Irwin Pact.
Congress's sole official representativeMahatma Gandhi — this was the only RTC Congress participated in. Gandhi travelled on the ship S.S. Rajputana and stayed at Kingsley Hall, London (not St. James Palace/India House).
Other Indian attendeesSarojini Naidu, Madan Mohan Malaviya, Annie Besant, Mahadev Desai, and Gandhi's private secretary Pyarelal Nayyar. Jawaharlal Nehru did NOT participate (MP PCS 2017), nor did Rajendra Prasad (UPPCS 2020) — both are common wrong-answer traps.
Other participantsPrincely States (Alwar, Baroda, Bhopal); Muslim League — Aga Khan III, Jinnah, Maulana Shaukat Ali; Hindu Mahasabha — M.R. Jayakar, B.S. Moonje; Liberals — Sapru, Srinivasa Sastri, Chintamani; Depressed Classes — Ambedkar, Rettamalai Srinivasan; Industry/Labour — G.D. Birla, N.M. Joshi, V.V. Giri; Women — Naidu, Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz, Radhabai Subbarayan; Govt of India — C.P. Ramaswami Iyer, Narendra Nath Law, M. Ramachandra Rao.
Change in leadershipLord Irwin replaced by Lord Willingdon; a National Government took charge in Britain, Conservatives demanding stronger Indian governance.
Gandhi's demandsPartnership on equality between British and India; immediate responsible government at centre and provinces; opposed separate electorates for minorities (Muslims, Depressed Classes).
DeadlockSeparate electorates demanded by Muslims, Depressed Classes, Anglo-Indians, Christians — forming a "Minorities' Pact"; Gandhi opposed this strongly, causing a stalemate on communal issues (41st BPSC 1996 — the conference specifically failed over "communal delegations").
OutcomeNo significant agreement on India's constitutional future. MacDonald announced: two Muslim-majority provinces (NWFP, Sindh); an Indian Consultative Committee; three expert committees (finance, franchise, states); prospect of a British Communal Award if no consensus was reached.
Gandhi's returnReturned to India 28 December 1931, with the fundamental demand for freedom still unmet; remarked he returned "barehanded" but had never let the flag's honour down.
Third RTC — 17 Nov – 24 Dec 1932
AspectDetail
CongressBoth Congress and Gandhi boycotted (63rd BPSC) — limited representation of key Indian leaders as a result.
Indian States' representativesAkbar Hydari (Dewan of Hyderabad), Mirza Ismail (Dewan of Mysore), V.T. Krishnamachari (Dewan of Baroda), Wajahat Hussain (Jammu & Kashmir).

AftermathRecommendations published as a White Paper (March 1933); debated by a Joint Select Committee of the British Parliament; draft Bill produced February 1935 → Government of India Act, July 1935.
Key facts tested across all three RTCs
Gandhi/Congress
Participated ONLY in the Second RTC; did not attend the First or Third.
Jinnah
Attended the First and Second RTCs, but NOT the Third.
Attended all three
B.R. Ambedkar and Tej Bahadur Sapru — the only two Indian leaders who participated in all three conferences (an extremely frequently repeated PYQ fact).
"Three conferences" is a misnomer
Technically these were three sessions of a single conference, not three separate conferences — because non-Congress Indian parties represented only sectional interests, and Congress was absent from two of the three (I.A.S. Pre 1996).

Communal Award, Poona Pact & the Harijan Campaign (1932–34)

A fast unto death, a pact Gandhi never signed
Communal Award (16 August 1932)
AspectDetail
Announced byRamsay MacDonald, British PM, based on the Indian Franchise Committee (Lothian Committee) recommendations.
Separate electoratesEstablished for Muslims, Europeans, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, marginalised women, and Marathas in Bombay (7 seats) — Buddhists were NOT given a separate electorate (UP PCS 2001 trap).
Depressed ClassesTreated as a minority for the first time (earlier only Muslims and Sikhs were "minorities"; Europeans/Anglo-Indians/Christians already had separate electorates since 1919); arrangement to last 20 years; 71 seats reserved in provincial legislatures; double voting right — in both separate and general electorates.
Other provisionsProvincial legislature seats doubled; weightage for Muslims where a minority; 3% of seats reserved for women in all provinces except NWFP; seats allocated for labourers, landlords, traders, industrialists.
Congress positionStrongly opposed but neither outright rejected nor accepted — emphasised minority consent.
Gandhi's reactionSaw the award as detrimental to Hinduism and the depressed classes, perpetuating untouchability; called for a common electorate and eradication of untouchability.

Gandhi's fast & the Poona Pact

  • Gandhi began his first fast unto death on 20 September 1932 at Yerwada Jail, demanding joint electorates with increased reserved seats for depressed classes.
  • Poona Pact signed 24 September 1932, between B.R. Ambedkar (depressed classes) and Madan Mohan Malaviya (representing the Hindu community) — Gandhi himself did NOT sign the document (UP PCS 2001, 2004 — a frequent trap, since many MCQs frame it loosely as "between Gandhi and Ambedkar").
  • Other signatories: M.R. Jayakar, Devdas Gandhi, P. Baloo, Gavai, Thakkar, Solanki, Tej Bahadur Sapru, G.D. Birla, Rajagopalachari, Rajendra Prasad, Rao Bahadur Srinivasan, M.C. Rajah, C.V. Mehta, Bakhale, Kamath — Rajagopalachari famously exchanged pens with Ambedkar while signing.
  • Fast ended 26 September 1932.
  • Effect: abandoned separate electorates for the depressed classes; reserved seats raised from 71 to 147/148 (sources differ) in provincial legislatures — Madras 30, Sindh-with-Bombay 15, Punjab 8, Bihar & Orissa 18, Central Provinces 20, Assam 7, Bengal 30, United Provinces 20; plus 18% of general seats in the Central Legislature reserved for depressed classes.
  • The government recognised the Pact as an amendment to the Communal Award.
Assertion-Reason (UPPCS Mains 2005) — both TRUE, reason correctly explains assertion The Poona Pact defeated the purpose of the Communal Award, because it paved the way for reservation of seats for SC/ST in Parliament and State Assemblies.

Harijan campaign that followed

  • Subsequent to the Poona Pact specifically (I.A.S. Pre 2025 — not the Gandhi-Irwin Agreement, not Quit India arrests, not the GoI Act 1935), Gandhi decided to include the upliftment of "Harijans" in his political and social programme.
  • Formed the All India Anti-Untouchability League in September 1932 (specifically 30 September per one source) while Gandhi was imprisoned — first President was Ghanshyam Das Birla (multiple PYQs — trap: not Ambedkar, Amrit Lal Thakkar, Jagjivan Ram). Later renamed Harijan Sevak Sangh.
  • Published the weekly Harijan from January 1933.
  • After his release in August 1933, Gandhi moved to the Satyagraha Ashram, Wardha, honouring his vow to stay away from Sabarmati Ashram until swaraj was achieved.
  • Nationwide tour from November 1933 to July 1934: travelled 20,000 km, raising funds for the Harijan Sevak Sangh and campaigning against untouchability (68th BPSC).
  • Undertook two further fasts on 8 May and 16 August 1934 to highlight the seriousness of untouchability.
  • Faced opposition: orthodox factions disrupted meetings, accused Gandhi of attacking Hinduism; in August 1934 the government defeated the Temple Entry Bill, reflecting orthodox Hindu opposition.
Related Dalit-politics organisations

Founded by Babu Jagjivan Ram.
All India Scheduled Castes Federation

All India Depressed Classes Association
Founded in 1926 by M.C. Rajah.
Ambedkar's remark to Gandhi (14 Aug 1931, Mumbai) Per Nehru-era study material, B.R. Ambedkar told Gandhi: "History tells that Mahatmas, like fleeting phantoms, raise dust, but raise no level" — a UPPCS Mains 2004 trap sometimes wrongly attributed to Jinnah or Savarkar.

Post-CDM Debate & the Congress Socialist Party (1934–36)

Constructive work, council entry, or class struggle?

Withdrawal of the CDM & Gandhi's disquiet

  • The CDM was withdrawn by Gandhi in April 1934; reasons: lack of time to build momentum, and masses inadequately prepared for a prolonged struggle. Gandhi acknowledged its limitations.
  • Gandhi's post-CDM discontent: perceived a rise in corruption and indiscipline within Congress; leadership rivalries; bogus membership inflated to manipulate internal elections; underhanded tactics contradicting his principles; he stressed the need for internal cleansing/self-reform before confronting colonial rule again, sensing mass fatigue.
  • Gandhi publicly announced his resignation from Congress on 17 September 1934; officially resigned October 1934, "to serve it more effectively in thought, word and deed," owing to disagreements with pro-parliamentary-politics factions and Nehru's socialist agenda.
First Stage Debate (1934–35) — three perspectives (70th BPSC)
PerspectivePosition
Constructive work (Gandhian)Focus on social reforms, rural upliftment, Khadi, village industries — building people's power for the next mass struggle.
Constitutional struggle / council entrySupported by M.A. Ansari, Asaf Ali, Bhulabhai Desai, S. Satyamurthy, B.C. Roy — proposed contesting the 1934 Central Legislature elections to sustain political interest and build a political front.

  • Gandhi's conciliatory shift: fearing a Surat-style split, Gandhi agreed to permit council entry — parliamentary politics wouldn't directly bring freedom, but those unable to do satyagraha/constructive work shouldn't stay idle; he assured leftist critics the CDM withdrawal wasn't a compromise with imperialism.
  • May 1934: the AICC convened at Patna, establishing a Congress Parliamentary Board to contest elections under the Congress banner.
  • November 1934 elections to the Central Legislative Assembly: Congress secured 45 of 75 seats reserved for Indians.

Bihar Socialist Party & J.P. Narayan

  • Founded 1931 by Phulan Prasad Varma (at a meeting in his home); influenced by Marx/Lenin and the Fabian Society; formed from within the INC; associated figures — Jaya Prakash Narayan, Ganga Sharan Sinha, Rambriksh Benipuri, Ramanand Mishra.
  • J.P. Narayan, known as "Loknayak," secretly organised Indian masses during the 1942 Quit India Movement with Ram Manohar Lohia and Aruna Asaf Ali; declared "Sampoorna Kranti" (Total Revolution) from Gandhi Maidan, Patna, on 5 June 1974; awarded Bharat Ratna posthumously in 1999.
  • April 1946: "Jaya Prakash Day" was celebrated for his release from prison — a rally at Bankipur Maidan, Patna demanded his release and criticised government policy on political arrests.
Bombay Manifesto (May 1936) Signed by 21 Bombay businessmen — an open indictment of Nehru's socialist preaching at the Lucknow Session; evoked wide support from the business community across India (I.A.S. Pre 2010 — both claims about it are correct).

Government of India Act, 1935

A federation that was proposed but never born
Origin Emerged from the recommendations of the Third Round Table Conference (1932), held without Congress. Proposed an All India Federation of British Indian Provinces, Chief Commissioner's Provinces, and Princely States (voluntary participation) — conditional on states with significant population agreeing to join. The federation never materialised, as most princely states refused. Residuary powers, in the proposed federation, were given to the Governor General.
Government of India Act, 1935 — Federal vs Provincial structure
AspectFederal LevelProvincial Level
ExecutiveGovernor General held pivotal authority as Crown's representative; reserved subjects (foreign affairs, defence, tribal/ecclesiastical matters) handled by GG with advisors not accountable to the legislature; transferred subjects administered by ministers accountable to the federal legislature; GG could act on "individual judgement" for special responsibilities.Provincial autonomy replaced dyarchy — legal and financial autonomy (provinces could borrow on their own security); Governor retained control as Crown's representative, could assume full control indefinitely in a crisis; special authority over minorities, civil servants, law and order, British business interests, princely states.
LegislatureBicameral: Council of States (Upper House, 260 members, partially elected, 40% nominated by princely states, permanent body, 1/3 retiring every third year); Federal Assembly (Lower House, 375 members, indirectly elected in part, 1/3 nominated by princely states, 5-year term). Legislative Lists: federal, provincial, concurrent. Only 20% of the budget votable; GG held residuary powers (restore grant cuts, certify bills, issue ordinances, veto); no-confidence motions allowed only in the Federal Assembly, not the Council of States; communal/class-based electorates extended.Direct elections — all members directly elected; separate electorates per the Communal Award; equal enfranchisement for men and women; Premier-headed ministries responsible to the legislature, removable by no-confidence; legislative power over provincial + concurrent lists; 40% of budget non-votable; Governor could refuse assent to bills, promulgate ordinances, enact Governor's Acts without legislative approval.
Provincial Governments were constituted under this Act, 1935 (53rd–55th BPSC 2011).

Provincial Elections & Congress Ministries (1937–39)

"Crowns of thorns" — 28 months of Congress rule

Second Stage Debate (1937)

  • Provincial assembly elections were announced early 1937, sparking debate on strategy to oppose the 1935 Act without a feasible mass movement. Congress unanimously opposed the Act but lacked clarity on method — agreed to contest with a comprehensive political/economic programme.
  • Left faction (Nehru, Bose, Congress socialists, communists) opposed forming governments post-election, fearing it would dilute opposition to the 1935 Act; proposed entering councils to create deadlocks while integrating workers/peasants into Congress.
  • Office-acceptance proponents argued for a short-term tactical approach in the absence of a mass movement, believing provincial ministries could still promote constructive work.
  • Gandhi's evolving stance: initially against office acceptance, shifted by 1936; at the Lucknow (1936) and Faizpur (1937) sessions, Congress agreed to contest elections but postponed the decision on forming governments until after results.

Work under Congress ministries (1937–39)

  • Civil liberties: repealed 1932 emergency powers (incl. Public Safety Acts); lifted bans on organisations (Hindustan Seva Dal, Youth Leagues), books, journals; removed press restrictions, restored blacklisted newspapers; returned confiscated arms/licences; released political prisoners, revoked deportation orders (incl. Kakori Conspiracy revolutionaries); restored confiscated land in Bombay and pensions for CDM officials. Shortcomings: arrests of Yusuf Meherally and S.S. Batliwala (Madras, inflammatory/seditious speech); CID surveillance on leftists in Bombay (K.M. Munshi).
  • Agrarian reforms: the Working Committee decided in 1937 to abolish feudal intermediaries/peasant proprietorship (39th BPSC) — U.P. Tenancy Act (1939) gave full hereditary rights to tenants, abolished forced labour (begar) and illegal levies (nazrana); Orissa Tenancy Bill (1938) allowed free transfer of occupancy holdings, cut rent-arrears interest from 12.5% to 6%; Debt Relief Acts regulated moneylenders in UP/Orissa; Madras Proposals (T. Prakasam) to recognise cultivators as landowners went unenacted due to ministry resignation. Constraints: limited powers under the 1935 Act, financial constraints, need to conciliate zamindars.
  • Labour: pro-labour stance — Industrial Disputes Act (1938) promoted conciliation, empowered the Court of Industrial Arbitration; the Labour Enquiry Committee (led by Rajendra Prasad) recommended wage increases, maternity benefits, labour recruitment reforms. Challenges: repressive measures (Section 144, arrests) against militant trade-union protests; Nehru's discontent but continued support for the ministries; Gandhi opposed violent methods and reliance on colonial laws.
  • Social welfare: Harijan advancement (temple entry, public facilities, scholarships); education focus on primary/technical/higher education for Harijans and girls; public health and sanitation; Khadi subsidies; prison reforms; the National Planning Committee (1938), led by Subhas Chandra Bose, for structured development planning.
  • Extra-parliamentary activity: literacy campaigns, Congress police stations, panchayats, grievance committees — effective coordination in UP via village Congress committees.

Resignation & aftermath

  • Congress ministries resigned 22–23 October 1939 in protest against Linlithgow's decision to involve India in WWII without consultation — increased industrial unrest followed, and difficulty meeting public expectations.
  • Muslim League celebrated 22 December 1939 as the "Day of Deliverance" (RAS/RTS 1999) — Jinnah called for the celebration.
  • Muslim-grievance reports, chronologically: Pirpur Report (1938) → Sharif Report (March 1939) → Fazlul Haq Report / "Muslim Sufferings Under Congress Rule" (December 1939).

Tripuri Crisis, Bose vs Gandhi & the Forward Bloc (1938–39)

1580 votes to 1377 — a victory Gandhi called his own loss

Bose's rise within Congress

  • As President of the Bengal Provincial Congress Committee, Bose organised youth and promoted the trade-union movement.
  • Disagreed with Gandhi over the intensity/nature of the freedom struggle; along with Nehru, opposed Dominion Status in the Motilal Nehru Report, calling for complete independence; announced the Independence League.
Haripura vs Tripuri Sessions
AspectHaripura Session (Feb 1938)Tripuri Session (1939)
LocationHaripura, GujaratTripuri, near Jabalpur, Madhya Pradesh
OutcomeBose elected President unanimously for the first time.Bose re-elected President, defeating Gandhi's candidate Pattabhi Sitaramayya by 1580 votes to 1377, with support from the Congress Socialist Party and communists.
Vision / eventsPresidential address stressed the revolutionary potential of Congress provincial ministries and economic planning → led to the National Planning Committee; extended moral support to agitators against autocratic princely-state rule; foresaw the approaching war's potential to benefit India's independence struggle.Despite poor health, Bose attended and called for a six-month ultimatum to the British for independence, followed by mass civil disobedience if unmet. Gandhi opposed the ultimatum, citing internal divisions, communal discord, and Congress's unpreparedness. A resolution by Govind Ballabh Pant urged Bose to align with Gandhi's wishes on the Working Committee's composition.
  • Gandhi acknowledged Bose's electoral victory but regarded Sitaramayya's defeat as his own loss — the re-election highlighted ideological polarisation; Bose's critique of the Working Committee's compromises with the British caused several committee members to resign.
  • Unable to secure Gandhi's support or unify Congress around his radical vision, Bose resigned as President in April 1939 (70th BPSC re-exam), paving the way for Rajendra Prasad's presidency.
  • Forward Bloc: founded 3 May 1939 (67th BPSC) by Bose and followers within Congress, at Makur, Unnao — a left-wing nationalist platform for his radical approach to independence.
  • Disciplinary action: after Bose's call for an all-India protest defied the AICC directive, the CWC in August 1939 removed him as President of the Bengal Provincial Congress Committee and barred him from any elective Congress office for three years.
  • The Tripuri session also passed a resolution expressing solidarity with China in its fight against imperialism.
Bose on the coming war Bose viewed a Germany–Britain war as a "godsend opportunity" for Indians to exploit toward independence (I.A.S. Pre 1999) — echoing the Irish saying "England's difficulty is Ireland's opportunity."

Indian Princely States — parallel thread

Butler Committee to the States People's Conference
  • Butler Committee (1927): investigated the relationship between paramount power and princely states; Sir Leslie Scott represented the princes' viewpoint. Findings: the relationship was living/growing, not merely contractual; British paramountcy preserved the princely states; a state should not be transferred to a new government responsible to an Indian legislature without its agreement.
  • All India States People's Conference: organised December 1927, with over 1,500 political workers from various states; key organisers — Balwant Rai Mehta, Manilal Kothari, G.R. Abhyankar.
  • Jawaharlal Nehru was elected President of the All India States People's Conference in 1939.

WWII, the Ramgarh Session & the August Offer (1939–40)

"Dead as a doornail" — Nehru on Britain's first war-time offer
Outbreak of WWII & India's forced entry
AspectDetail
WWII begins1 September 1939 — Germany attacked Poland; war ended September 1945. British PMs during the war: Neville Chamberlain (1937–40) and Winston Churchill (1940–45, also 1951–55).
India declared belligerent3 September 1939 — Viceroy Lord Linlithgow declared India's support for the war without consulting Indian opinion/elected councils, and declared an Emergency.
Congress's conditional supportDemanded a post-war constituent assembly and immediate responsible government at the Centre; Linlithgow rejected both conditions.
Leaders' viewsGandhi preferred to avoid immediate mass struggle, favouring unity and negotiation. Nehru gave conditional support, demanding complete independence first, prioritising sovereignty. Bose and socialists (Acharya Narendra Dev, Jayaprakash Narayan) saw the war as an imperialist conflict and proposed civil disobedience.
CWC at Wardha / Oct 193923 October 1939: CWC rejected Linlithgow's statement as continued imperialism; condemned Fascist aggression while noting India couldn't fight for democratic freedom while denied the same; demanded Britain end imperialism and clarify India's post-war freedom. 17 October 1939: Linlithgow refused to define war aims regarding India, choosing instead to consult princely states/communities — seen by Congress as a divide-and-weaken tactic.

Ramgarh Session (March 1940)

  • Presided by Maulana Abul Kalam Azad; Congress reiterated complete independence as non-negotiable, rejecting dominion status, and considered civil disobedience if necessary.
  • Consensus on need for a movement, disagreement on form: Gandhi favoured continued provincial cooperation and non-violent moral support to Britain; Nehru insisted on complete independence as a precondition for support; Bose advocated direct action, urging India to seize Britain's wartime vulnerability.
  • Declaration: only complete independence acceptable; dominion status within the imperial structure rejected; civil disobedience to be launched when deemed necessary.

August Offer (August 1940)

  • Background: Hitler's success and the fall of Belgium, Holland, France put Britain in a conciliatory mood; Gandhi and Nehru opposed exploiting Britain's weakness; Congress sought to form an interim government, but Britain was uninterested and came up with its own offer.
  • Proposals: Dominion Status explicitly stated, for the first time, as a post-war objective; expansion of the Viceroy's Executive Council to include a majority of Indian members from major parties; a post-war Constituent Assembly (mainly Indians); no future constitution without minority consent (mainly to assure the Muslim League).
  • Congress response: rejected the offer — Nehru called it "as dead as a doornail"; Gandhi felt it widened the gap between British and nationalist aspirations.
  • Muslim League response: welcomed the offer, especially the assurance of a minority veto, seeing it as a step toward partition.

Individual Satyagraha (1940–41)

Vinoba Bhave, then Nehru, then a Sevagram volunteer nobody had heard of
Individual Satyagraha
AspectDetail
ContextBritish ordinances restricted speech/press/assembly; insisted on no constitutional progress until Congress-League agreement.
LaunchedGandhi initiated it in late 1940 — specifically 17 October 1940 — as a limited satyagraha strategy.
ObjectiveShow nationalist strength and patience, protest the war, and highlight parallels between Nazism and British colonial rule.
Key featuresFreedom of Speech — satyagrahis demanded the freedom to make anti-war declarations; Delhi Chalo Movement — if not arrested, satyagrahis would march to Delhi, escalating the protest.
Order of satyagrahisVinoba Bhave — first, started from Pavnar, Maharashtra (68th BPSC); Jawaharlal Nehru — second; Brahma Dutt, a young Sevagram volunteer — third, chosen specifically by Gandhi (67th BPSC re-exam), underscoring that moral conviction — not political prominence — defined genuine satyagraha.
ScaleBy May 1941, around 25,000 people had been arrested for civil disobedience.
Assertion-Reason (UP RO/ARO 2023) — both TRUE, reason correctly explains assertion In October 1940 Gandhi called for a limited satyagraha by a few selected individuals, because the Satyagraha was kept limited so as not to disrupt Britain's war efforts — Congress and Gandhi held an anti-Nazi stance and did not want to obstruct the British war effort while still protesting India's forced inclusion.

"Sarvodaya"

  • The term "Sarvodaya" ("Universal Uplift" / "Progress of All") was first coined by Gandhi as the title of his 1908 translation of John Ruskin's tract on political economy, Unto This Last; Gandhi used it thereafter as the ideal of his own political philosophy (46th BPSC 2004).
  • Vinoba Bhave established the "Sarvodaya Samaj" in 1948 to propagate Gandhi's ideals (aftermath, post-dates this dossier's window but sourced from the same PYQ material).

Cripps Mission (March 1942)

"A post-dated cheque on a crashing bank"
Cripps Mission
AspectDetail
ContextRising Japanese power; international pressure (USA, China, Australia) on Britain for a political settlement; sent by PM Winston Churchill; Stafford Cripps arrived in India, March 1942, to negotiate.
Main proposalsPost-war Dominion Status for an Indian Union, free within the Commonwealth, with UN participation; a post-war Constituent Assembly (elected members from provincial legislatures + princely-state nominees); provinces not agreeing to the new constitution could keep their present status / sign a separate agreement with Britain; negotiation of a treaty for transfer of power; interim period — Britain retained control over defence and the Governor-General's powers.
Departures from earlier proposalsPlaced constitution-making entirely in Indian hands; acknowledged provinces could form separate unions and exit the Commonwealth; offered a preliminary blueprint for partition.
Congress's official negotiatorsJawaharlal Nehru and Maulana Azad (I.A.S. Pre 2010) — rejected the proposal for withholding complete independence, criticising the princely-states representation method and the right to secession (threatened national unity); opposed the lack of immediate power transfer and retained GG supremacy.
Muslim League critiqueOpposed a single Indian Union; criticised the constituent-assembly procedures; rejected the denial of Pakistan as a separate nation.
Others' concernsLiberals, Hindu Mahasabha, Sikhs, and depressed classes voiced concerns about the right to secession and its impact on India's unity.
Reasons for failureCripps's rigid "take it or leave it" stance; disputes over the Viceroy's veto powers; British opposition — Churchill undermined the mission; uncertainties over the treaty's execution/interpretation.

Aftermath

  • Gandhi described the proposals as "a post-dated cheque on a crashing/falling bank" (both phrasings appear across sources — trap: not Nehru, J.B. Kripalani, or Jaiprakash Narayan).
  • Nehru criticised the mission as a continuation of autocratic rule.
  • The mission's failure escalated Indian frustrations and directly fuelled the demand that culminated in the Quit India Movement.
What the Cripps proposals did NOT include
  • Full independence for India — only Dominion Status was on offer (I.A.S. Pre 2009).
  • A new executive council with equal Hindu-Muslim representation — not part of the actual proposal (UP PCS 2005).
