Part A
The Gupta Empire

Origins, rulers, administration, economy, society, culture and fall of the Guptas.

1

Rise of the Guptas

Origins & Extent

  • The Guptas rose in the middle of the 3rd century AD after the decline of the Satavahanas, Kushanas and Murundas. They were possibly feudatories of the Kushans in Uttar Pradesh, with their power centre at Prayaga.
  • Murundas were kinsmen of the Kushans who ruled central India from 230 to 250 AD after Kushan decline in North India.
  • Many historians hold the Guptas were of Vaishya origin, but there is no scholarly consensus on their varna.
  • The empire was smaller than the Mauryan empire but kept North India united for over a century with a strong central government bringing many kingdoms under its hegemony. Rule extended over Anuganga (middle Gangetic basin), Prayaga, Saketa (Ayodhya), Magadha, large parts of the west and north-west, and the east coast of the Deccan as far as the Pallava kingdom.

Reasons for Rise

  • Fertile land in Madhyadesha (Bihar and UP); access to iron ores of south Bihar and central India; proximity to north Indian areas carrying the silk trade with the Byzantine Empire.

Dating the Dynasty

  • The Gupta dynasty ruled about 275–550 AD, founded by Srigupta (c. 275 AD; the other source writes 240–280 AD), followed by Ghatotkacha (280–319 AD); both used only the title Maharaja. The first powerful ruler was Chandragupta I.
  • The Gupta Samvat was started by Chandragupta I in 319–20 AD (not Srigupta, Chandragupta II or Ghatotkacha).
  • Vikrama era (57/58 BC), Saka era (78 AD, by Kanishka), Gupta era (319 AD; the match question uses 320 AD as the nearest option) and Kali era (3102 BC).
MCQ Trap / Source ConflictThe question 'Gupta dynasty ruled during' has the key (a) 319–500 AD, though the explanation says 275–550 AD — choose the option nearest 275–550. For the first Gupta ruler to issue coins: Chandragupta I, not Srigupta or Ghatotkacha (no evidence of coins), nor Samudragupta (later).
2

Gupta Rulers

Chandragupta I (319–335 AD)

  • Started the Gupta era (319–20 AD); first to take the title Maharajadhiraja (king of kings) in contrast to his predecessors, indicating extensive conquests; the first powerful ruler.
  • Though a Vaishya, he married the Kshatriya Lichchhavi princess Kumaradevi to raise the dynasty's prestige and power; the Lichchhavis were an established gana-sangha between the Ganga and the Nepal Terai.
  • The first Gupta ruler to issue coins — the earliest Gupta coins bear Kumaradevi and Chandragupta I; no inscriptions or coins survive from his reign in the other account.
  • His son Samudragupta is called Lichchhavi-Dauhitra (daughter's son of the Lichchhavis).

Samudragupta (335–375 AD)

  • Chandragupta I's son; followed a policy of conquest (opposite to Ashoka's peace). V.A. Smith (The Early History of India) called him the 'Napoleon of India' — never defeated (not Chandragupta Maurya, Chandragupta II, Ashoka).
  • Conquests in five groups: (1) Ganga–Yamuna doab; (2) Himalayan and frontier states — Assam, Nepal, Bengal, Punjab; (3) the forest kingdoms of the Vindhyas (Atavika rajya); (4) Dakshinapatha — 12 rulers of eastern Deccan and the south, reaching Kanchi where the Pallavas accepted his suzerainty; (5) the Sakas and Kushans (some ruling in Afghanistan).
  • Nine republics of Rajasthan including the Malavas and Yaudheyas accepted his suzerainty.
  • Meghavarman, king of Sri Lanka, sent a missionary asking permission to build a Buddhist temple/monastery at Bodh Gaya — Samudragupta granted it (Chinese source).
  • Performed the Ashvamedha and issued gold and silver coins with the legend 'restorer of the Ashvamedha'; after the sacrifice took the title Prithivyam Apratiratha ('no rival on earth').
  • Ardent Vaishnava yet tolerant; patron of the Buddhist scholar Vasubandhu; Kaviraja (lover of poetry and music) — shown playing the veena on coins. First Gupta ruler to take the title Parama Bhagavata (copper-plate of Samudragupta from Nalanda and Gaya); a title Parakramanka is matched with him (Ashoka–Priyadarsin, Chandragupta II–Vikramaditya, Skandagupta–Kramaditya).
  • Coins: 8 types of gold in one account; 6 gold types in another — Garuda, Archer, Parashu (battle-axe), Ashvamedha, Vyaghra-parakramah, Veena.
MCQ Trap / Source ConflictTitle conflicts: for 'first to assume Parama Bhagavata' UPPSC's key gave Chandragupta II, but UGC and the evidence give Samudragupta. The question 'Prithivyah Pratham Veer' was dropped by UPPSC (the real title is Prithivyam Apratiratha). Coin types: 8 (theory) vs 6 (listed names).

Prayaga Prashasti (Allahabad Pillar Inscription)

  • Composed by Harisena (court poet, minister and Kumaramatya, Sandhivigrahaka and Mahadandanayaka, son of the mahadandanayaka Dhruvabhuti) in classical Sanskrit in Nagari/Brahmi script; a panegyric on Samudragupta — his accession, campaigns in North India and the Deccan, relations with contemporary rulers, and his skills as poet and scholar; compares him to the Purusha. Harisena is not the author of Devichandraguptam (that is Vishakhadatta) and is not Harsha's poet (that is Bana).
  • Engraved on an Ashokan pillar originally at Kaushambi, shifted to Allahabad (Prayagraj) by Akbar. It carries four inscriptions: (1) Ashoka's in Brahmi; (2) the Queen's edict on the charity of Ashoka's wife Karuvaki; (3) Samudragupta's by Harisena (Sanskrit, Brahmi); (4) Jahangir's in Persian (Birbal's name is also there) — the only other ruler's inscription on it is Jahangir's. It is the most important historical document of the classical Gupta age; the pillar gives information about Samudragupta (not Mahapadma Nanda, Chandragupta Maurya, Chandragupta I or II).
  • It names the king of Koshala in the Dakshinapatha expedition as Mahendra. Matched contemporaries: Dhananjaya – Kusthalapura, Nilaraja – Avamukta, Ugrasena – Palakka, Vishnugopa – Kanchi.

Chandragupta II 'Vikramaditya' (375–415 AD)

  • Samudragupta's son; peak of Gupta territorial expansion (highest watermark). Came to power after a succession struggle with his brother Ramagupta — he killed his elder brother Ramagupta and married Dhruvadevi (Vishakhadatta's Devichandraguptam: Ramagupta agrees to surrender his queen Dhruvadevi to a Shaka invader; Chandragupta enters the enemy camp disguised as the queen and kills him, dethrones Ramagupta and marries Dhruvadevi).
  • Other names: Devagupta (Sanchi and Vakataka inscriptions), Devaraja (Sanchi), Devashri, Vikrama, Simhavikrama; title Vikramaditya (first used by a Ujjain ruler in 57 BC). Queens: Kuberanaga (a Naga princess of central India) and Dhruvadevi.
  • Marriage alliances: daughter Prabhavati Gupta married the Vakataka Rudrasena II; after his death she ruled as regent (not recorded in the Udayagiri cave inscription, which is Chandragupta II's).
  • Saka conqueror: killed the last Saka king Rudrasimha III of western India (first decade of the 5th century), took the title Sakari, annexed western Malwa and Kathiawar/Gujarat, and gained the ports of Broach, Sopara, Cambay. Issued silver coins (Rupaka) — first Gupta to do so — weighing ~30–33 grains; dated silver coins are the strongest evidence of the Saka victory.
  • Later defeated the Huns, Kambojas and Kiratas; Ujjain seems made a second capital; the Mehrauli Iron Pillar inscription of King Chandra (identified with him) records his conquests.
  • Mehrauli iron pillar: stands in the courtyard of the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque, Delhi (Qutb complex); King Chandra, devotee of Vishnu, set up the Vishnu-dhvaja on the hill Vishnupada (Vishnugiri near Udayagiri) around 410 AD, with a Garuda at its apex; 4th-century iron that has not rusted.
  • Kalidasa belonged to his reign (not Samudragupta, Ashoka or Chandragupta I). Fa-Hien visited in his reign.

Vakatakas (Allies of Chandragupta II)

  • Dynasty ruled central and southern India from the 3rd century to the late 5th/early 6th century AD (western Deccan). Vindhyashakti is the traditional founder (Ajanta inscriptions call him a great warrior, little else known); his son and successor Pravarasena I (c. 275–335 AD) is the real founder who made it an empire — the only Vakataka ruler to take the imperial title Samrat (also Haritiputra; Pravira in the Puranas, which mention him).
  • His realm stretched from Bundelkhand in the north to the Deccan (Hyderabad) in the south; he defeated the Naga king and extended power over north India; capital Kanchanaka. He performed four Ashvamedhas (not Pushyamitra, Samudragupta or Chandragupta II) and many Vedic sacrifices; the Allahabad pillar mentions Rudrasena I and Prithvisena I. His descendant Pravarasena II wrote the Setubandha. All four statements about Pravarasena (real founder, Bundelkhand–Hyderabad, son of Vindhyashakti, in the Puranas) are correct; Chalukyas of Gujarat (10th century) are far later.

Queens of the Guptas (match)

  • Chandragupta I – Kumaradevi; Samudragupta – Duttadevi; Chandragupta II – Kuberanaga (and Dhruvadevi); Kumaragupta I – Anantadevi.

Fa-Hien (399–414 AD)

  • Chinese Buddhist monk; came in the reign of Chandragupta II (not Harsha, not Kanishka's council), mainly to see the land of the Buddha and collect Buddhist manuscripts; not interested in politics and did not meet the emperor/court (as one account states). His stay in India ~10 years (402–412); whole journey 399–414 AD.
  • Route: by land via Khotan, Kashgar, Gandhara, Punjab (Silk Route through Central Asia); returned by sea via Ceylon and Java. Visited Peshawar, Mathura, Kannauj, Shravasti, Kapilavastu, Kushinagar, Pataliputra, Kashi, Bodh Gaya. Mathura's people were numerous and happy; Pataliputra's rich and prosperous.
  • Described a charitable hospital at Pataliputra (founded by benevolent citizens; food, medicine, doctors for the poor); called the Gangetic valley 'the island of Brahmanism'; mentioned Tamralipti as a Bengal port trading with China, Sri Lanka, Java and Sumatra; noted cowrie shells and dinaras in daily trade.

Kumaragupta I (415–455 AD)

  • Called Mahendraditya (and Sakraditya); son of Chandragupta II and Dhruvadevi; queen Anantadevi. Peaceful and prosperous; issued many coins; performed the Ashvamedha; laid the foundation of Nalanda University. A branch of the Huns tried to cross the Hindu Kush in his reign.
  • Mandsaur / Dashpur inscription (also called the Dashpur inscription), composed by Vatsabhatti (not Bhavabhuti): records the silk-weavers' guild (Tantuvayas) from the Lata region (Gujarat) building a Sun temple at Dashpur, later repaired; governor Bandhuvarman; calls Dashpur Pashchimapura; regarded as the world's earliest advertisement (silk fabrics).

Skandagupta (455–467 AD)

  • Kumaragupta's son and the last great Gupta king; repulsed the first Huna invasion, but renewed attacks strained the treasury. Title Kramaditya.
  • Bhitari pillar inscription (Bhitari, Saidpur tehsil, Ghazipur, UP) describes his war with the Huns (also the Skandagupta record paired with Bhitari). The Junagadh (Girnar) inscription says he appointed governors in every province, including Parnadatta over Saurashtra.
3

Decline of the Guptas & the Huns

The Huna Invasions

  • The first Huna invasion took place in Skandagupta's reign (not Chandragupta II or Kumaragupta I) — the Huns were defeated by him; it was like a raid with little lasting impact but it hastened Gupta decline; the Gupta dynasty was the one most affected. After his death the Huns annexed many north-western regions in the 5th–6th century.
  • Toramana (a Huna) led the second Huna attack; Mihirakula (Central Asian Huna, a Hephthalite emperor; Gwalior inscription shows Gwalior in his territory) was beaten by the Aulikara king Yashodharman of Malwa in 528 AD (other sources 495 AD).
  • By 485 AD the Huns held eastern Malwa and central India. Yashodharman overthrew them and set up victory pillars at Mandsaur in 532 AD claiming conquest of almost all northern India — a severe blow to the Guptas. The Sondani inscription of Yashodharman was composed by Vasula (Vatsula) Kavi.

Causes & End

  • Causes: Huna invasions; feudal set-up and rise of feudatories in Bengal, Bihar and UP; loss of western India; land grants cutting revenue; weak successors (Purugupta, Narasimhagupta, Buddhagupta, Baladitya). The Arab invasion was not a cause (it came much later). Also listed as a cause: acceptance of Buddhism by later Guptas.
  • Vishnugupta (540–550 AD) was the last recognised Gupta ruler. The Later Guptas of Magadha rose in Bihar and the Maukharis at Kanauj; imperial glory ended a century before the dynasty did.
  • Sungyun (Sung-yun), the Chinese pilgrim of the early 6th century, travelled 518–521 AD and collected Buddhist texts over three years (the question's options 515–520, 525–529, 545–552 and 592–597 do not match, so the key is 'none/more than one').
  • After the Guptas: Maitrakas (Gujarat, Valabhi), Maukharis (Agra and Oudh), Vakatakas (western Deccan), Later Guptas of Magadha and Pushyabhutis of Thanesar ruled the north before Harsha; the Paramaras (9th–14th century) and Yadavas of Devagiri (1187–1317) did not.

4

Gupta Administration

Kingship & Polity

  • Rulers took titles Paramabhattaraka, Maharajadhiraja, Parameshvara, Samrat, Chakravartin; epithets Parama-Daivata and Parama-Bhagavata (foremost worshipper of Vasudeva-Krishna). The king was regarded as Vishnu the protector; Samudragupta compared to the Purusha in the Allahabad inscription. Gupta rulers claimed divine origin (more than the Mauryas) and extended land grants; the administration was not highly centralised — a federal/feudal set-up; the Gupta king was king of many smaller kingdoms and feudatories were nearly independent. Feudalism began to take root.
  • Kingship was hereditary, but with no firm primogeniture.
  • The king's council: chief minister, Senapati and key officials; contact with provinces through Kumaramatyas and Ayuktas. Kumaramatya occurs on six Vaishali seals, tied to his own adhikarana; equal to princes in rank, attached to the king, crown prince, revenue department or province; paid in cash; Harisena held several titles.
  • The Guptas needed fewer officials than the Mauryas (less state role in the economy, guilds administered); recruitment not confined to upper varnas; posts became hereditary, weakening royal control.

Army

  • Standing army plus feudatory contingents. The Guptas learned the saddle, reins, buttoned coats, trousers and boots from the Kushans — chariots and elephants took a back seat; cavalry and horse archery came forward. Baladhikrita / Mahabaladhikrita (infantry and cavalry commanders); the term Senapati does not occur in Gupta inscriptions but in some Vakataka ones. A Vaishali seal mentions the Ranabhandagarādhikarana (military storehouse office).

Territorial Divisions

  • Order: Empire (King) → Bhukti/Desha (Uparika) → Vishaya (Vishayapati) → Vithi (Ayuktaka and Vithi-Mahattara) → Grama (Gramika / Ashtakuladhikarana). The sequence 'Bhukti, Vishaya, Vithi, Grama' is correct.
  • Uparikas were appointed directly by the king, ran the military machinery and appointed district heads and boards; the Damodarpur plates mention an Uparika with the title Maharaja. The Eran pillar of Budhagupta (Gupta year 165) calls Maharaja Surashmichandra a Lokapala governing between the Kalindi and Narmada.
  • Vishayapatis were appointed by the governor (sometimes by the king); town notables assisted. Guilds (Shrenis) governed urban affairs, managed their own matters and punished members. Units below district: vithi, bhumi, pathaka, peta. Villagers chose Gramika and Gramadhyaksha; the Damodarpur copper plate of Budhagupta mentions an Ashtakula-Adhikarana (board of eight) headed by the Mahattara; the Sanchi inscription of Chandragupta II mentions a Panchamandali.
  • Feudatories (Samantas) held most of the empire; charters for outlying vassals carried the royal Garuda seal; duties: attend the king, pay tribute, offer daughters in marriage.
5

Gupta Economy

Land, Agriculture & Irrigation

  • Agriculture thrived through irrigation; fenced fields; crops: rice, wheat, barley, peas, lentils, pulses, sugarcane, oilseeds. Kalidasa: the south was famous for pepper and cardamom; Varahamihira gave rules for planting fruit trees. Kamandaka's Nitisara (c. 400 AD) is an Arthashastra-like polity text stressing the treasury and revenue sources.
  • Paharpur copper plate: the king was sole owner of land; the Ustapala kept land-transaction records in the district and the village accountant kept village records.
  • Narada Smriti: two dykes — Bardhya (flood protection) and Khara (irrigation); Jalanirgamah drains; canals from rivers, tanks and lakes. Sudarshana lake at the foot of Girnar, Gujarat, was the most famous. The Araghatta (Persian wheel) = a large wheel with earthen pots tied to its spokes.

Land Grants

  • Fiscal and administrative concessions to priests and officials began under the Satavahanas and became regular under the Guptas; the Gupta dynasty donated the maximum number of villages to temples and Brahmins (more than Palas, Rashtrakutas, Pratiharas). Tax-free land was granted to religious functionaries (Brahmins, not military or civil officials) who could still collect taxes from peasants and punish criminals on that land — priestly landlords rose at the cost of peasants, who faced forced labour and serf-like status; many new areas came under cultivation.
  • Agrahara: to Brahmins, perpetual, hereditary, tax-free. Devagraha: land to a Brahmin and gifts to merchants for temple repair and worship. Secular grants: to feudatories.

Taxation

  • Land tax (Bhaga / Bhoga, Udranga) rose while trade taxes fell; rates 1/4 to 1/6, the standard one-sixth (religious texts and Hindu law also say 1/6). Forced labour Vishti (also Iranyavesti), found mostly in Madhya Pradesh, Kathiawar and Gujarat/Malwa (Junagadh inscription) — an income source, a sort of tax; not wage labour, not weekly paid, not the eldest son only.
  • Udakabhaga / Udakabhoga = irrigation tax; Hiranya = cash tax (Mauryan context) or king's share of certain crops in kind; Uparikara = tax on cultivators without proprietary rights / temporary tenants / extra cess.

Trade, Ports & Guilds

  • Internal trade by road and river with safe roads; the Narada and Brihaspati Smritis describe guilds. Traders: Shresthi (settled) and Sarthavaha (caravan trader; head of trade guild, also called Srenipramukh) — Sarthavaha is a Vaishya. Usury was practised.
  • Guilds (Shrenis) regulated trade, had judicial power over members and fixed wages, rules of work, standards and prices; each had its head and worked under royal recognition without state interference (guilds were not registered with the central authority nor was the king their chief administrator).
  • Ports: east — Tamralipti (Bengal), Kadura, Ghantasala (Andhra); west — Broach (Bhrigukachchha) and Cambay (Gujarat), Kalyan (Calliena) and Chaul (Maharashtra). Tamralipti handled the north Indian trade with South-East Asia, China, Lanka, Java and Sumatra and was the most important; Bharuch, Kalyan and Cambay also served north India; international centres: Tamralipti, Bhrigukachchha, Arikamedu, Kaveripattanam, Muziris, Pratishthana, Sopara, Barbaricum. Monsoon winds best explain the Bay of Bengal sea contacts.
  • Exports: Bengal cotton cloth, Bihar indigo, Banaras silk, Himalayan scents, sandal and spices, pearls, gems, coconuts, ivory. Imports: gold, silver, tin, lead, silk, horses; Roman gold came in. Silk trade with the Eastern Roman Empire declined around 550 AD when Rome learnt sericulture. Textiles (Kausheya = silk) were the principal industry in Gujarat, Bengal, Deccan, Tamil Nadu.

Mining, Metallurgy & Coinage

  • Iron from Bihar, copper from Rajasthan; metals listed include gold, copper, tin, lead, brass, bronze, bell-metal, mica, manganese, antimony, red chalk and red arsenic (mentioned by Amarasimha, Varahamihira, Kalidasa). Warangal was known for iron implements (the Agaria people). The Mehrauli iron pillar shows Gupta craftsmanship.
  • Gold coins = Dinara (not Karshapana, Nishka, Suvarna, Kaudi or Pana); silver coins = Rupaka; gold coin issue large, silver and copper comparatively few; after the mid-5th century the gold content was reduced; gold coin circulation declined in the post-Gupta period. Coins carry legends and symbols telling of titles and sacrifices; Lakshmi appears as Vishnu's wife; designs resemble Greek and Kushan coins; Chandragupta II and successors issued gold, silver and copper. Hoards are found in UP, Bihar, Bengal, MP, Rajasthan and Odisha; Bayana (Bharatpur, Rajasthan) yielded the most. Gandhaiya was a silver coin made on Sassanian lines.

Urban Decline

  • Towns declined gradually — Pataliputra became a village-like ruin by Hiuen Tsang's time; Mathura, Sonpur, Hastinapur, Shravasti show decline.
6

Society, Religion & Law under the Guptas

Society & Women

  • Brahmana supremacy continued with wealth from grants; castes became rigid and multiplied into sub-castes through assimilation of foreigners (the Huns came to be counted as a Rajput clan) and tribes. Shudras became agriculturists, the Vaishya–Shudra gap blurred; untouchability grew and chandalas were segregated.
  • Women's position was poor — only the Vedic age was better; Gupta, Maurya and Chola periods all show inferior status, so equality with men was not found in any age. Women could hear the epics and Puranas; Swayamvara ended; Manusmriti advised early marriage; Sati gained acceptance — the Eran inscription (510 AD) of Bhanugupta gives the first epigraphic evidence of sati for the wife of the war commander Goparaja. Women had few property rights (Prabhavati's land inscription is an exception); changing gotra on marriage dates after the 5th century. Child marriage had started earlier (Kushan period).
  • In Sanskrit plays women and Shudras speak Prakrit, other characters Sanskrit. Chess (Chaturanga) originated in India in the Gupta period and reached Iran and then Europe.

Religion

  • Brahmanism and the worship of Shiva and Vishnu rose; first images of Vishnu and Shiva; Krishna worship began; most Gupta kings were Vaishnavas; Puranas composed; Vishnu a god of devotion and saviour of the varna system (Vishnu Purana, Vishnu Smriti); the Bhagavad Gita appeared in the 4th century AD, teaching devotion to Krishna and performance of varna duty; idol worship common; agricultural festivals got religious colour.
  • Buddhism flourished in the north-west but was neglected in the Gangetic valley, without royal patronage like Ashoka's or Kanishka's. Jainism flourished in the west and south; the Valabhi Jain council compiled the Svetambara canon. Kings were tolerant — no persecution of Buddhists or Jains.

Judicial System

  • Civil and criminal law clearly separated for the first time: theft and adultery criminal, property cases civil; elaborate inheritance laws; courts such as Karana, Adhikarana and Dharmasana; the king tried cases with Brahmana priests; guilds had their own laws; laws varied by varna (higher varna lighter punishment); punishments were mild, fines common.
7

Gupta Art, Literature & Science

Art & Architecture

  • The Gupta period is the Golden Age of ancient India for art and culture (not Mauryan, Shunga or Magadha); art mostly religious.
  • Buddhist: bronze Buddha at Sultanganj (near Bhagalpur); Buddha images from Mathura and Sarnath (standing stone Buddha of Sarnath); copper Buddha at Nalanda; life-like Ajanta paintings of the Buddha's life; paintings at Bagh caves (Dhar, MP); Sigiriya (Sri Lanka) painting influenced by Ajanta; stupas at Samat (UP), Ratnagiri (Odisha), Mirpur Khas (Sindh).
  • Temples: Nagara and Dravida styles evolved but most was lost to foreign invasions like the Huns; no Gandhara influence; the main god large in the centre with smaller gods around. Brick temples at Bhitargaon (Kanpur), Bhitari (Ghazipur), Nachna-Kuthara (Panna), Deogarh (Jhansi); sculptures at Garhwa near Allahabad; the Varaha at Udayagiri cave; earliest brick structures of Nalanda. Clay figurines served religious and secular purposes; red ware pottery was typical (sites: Ahichchhatra, Rajgarh, Hastinapur, Bashar).

Language & Literature

  • Sanskrit became the official language (all inscriptions) in the last phase of Smriti literature; the Nagari script evolved from Brahmi. Prakrit forms: Shauraseni (Mathura), Ardhamagadhi (Awadh, Bundelkhand), Magadhi (Bihar). Plays were comedies (higher and lower classes spoke different languages).
  • Bhasa wrote 13 plays: Pratijna-Yaugandharayana, Svapnavasavadatta, Charudatta, Pancharatra, Madhyama-vyayoga, Pratima-nataka, Duta-vakya, Duta-Ghatotkacha, Karnabhara, Urubhanga, Avimaraka, Balacharita, Abhisheka.
  • Present form of the 18 Puranas (Bhagavata, Vishnu, Vayu, Matsya most important), the Mahabharata and Ramayana were completed; commentaries on Smritis began after the Guptas. Buddhist writers: Aryadeva, Asanga, Vasubandhu, Dignaga (Pali earlier, Sanskrit later; Vasubandhu wrote the first regular Buddhist work on logic). Jain: Vimala (Jain Ramayana), Siddhasena Divakara (logic).
  • Other sources: Narada, Vishnu, Brihaspati, Katyayana smritis; Kamandaka's Nitisara. Pravarasena II of the Vakatakas wrote Setubandha.
  • Kalidasa: Abhijnanashakuntalam (among the world's best 100 works; among the earliest Indian works translated into European languages), plays Malavikagnimitram (Agnimitra and the dancer Malavika) and Vikramorvashiyam; epics Raghuvamsha, Kumarasambhava; lyrics Ritusamhara, Meghaduta.
  • Vishakhadatta: Devichandraguptam (Ramagupta–Dhruvadevi story) and Mudrarakshasa (rise of Chandragupta Maurya).

  • Zero and the decimal system are credited to thinkers of this age. Part B
The Post-Gupta Period & Harshavardhana

Fragmented North India, the Pushyabhutis and Harsha, Chinese pilgrims, and the Shankara era.

9

North India after the Guptas

Successor Kingdoms

  • After the fall North India fragmented: Maitrakas (Gujarat; capital Valabhi), Maukharis (Agra and Oudh; capital Kannauj), Vakatakas (western Deccan), Later Guptas of Magadha, and the Pushyabhutis of Thanesar (between the Sutlej and Yamuna, north of Delhi), who founded the Vardhana dynasty.
  • Maukharis: feudatories of the Guptas originally from Gaya; capital Kannauj; main rulers Harivarman, Adityavarman, Ishvaravarman (Ishanavarman), Sarvavarman, Grahavarman. Inscriptions: Sarvavarman — copper seal found at Asirgarh (north of Burhanpur, MP), not a 'Gaya copper plate'; Ishvaravarman — Jaunpur stone inscription; Ishanavarman — Harha stone inscription; Jivitagupta II — Deva-Baranark inscription. The Maukhari line ended about 606 AD when the Later Guptas reconquered Kannauj.
  • Maitrakas of Valabhi ruled Gujarat from the late 5th to late 8th century; the Pushyabhutis ruled in the 6th–7th century. Pushyabhuti (not Rajyavardhana, Adityavardhana or Prabhakaravardhana) laid the foundation of the Vardhana dynasty at Thanesar (per Bana's Harshacharita); they began as military subordinates of the Guptas.
  • Sthanesvara (Thanesar) is a historic pilgrimage town in Haryana on the Saraswati; Bhilsa (Vidisha) is in MP, Girinagar (Girnar) in Gujarat, Dvarasamudra the Hoysala capital (Karnataka).

Early Vardhanas

  • Prabhakaravardhana (580–605 AD): fought the Gurjaras and Huns, extended influence to Malwa and Gujarat; married his daughter Rajyashri to the Maukhari king Grahavarman of Kannauj. His wife Yashomati Devi immolated herself after his death (sati).
  • Rajyavardhana (605–606): treacherously killed by Shashanka (Sasanka), the Gauda king of Bengal (capital Karnasuvarna, West Bengal; Murshidabad region); his younger brother Harsha took the throne.
10

Harshavardhana (606–647 AD)

Accession & Capital

  • Became king at 16, called the last great Hindu ruler of Northern India; began a ~30-year campaign for supremacy and vengeance on Shashanka. His reign started as a rescue of his widowed sister Rajyashri from sati in the Vindhya forests, which gave him both Thanesar and Kannauj.
  • The magnates of Kannauj invited him to rule; he accepted on the advice of Avalokiteshvara Bodhisattva with the titles Rajaputra and Shiladitya. He moved the capital from Thanesar to Kannauj (Chinese sources say Harsha and Rajyashri ruled together) — suitable for his large empire; Kannauj became the main commercial centre of the post-Gupta period (replacing Ujjain), attracting Palas, Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas.

Wars & Empire

  • Killed Devagupta of Malwa who coveted Rajyashri; allied with Kamarupa (Assam) against Shashanka and, after Shashanka's death, subjugated Gauda. Bhaskaravarman (the last Varman king) ruled Kamarupa (Pragjyotishpura, present Assam) in his time.
  • His southward march was stopped on the Narmada by the Chalukya Pulakeshin II (not Pulakeshin I or Vikramaditya I/II or Kirtivarman II), who took the title Parameshvara. Sources: Aihole inscription by Ravikirti (634 AD) and Hiuen Tsang; Badami inscriptions. Date: between 630–634 AD in one account; 618 AD by a BORI claim from a Pulakeshin II copper plate (the Jharkhand key accepts 618).
  • Hostility with the Maitrakas ended when Dhruvabhatta married Harsha's daughter, making Valabhi a subordinate ally.
  • Claimed extent: Assam, Bengal, Bihar, Kannauj, Malwa, Odisha, Punjab, Kashmir, Nepal, Sindh; real power only the compact territory between the Ganga and Yamuna. China: friendly with the Tang emperor Tai Tsung (Taizong), who sent embassies in 643 and 647 AD.

Administration

  • More feudal and decentralised than the Guptas; a Mantri Parishad. Officers: Avanti (foreign relations and war), Simhananda (commander-in-chief), Kuntala (chief cavalry), Skandagupta (elephant force), Banu (keeper of records), Dirghadhvajas (messengers), Mahapratihara (palace guard), Sarvagata (secret service).
  • Revenue: Bhaga (1/6 in kind), Hiranya (cash from farmers and traders), Bali; traders paid duties at ferries and barrier stations; land grants to officials began; revenue split in four: state affairs, ministers/officers, men of letters, religious charity.
  • Judicial: stricter than Gupta times — banishment, limb-cutting, trial by ordeal (fire, water, poison), life imprisonment; Mimamsakas dispensed justice; roads and river routes were not free from robbery (Hiuen Tsang was robbed).
  • Divisions: provinces → Bhukti → Vishaya → Pathaka; local officers (Harshacharita): Bhogapati, Ayuktaka, Pratapalaka-purusha.
  • Pataliputra declined and Kannauj rose: trade, coins and tolls declined at Pataliputra, power shifted to military camps (Skandhavaras); Kannauj stood on high ground, easily fortified, in the middle of the doab controlling east and west. Free hospitals and dharamshalas were founded. Army (chaturanga): Chatas, Bhatas (ordinary soldiers), Brihadishvaras (cavalry officers), Baladhikritas, Mahabaladhikritas (infantry officers).

Religion & Society

  • Began as a Shiva worshipper, embraced Mahayana Buddhism under his sister Rajyashri (Hiuen Tsang's influence added patronage — he was not hostile to Buddhism; he leaned to it earlier). He treated Vedic scholars and Buddhist monks equally; slaughter and meat-eating restricted.
  • Kannauj assembly: 20 kings attended including Bhaskaravarman; presided by Harsha; Buddhist, Jain and Vedic scholars; a golden Buddha statue; held to prove Mahayana's excellence. Prayaga assembly (Mahamoksha Parishad): every five years at the Ganga–Yamuna sangam with lavish gifts; Hiuen Tsang attended the 6th, with 18 kings.
  • Caste (Hiuen Tsang): four varnas; farmers called Shudras; untouchables (scavengers, executioners) lived outside and announced their entry; butchers, fishermen, dancers, sweepers outside the city; rigid caste but no conflict; people honest. Women: purdah mostly not among higher classes (Rajyashri didn't veil); sati existed. Diet: mostly vegetarian; onion and garlic rare; sugar, milk, ghee, rice common; beef and some meats forbidden.
  • Education in monasteries (Kannauj, Gaya, Jalandhar, Manipur and others), religious; Vedas taught orally; Sanskrit for the learned.

Harsha's Literature

  • Patron of Bana — Harshacharita (first formal royal biography; main source on the Vardhanas) and Kadambari; Bana was born at Prithikuta on the Son (Aurangabad, Bihar; some say Kannauj), father Chitrabhanu, mother Rajadevi; Kalhana's Rajatarangini also mentions Harsha; no information in Kalidasa or Harisena. Harsha wrote Priyadarshika, Ratnavali (Holi festival mentioned) and Nagananda.
  • Epigraphic sources: Madhuban copper plate, Sonpat copper seal, Banskhera copper plate, Nalanda clay seals.
11

Hiuen Tsang, Nalanda & Other Chinese Travellers

Hiuen Tsang (Yuan Chwang, 629/630–645 AD)

  • Chinese Buddhist monk who came in Harsha's reign; left Changan (Tang capital) in 629 AD (via the Silk Route), despite Taizong's ban on foreign travel (the emperor did not send him; he welcomed him back after 13 years and made him a spiritual adviser); the 'prince of pilgrims'; account Si-Yu-Ki / Sei-Yu-Kei (Buddhist Records of the Western World), the reason he is remembered. Visited Nalanda in 637 AD, spent ~five years there, learned Yogashastra for about 1.5 years under the vice-chancellor Shilabhadra, and visited Bhinmal. He carried back Buddha relics, images and manuscripts; Harsha admired him.
  • Observations: Mathura famous for cotton textiles, Varanasi for silk; Thanesar prospered from trade; Ujjayini and Kannauj were prosperous; the Sun temple of Multan is mentioned by Hiuen Tsang, Al-Masudi and Al-Biruni, and the pilgrimage to Sambapura was linked to Sun worship; Pataliputra in ruins.

Nalanda under Harsha

  • Peak under Harsha; attracted scholars from China, Japan, Mongolia, Tibet, Central and South-East Asia (and Greece and Persia per another account); teachers Dharmapala, Chandrapala, Shilabhadra, Bhadrihari, Jayasena, Devakara, Matanga; supported by the revenue of 200 villages; I-Tsing visited in 670 AD. Nalanda was destroyed by Muslims (Bakhtiyar Khalji), not Kushans, Scythians or Mughals.

Post-Harsha & Post-Gupta Features

Society, Economy & Dynasties

  • Feudalism was the main feature of the post-Gupta agrarian structure: large grants to officials, temples and nobles created landed intermediaries and dependent peasants who paid rent and service — essential elements were a land-based administrative structure and a lord–vassal relationship (not a very strong central power with weak local rulers). Indian feudalism began in the Saka–Kushan period, peaked under the Rajputs, and differed from Europe's (no master–slave bond). Hundi was a bill of exchange in post-Harsha sources. Harsha's kingdom disintegrated into small states after his death. After Harsha, Kannauj was called Mahodaya/Mahodayashri; the tripartite struggle of Palas, Gurjara-Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas (8th–9th century) for it ended in Pratihara success.
  • Pratiharas: Agnikula clan, Gurjara-Pratiharas, first mention of the Gurjara in Pulakeshin II's Aihole text and in the Harshacharita; founder Nagabhata I (730–756 AD), whose Gwalior inscription records destroying a large Mlechchha (Arab, Sindh) army. Rashtrakutas: founded 753 AD by Dantidurga, capital Manyakheta, performed the Hiranyagarbha (Mahadana) yajna at Ujjaini; Amoghavarsha I born 800 AD at Shribhavan camp on the Narmada when Govinda III returned from the north.
  • Chandravamsha (Somavamsha): prime Kshatriya dynasty in the Puranas; chief place Prayaga, then — after a calamity — King Samvarana moved the capital to Pratishthanapura (present Jhunsi, Prayagraj).

Shankaracharya & the Four Mathas

Adi Shankara

  • Born at Kaladi, Kerala, in the 8th century; philosophy Advaita Vedanta; revived Hinduism against the spread of Buddhism and Jainism (hence 'Crypto-Buddhist' for the Buddhist concepts in his thought); he did not name Prayaga 'Teerthraj'.
  • Four mathas: Sringeri (Karnataka, south), Dwarka (Gujarat, west), Jagannath Puri (Odisha, east), Jyotirmath / Badrikashrama (Joshimath, Uttarakhand, north). Chardham = Badrinath, Dwarka, Puri, Rameshwaram; the Uttarakhand 'little' Char Dham = Gangotri, Yamunotri, Kedarnath, Badrinath; Mansarovar is not in the Chardham.

Part C
Indian Philosophy — The Six Schools

Goals of life, the six astika darshanas, Vedanta sub-schools, Charvaka and the Nastika schools.

15

Goals of Life & the Six Schools

Four Goals (Purusharthas)

  • Once the state and varna order were firm, thinkers set four goals: dharma (social regulation), artha (economic resources), kama (physical pleasure) and moksha (salvation). Each got a text: Arthashastra by Kautilya (economy), Dharmashastra (laws of state and society), Kamasutra (pleasure) — all concerned with the material world, touching salvation only marginally.
  • Moksha — deliverance from the cycle of birth and death, first recommended by Gautama Buddha and later stressed by some Brahmanical thinkers — became the central subject of the darshana (philosophy) texts.

The Six Astika Schools

  • By the beginning of the Christian era six schools had developed: Samkhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa (Purva Mimamsa), Vedanta (Uttara Mimamsa) — the astika schools (originally Sanatana Dharma), each with a nucleus of sutras. Lokayata (Charvaka) and Kapalika are not among them.
  • The first four accept Vedic authority but derive their principles from sages' teachings, not the Vedas; Purva Mimamsa and Vedanta base their theology on the Vedas themselves.
  • Four Vedas (Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva) each have four parts: Samhita, Brahmana, Aranyaka, Upanishad — the first two ritualistic; Aranyakas mark the shift from ritual to theology, culminating in the Upanishads. Purva Mimamsa ('earlier deliberation') rests on the Samhitas and Brahmanas; Vedanta ('last part of the Vedas') on the Upanishads, hence Uttara Mimamsa.

Samkhya & Yoga

Samkhya

  • 'Samkhya' literally means 'count'; probably the earliest school, founded by Kapila Muni — the earliest exponent; Sankhya Sutra and Tattvasamasa (a late 14th–15th-century text of uncertain authorship) are traditionally attributed to him. Early Samkhya held creation owed more to prakriti than God (a rational, scientific view) — man's life is shaped by the forces of nature, not any divine agency; around the 4th century AD purusha was added, so the school moved from materialistic to spiritualistic.
  • Two principles: prakriti (primordial matter, creative agency, energy; inert, evolves from subtle to gross — 24 elements constitute matter) and purusha (individual conscious being, atma; immutable, eternal). Jiva = purusha bonded to prakriti. A dualism of self and matter, not mind and body.
  • Salvation through real knowledge that purusha differs from prakriti, acquired by perception (pratyaksha), inference (anumana) and hearing (shabda); ignorance (nescience) causes sorrow; self-knowledge, not external agent, liberates. Sankhya accepts rebirth/transmigration. God's existence is not asserted nor considered relevant. Its doctrine Satkaryavada says the effect pre-exists in the cause (clay → pot).

Yoga

  • Founded by Hiranyagarbha and systematised by Patanjali (the founder/propagator of Yoga Darshan, hence Patanjali Darshan; yoga is also described in the Upanishads, Mahabharata, Bhagavad Gita and Jain and Buddhist texts). Patanjali was contemporary of Pushyamitra Shunga (184–148 BC). Yoga gives practical steps to realise purusha as distinct from prakriti; salvation via meditation and physical application; accepts Samkhya's 25 principles plus Ishvara as the 26th (more theistic); goal kaivalya.
  • Yoga = cessation of mental modifications. Ashtanga (eight limbs): Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama, Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi — reminiscent of the Buddhist Noble Eightfold Path. Anusmriti is not a limb. The Antaranga (internal) sadhana = the last three limbs: Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi (Pratyahara, Niyama, Pranayama are not internal). Asanas and pranayama imply some knowledge of anatomy and physiology.
17

Nyaya, Vaisheshika & Mimamsa

Nyaya

  • School of analysis based on the Nyaya Sutras of Gautama (Akshapada) (2nd century BC per the second source); Nyaya means decision/logic; recognises 16 padarthas; its logic was adopted by most schools. Salvation by knowledge, tested through perception, inference, comparison and testimony; suffering comes from activity under wrong knowledge. Example: smoke on the hill → fire (as in a hearth). Unlike Buddhism, it believes in soul and self. In Nyaya, jnana is synonymous with buddhi (cognition) and upalabdhi (apprehension); means of knowledge = pramana. Later Navya-Nyaya, founded by the Mithila scholars Gangesha and Udayana (Udayanacharya). Vaisheshika and Nyaya eventually merged.

Vaisheshika

  • Founded by Kanada (6th century BC per theory) — atomist and pluralist; founder of Indian atomism: matter is made of paramanu, the smallest, invisible, indivisible unit; this was the beginning of physics in India. Stress on dravya: earth, water, fire, air, ether combine into new objects; Brahman as the force that causes consciousness in atoms. Seven padarthas: dravya, guna, karma, samanya, vishesha, samavaya, abhava. Accepts only perception and inference. Diluted by belief in god, heaven and salvation.

Purva Mimamsa

  • Propagated by Jaimini; means reasoning and interpretation of the Vedas, used to justify Vedic ritual; the theory of karma belongs to it (also called Karma, Purva or Dharma Mimamsa); the Vedas hold eternal truth, and their essence is dharma; Purva Mimamsa concerns karmakanda, Uttara Mimamsa jnanakanda. Doctrine of Apurva — an unseen force mediating between deeds and results. Kumarila Bhatta links Purva Mimamsa and Vedanta. Merit earns heaven until it is exhausted; sacrifices needed priests and legitimised varna distance, preserving Brahmanical hierarchy.
18

Vedanta & Its Sub-Schools

Vedanta

  • 'End of the Veda'; focuses on the Upanishads (meditation, self-discipline) and contends the Vedas teach realising Brahman, not dharma as injunction. Prasthanatrayi: Upanishads, Brahmasutra, Bhagavad Gita. Badarayana's Brahmasutra (2nd century BC) is the basic text; commentaries by Shankara (8th–9th century) and Ramanuja (11th–12th century). Brahma is real, all else maya; atma = brahma; karma and rebirth link in; stability of the social order is implied. Vedanta is the apex of the six systems. Abhinavagupta is not a Vedantin; the great Vedantins are Shankara (8th), Ramanuja (11th) and Madhva (13th century).
  • Six sub-schools: Advaita, Vishishtadvaita, Dvaita, Dvaitadvaita, Shuddhadvaita, Achintya Bhedabheda; grouped as impersonal (Advaita) and personal (devotion to a Personal God; Absolute Truth = Bhagavan/Purushottama; the world real; jivas never non-different).
  • Shankara's Brahman is homogeneous and impersonal (all plurality illusory); Ramanuja's Brahman contains plurality within itself and is personal.

Charvaka & the Nastika Schools

Charvaka / Lokayata

  • Ancient Indian materialism, originally Lokayata; Ajita Kesakambali was a forerunner. Accepts only direct perception (empiricism, conditional inference), rejects the Vedas, ritualism and supernaturalism; the soul equals the body (Dehatmavada: 'a body without consciousness is a corpse, consciousness without body is not a soul'); denies the afterlife — 'live well as long as you live, even by borrowing, for once cremated there is no return'. The highest value is Kama (pleasure); moksha is merely death (Sarvasiddhanta Samgraha); no austerity. Bauddha is a Nastika school (does not accept the Vedas); the astika schools are Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, etc.
