The Earliest Reform Voice — Raja Ram Mohan Roy

Before any organisation existed
  • Raja Ram Mohan Roy is widely regarded as the guide of social reform and political movement in Modern India, and — per Bipin Chandra Pal — the first Indian political reformer (48th–52nd BPSC); some historians instead credit this "first" tag to Surendranath Banerjee, so exams sometimes test this as a debated claim rather than a settled fact.
  • He wrote on human rights and democratic values, and personally lobbied in England against Company excesses.
  • Bangabhasha Prakasika Sabha (1836), Calcutta — India's first reform-adjacent society, started by Ram Mohan Roy's associates, not by him in person.
Context — the timeline gap the PYQ book doesn't spell out Ram Mohan Roy died in 1833 in Bristol, England — three years before the Bangabhasha Prakasika Sabha was even founded (1836). So when a question calls him "the earliest individual to struggle for political reform," it refers to his decades of individual agitation (1815 onward: press freedom, sati abolition, judicial reform, petitions to Parliament) — not to founding this specific Sabha, which was assembled by his circle after his death to carry his agenda forward.

The Landholders' Lineage: Zamindari Association → British Indian Association

India's first true political organisation, and how it merged into a bigger one

Zamindari Association / Landholders' Society (1837/1838)

  • Formally launched in Calcutta, March 1838 (some sources date its origin to 1837 — both years appear across PYQs, so treat "1837–38" as the safe window).
  • Founders: Dwarkanath Tagore, Radhakant Deb, Prasanna Kumar Tagore, Rajkamal/Ramkamal Sen, Bhabani Charan Mitra — all leading Bengal zamindars.
  • Marks the beginning of organised political activity and constitutional agitation in India — the first Indian body to use petitions and lawful persuasion rather than personal appeal (I.A.S. 1993; Uttarakhand PCS 2010).
  • Aim: protect landlord interests — extend the Permanent Settlement all over India, halt resumption of rent-free tenures, reform judiciary/police/revenue departments.
  • Maintained close ties with the British India Society of London (see Ch.3) and appointed its president, George Thompson, as its own London agent.
  • Membership was open to non-official Britons in trade too — but ordinary raiyats (peasants) could never join; it remained an exclusive zamindar/comprador club.
  • Confined to the Bengal Presidency (Permanent Settlement did not extend elsewhere); began declining from 1842, effectively closed by 1850.

Bengal British India Society (1843)

  • Founded in Calcutta, 1843, by George Thompson — a Briton who had already set up the British India Society (1839) in London.
  • Other associated names: Dwarkanath Tagore, Chandra Mohan Chatterjee, Parmananda Maitra.
  • Unlike the introverted, purely zamindar-interest Landholders' Society, this body was dominated by "Young Bengal" intellectuals with Western education.
  • Objective: collect and disseminate information on people's welfare, and pursue "just rights" through peaceful, lawful, loyalist means.

British Indian Association (1851) — the merger

  • Established 29 October 1851, Calcutta, by merging the Landholders' Society and the Bengal British India Society.
  • Immediate trigger: European community opposition to four 1849 "Black Act" bills (drafted by law member Bethune) that extended East India Company criminal-court jurisdiction over British-born subjects — Indians supported the bills, Europeans protested them.
  • First President: Radhakant Deb; first General Secretary: Debendranath Tagore; other key members — Ramgopal Ghosh, Peary Chand Mitra, Krishnadas Pal.
  • Petitioned Parliament for: a separate legislature of a popular character; separation of executive from judicial functions; reduction of salaries/taxes; establishment of universities in the three Presidencies.
  • In 1859, despite its landed-aristocracy leanings, it notably refused to back Indigo planters seeking repeal of Act X of 1859, siding instead with the ryots.
Two overlapping "first political organisation" claims
  • Uttarakhand PCS 2010 / I.A.S. 1993 pick Zamindari Association (1838) as "the first political organization established in India" — not British India Society, Bengal British India Society, or the Settlers Association (all distractor options in that question).
  • First President of the British Indian Association was Radhakant Deb (Chhattisgarh PCS 2020) — do not confuse this with the presidency of the earlier, separate Zamindari Association.

Bombay & Poona Organisations

From the "Maker of Modern Bombay" to the Ranade-led Sabha

Bombay Association (1852)

  • Founded 26 August 1852, Bombay, by Jagannath Shankarsheth ("Nana Shankarsheth") — Bombay Presidency's first political organisation.
  • Prominent participants/office-bearers included Sir Jamsetjee Jejeebhoy, Dr. Bhau Daji, and a young Dadabhai Naoroji.
  • Petitioned Parliament for a new legislative council with Indian representation, and criticised the exclusion of Indians from senior administrative posts, plus excessive spending on European postings; some recommendations were folded into the Charter Act of 1853 (which added six legislative members to the Governor-General's council).
  • Revived in 1876 by Dadabhai Naoroji after a period of dormancy — this is the resolution to the "who founded Bombay Association" contradiction across PYQs: Shankarsheth founded it (1852); Naoroji later revived/re-energised it (1876), so both names correctly attach to the same body at different moments, and a matching-question answer key that pairs "Bombay Association — Dadabhai Naoroji" is referring to the revival, not the original 1852 founding.
  • Eventually overshadowed by the newer Bombay Presidency Association (1885).

Poona Sarvajanik Sabha

  • One of the strongest 19th-century Indian political organisations, based in Pune, Maharashtra.
  • Founding effort credited to G.V. Joshi, S.H. Sathe, S.H. Chiplonkar — most PYQ explanations date the actual launch to 2 April 1870, though the reference table lists 1867 as the founding year, so expect either date in different question sets.
  • M.G. Ranade joined and infused it with fresh energy in November 1871 (66th & 70th BPSC Re-exams both confirm 1871 for Ranade's entry regardless of the Sabha's own founding-year debate) — he is the name most PYQs attach to its leadership, even though he was not among the original founders.
  • In 1875, it presented a petition to the British House of Commons demanding India's direct representation in the British Parliament — the correct answer whenever this specific 1875 petition is asked about (I.A.S. 2002), ruling out the Deccan Association, Indian Association, and Madras Mahajan Sabha as distractors.
  • Maintained strong working ties with the Bombay Presidency Association after 1885.

Bombay Presidency Association (1885)

  • Founded January 1885, Bombay, by three co-founders together: Pherozeshah Mehta, K.T. Telang, Badruddin Tyabji — nicknamed the "Trimurti of Bombay" / "Three bright boys of Bombay."
  • Direct trigger: Lord Lytton's reactionary policies and the Ilbert Bill controversy of the early 1880s.
  • Succeeded the (by-then largely dormant) Bombay Association; worked closely with the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha; after the Congress's 1886 Calcutta session under Naoroji, it helped form the Bombay Provincial Congress Committee.
  • Pherozeshah Mehta served as its Secretary; Badruddin Tyabji went on to be the 3rd President of the INC (1887) — the same man who co-founded this association.
The "Naoroji vs Mehta" matching-question conflict — resolved One BPSC re-exam matching set pairs "Bombay Presidency Association — Dadabhai Naoroji" in its official key, while the overwhelming majority of PYQs and every independent source confirm Mehta, Telang, and Tyabji as its actual founders. The likely reason for that outlier key: Naoroji was Mehta's lifelong mentor and had financially backed Mehta's earlier work for the East India Association (1866), and Naoroji presided over the 1886 Calcutta Congress session under whose leadership the Association's provincial-committee work took shape — so the two names are historically entangled, even though Naoroji was not literally one of the three founders. If your exam question is phrased "who founded," go with Mehta/Telang/Tyabji; if it echoes a specific answer key pairing it with Naoroji, that key is treating Naoroji's mentorship/patronage as founding influence.

Bengal/Calcutta Political Bodies — the Direct Precursors to Congress

Indian League (1875)

  • Founded in Calcutta, 1875, by Shishir Kumar Ghosh (spelled Sisir Kumar Ghosh in some lists).
  • Purpose: promote nationalism and political education among Indians, aiming to extend political participation beyond the elite landholding class that earlier bodies represented.

Indian Association (1876) — the most important pre-Congress body

  • Founded 26 July 1876, Calcutta, by Surendranath Banerjee with Anand Mohan Bose — widely called the most important political association before Congress, effectively an All-India Nationalist Organisation in ambition (45th BPSC; 48th–52nd BPSC).
  • Deliberately positioned to include the middle class, not just landlords — a direct contrast with the earlier Zamindari-driven bodies.
  • Led campaigns for ICS reform (raising the age limit for the Indian Civil Service exam, holding it simultaneously in India) and Indianisation of services; protested repressive laws; organised the All-India Conference (1883); published the Indian Association's Journal.
  • Banerjee also published The Bengalee newspaper to build mass political consciousness, and was elected Congress President twice (1895 Poona, 1902 Ahmedabad).
Context — Surendranath Banerjee's Civil Service saga
  • Banerjee cleared the ICS exam in his first attempt (1869), but was disqualified over a dispute about his exact declared age.
  • He appealed, won in court, and cleared the exam again in 1871, and was posted to Sylhet.
  • He was then dismissed from the ICS in 1874 for a minor judicial/procedural error (60th–62nd BPSC gives 1874; a separate PYQ list mistakenly shows 1874 as one of four options where the "correct" marked answer is actually the first option corresponding to 1874 — so 1874 is the settled dismissal year, not the 1869 disqualification year, which was a separate earlier episode).
  • This personal grievance against arbitrary British administrative power is often cited as part of what radicalised Banerjee into founding the Indian Association two years later, in 1876.

All-India Conference (1883) & the Indian National Conference

  • Organised by the Indian Association of Calcutta in December 1883 — the first attempt at all-India political coordination, and a direct precursor of the INC (60th–62nd BPSC).
  • A second conference of this "Indian National Conference" met in Calcutta in December 1885, presided over by Surendranath Banerjee — which is why Banerjee, despite being the era's most prominent Bengal leader, was not present at the actual founding session of the INC (also held in late December 1885, but in Bombay) — he was busy chairing his own parallel conference in Calcutta at the same time (U.P.P.C.S. 1997).
  • This body merged into the INC in 1886 once it became clear their ideology and methods were near-identical — a logical, integrative step for Indian political unity (U.P.P.C.S. Mains 2007).

Sir Syed Ahmad Khan's wider loyalist project

  • Was in judicial service during the Revolt of 1857, and remained fundamentally loyal to British rule thereafter — he believed Muslim community advancement was possible only under British government (a stance the drain-of-wealth theorists explicitly rejected — cross-refer economic policy notes).
  • Published Rajbhakt Muslim to express loyalty to the British, and propagated his ideology through Tehzeeb-ul-Akhlaq.
  • Established the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College, Aligarh, teaching Western subjects, science, and Muslim religious education together.
  • Never associated with the Indian National Congress at any point (Uttarakhand PCS) — among Firozshah Mehta, Hakim Ajmal Khan, Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan, Syed Ahmad Khan, only the last was never connected to the INC.

Context — the many "Indian ___" name variants, untangled once and for all
  • British India Society (1839, London, William Adam) — the earliest Britain-based body.
  • Bengal British India Society (1843, Calcutta, George Thompson) — its Calcutta cousin/extension.
  • British Indian Association (1851, Calcutta, Radhakant Deb) — merger of Landholders' Society + Bengal British India Society.
  • National Indian Association (1870, Bristol → London, Mary Carpenter) — social/educational, women's education focus.
  • Indian Society (1872, linked to Anand Mohan Bose) — a distinct, smaller precursor body.
  • Indian Association (1876, Calcutta, Surendranath Banerjee & Anand Mohan Bose) — the major political body, most-tested in PYQs.
  • London Indian Society (1885, London, Dadabhai Naoroji) — a students' platform, later folded into the East India Association.
Six almost-identical names, six different bodies, spanning 46 years — the safest exam strategy is to always anchor each name to its founder + city + year triplet rather than the name alone.

The Founding of the Indian National Congress

  • Allan Octavian Hume — a retired British Indian Civil Service officer — established the Indian National Union in 1884, the direct precursor body to the INC.
  • Objective: obtain a share in government for educated Indians; create a platform for civil-political dialogue between educated Indians and the British Raj.
  • The first meeting was originally scheduled for Poona, but a cholera outbreak forced a shift to Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay.
  • First session held 28–30 December 1885; on Dadabhai Naoroji's advice, the "Indian National Union" was renamed the Indian National Congress in this very session.
  • 72 delegates attended; presided over by Womesh Chandra Bonnerjee (W.C. Bonnerjee), who became the first INC President; A.O. Hume became the first General Secretary (never President himself).
  • Viceroy at the time: Lord Dufferin (1884–88) — he mocked Congress as representing only a "microscopic minority" of Indians.

Objectives stated by W.C. Bonnerjee, 1885

  • Promote contact and friendship among those defending the country's interests.
  • Inculcate a feeling of national unity by removing caste, sect, and regional biases.
  • Unite all classes and communities of India into a sense of one nation.
  • Formulate and present public demands to government.
  • Not an original 1885 objective: raising social-reform issues from the Congress platform — early Congress deliberately kept social questions off its agenda (66th BPSC Re-exam).

In its first stage, Congress's core demands included: expanding and empowering Legislative Councils with more Indian representatives; separating the Executive from the Judiciary; repealing the Arms Act; and securing freedom of speech — not the unification of Executive and Judiciary, which would have run directly opposite to its actual demand (M.P.PCS 2023).

Context — the "Safety Valve Theory" debate
  • Lala Lajpat Rai, writing in Young India, was the first Indian leader to use the "safety valve theory" to attack the Moderates — the idea that Congress was Lord Dufferin's "brainchild," created to let the British use it as a controlled outlet ("safety valve") for Indian political discontent, protecting the government from more dangerous, uncontrolled unrest (69th BPSC 2023; 71st BPSC 2025).
  • Separately, Lajpat Rai also wrote that the "Congress movement was neither inspired by the people, nor devised or planned by them" (47th BPSC) — a related but distinct critique about Congress's top-down, elite origins.
  • Both quotes come from the same critical, extremist-leaning assessment of the Moderate-led early Congress — they are not contradictory, just two angles (British manipulation vs. elite disconnection from the masses) of the same argument.

Famous declarations, tied to their exact context

  • "Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it" — Bal Gangadhar Tilak, at the 1916 Lucknow session (the same session as the Lucknow Pact).
  • "The Congress is tottering to its fall, and one of my greatest ambitions while in India is to assist it to a peaceful demise" — Lord Curzon, declared in 1900 (not a Congress-session quote — a viceregal remark reflecting British hope that Moderate-led Congress would collapse on its own).
  • "Microscopic minority" — Lord Dufferin, Viceroy 1884–88, dismissing Congress's claim to represent Indians broadly.
Context — how the "Roman script for Hindi" episode connects to Bose's ideology Subhas Chandra Bose's advocacy for the Roman script (Haripura, 1938) sits oddly next to his fierce anti-colonial politics — but it reflects the era's broader debate on script standardisation for a national language across a linguistically diverse country, a technocratic/modernising strand of thought Bose held independently of his views on British rule; it was not adopted as Congress policy.

Context — how the 1907 Surat split and the 1916 Lucknow reunion bookend this table The 1907 Surat session (Ch.10) is where this ideological divide physically ruptured the party — the session was adjourned in disorder as Moderates and Extremists split. The 1916 Lucknow session (also Ch.10) is where the two factions formally reunited, alongside the historic Lucknow Pact with the Muslim League — meaning the "Extremist phase" the table labels 1905–1919 is really bookended by these two specific, nameable sessions: rupture at Surat, reunion at Lucknow.
