Forms of Government & Democracy

What a Government Is

  • A Government is an institution / system of people that takes care of or manages a country or State.
  • Every Government has its own Constitution — a set of fundamental principles it follows to ensure effective governance.

Autocracy

  • Autocracy = form of Government where supreme power / rule is in the hands of one individual or entity.
  • Absolute monarchy
    • A family or group of families rules the country; the post of monarch is inherited.
    • Monarchy is one of the oldest forms of Government.
  • Dictatorship
    • An individual possesses absolute power without any effective limitations.
    • Autocracy now also includes dictatorship.
  • Authoritarian Government is NOT a feature of Indian polity — India has a democratic Government, the opposite of authoritarian.

Democracy

  • Democracy is the most widely adopted system of governance at present — the final power resides with the people.
  • A democratic state = a state in which people are supreme and the Government is formed by representatives elected by the people.
  • Supreme power vested solely in one person, like a king, is NOT democratic (an "elected head" holding supreme power is also not the democratic formulation).
  • India's democracy rests on the fact that the public has the right to choose and change the Government — not merely the written Constitution, Fundamental Rights or DPSP.
  • Importance of citizens: Democracy builds on the intelligence and character of ordinary men and women; participation gives awareness and information to decide, so the success of democracy depends on the quality of decisions made by people.
  • Women, Dalits, the poor and minorities are the biggest stakeholders of democracy in India — democracy increases their self-respect and dignity and protects individual rights.
  • Elections: after every five years the people elect representatives who form the Government under the Constitution.

Political Parties & Party-less Democracy

  • Political parties are the life-blood of democracy: they bring people together to gain control of Government and develop policies in people's interest; people also usually blame parties for bad governance.
  • Party-less democracy was supported by Mahatma Gandhi and Jayaprakash Narayan (both accepted as the answer) — not S.A. Dange or Ram Manohar Lohia.

The State & Sovereignty

Definition of State

  • Garner: the State is a community of persons, more or less numerous, permanently occupying a definite portion of territory, independent (or nearly so) of external control, possessing an organized government to which the great body of inhabitants render habitual obedience.
  • The term 'State' is central to the study of Political Science.
  • The other definitions (people living long with own culture; society with executive responsible to a central authority and independent judiciary; authority to protect natural rights and sustenance) are not the best definition.

Four Essential Elements

  • Population (community of persons permanently living there), Territory (demarcated boundary separating one state from another), Government, Sovereignty (independent say in all domestic and external decision-making).
  • Sovereignty is the most important / prominent element — not the flag, capital or head of state.
  • India before 1947 was not a State: it had population, territory and government but sovereignty was missing because of British control.

Sovereignty of India

  • The word 'Sovereign' implies India is neither a dependency nor a dominion of any other nation but an independent state.
  • India is a member of the Commonwealth of Nations: in 1947 India declared continuation of full membership and accepted the British crown as head of the Commonwealth.
  • This was an extra-Constitutional declaration and does not curtail India's sovereignty in any manner.

Nature of the Indian Political System

Preamble & Source of Power

  • The idea of the Preamble was borrowed from the Constitution of the U.S.A. (not Italy, Canada, France); the language of the Preamble was borrowed from the Constitution of Australia.
  • The Constitution begins with "We the People of India" — the People are the main source of political power, not the Constitution, Parliament or President.
  • The Constitution is the source of authority for both Central and State governments — not the President, PM or Parliament.

Cardinal Features

  • India is a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic with a Parliamentary system — a Union of States.
  • Supreme power is vested in the People of India — a democratic republic.
  • India does not provide for a unified authority; it makes a division of powers between the Union and the States.
  • India is a democratic polity, a sovereign state, has a democratic society and is a welfare state — the architect of the Constitution provided for a welfare State through the Directive Principles of State Policy.
  • The Indian polity has: a Government following the Constitution, democratic Government and the Rule of Law.
  • India is a parliamentary democracy — not presidential, a titular monarchy or an aristocracy.
  • India is not "Semi-democratic"; the Constitution provides a Parliamentary governance system.

Republic

  • Republic = a state in which supreme power is held by the people and the Head of State is elected (no hereditary ruler).
  • India is a Republic because it has no hereditary rulers; the President is elected indirectly through elected representatives (the Head of State is elected, not the 'country is free' or 'Parliament has final authority' idea).
  • The President is elected indirectly because the parliamentary system in India is combined with republicanism (President as Head of State).

Written Constitution & Democracy

  • India has a written constitution and is a democracy; the United Kingdom is democratic but has no written constitution; non-democratic countries do have some form of constitution to govern the functioning of their Government.
  • So a constitution is not the reason (explanation) for democracy.

Welfare State & Philosophy

  • Welfare State: Government in which the State plays a key role in protection and promotion of social and economic well-being of citizens; based on equality of opportunity, equitable distribution of wealth and public responsibility for those unable to avail the minimum necessities of a good life.
  • "The State is everywhere: it leaves hardly a gap" describes a Welfare State (not a Communist, Democratic or Police State).
  • Philosophy of the Constitution (reflected in the Preamble): Welfare State, Socialist State, Political Equality — Communist State is NOT its philosophy; India is committed to equality of opportunity and status.
  • Political equality is provided through Adult Franchise.

Good Governance

  • Features of good governance: accountability, transparency, rule of law.
  • Red tapism = excessive regulation that hinders or prevents action or decision-making — NOT a feature of good governance.
  • The Second Administrative Reforms Commission identified barriers to good governance (and pre-conditions) in Report 12 — Citizen-Centric Administration: The Heart of Governance (not Ethics in Governance, e-Governance or Local Governance reports).

Continuity, Change & Constitution Size

  • The Indian political system embodies continuity and change — from the Mauryan period to the modern era its specific elements persist; it contains elements of the modern style, traditional style and saintly style.
  • The Indian Constitution is the longest written Constitution in the world — no single reason; all of these hold true: it incorporates the experience of many Constitutions, contains detailed administrative provisions, deals with a large country, and contains the Constitution of both Union and State governments.
  • Some experts call the Constitution unitary because it gives a strong Centre in an emergency, but in reality it is federal in nature with some unitary features.
  • Indian Constitution is partly rigid and partly flexible: some provisions amended by simple majority, some need two-thirds majority under Art 368, and some need ratification by at least 50% of State Legislatures also.
  • K.C. Wheare said the Constitution has a good balance between rigidity and flexibility because of the variety in the amending process.

Parliamentary System

Meaning & Origin

  • Two main types of governance systems: Parliamentary and Presidential.
  • India follows the Parliamentary form, influenced by the Parliamentary system of the United Kingdom; the Constitution establishes it at both the Centre and the States.
  • Also called the Cabinet form of Government, responsible Government or the Westminster model.
  • The modern concept of parliamentary government emerged in the Kingdom of Great Britain (UK = birthplace; also asserted by Prof. Pushpesh Pant in his book Vishwa ka Itihas) — not Belgium, France or Switzerland.

Working

  • Members of the executive are taken from the legislature and are accountable to the legislature; the Legislature controls the Executive (not the reverse; judiciary does not control it).
  • The Government is responsible to the popular House (House of the People) and stays in power only while it has the majority support of that House — the Government can be removed by Parliament.
  • The Cabinet form is a mechanism of parliamentary democracy ensuring collective responsibility of the Government to the people (through the popular House).
  • Main advantage: the executive remains responsible to the Legislature, resulting in a responsible Government deriving legitimacy from the Legislature.
  • Indian parliamentary system exists because the Council of Ministers is responsible to the Lok Sabha (not because Lok Sabha is directly elected, Parliament can amend the Constitution or Rajya Sabha cannot be dissolved).

Constitutional Provisions

  • Art 75(3): Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the House of the People.
  • Art 164(2): Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly (States).
  • Art 75(5): a Minister not a member of either House for six consecutive months ceases to be a Minister.
  • Institutional principles: Cabinet members are Members of Parliament; Ministers hold office till they enjoy confidence of Parliament; the Cabinet is not headed by the Head of State — it is headed by the Prime Minister (Head of Government), while the President is Head of State.

Real & Nominal Executive

  • The Indian Constitution adopted a secular State and a federal system.
  • In the Parliamentary system the Union Council of Ministers exercises executive authority.
  • The President is only the formal / nominal head of the executive (constitutional head); the real executive is the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister.

Features of Parliamentary Government

  • Executive responsible to legislature; majority party rule; collective responsibility; nominal and real executives; political homogeneity; double membership; leadership of Prime Minister; dissolution of the lower House; secrecy of procedure.
  • The Legislature is not responsible to the Judiciary or the Executive; the President is not responsible to the Judiciary — hence the correct option was "none / more than one".

Presidential System

Features

  • In the Presidential system the President is the head of the executive branch — all executive powers are vested in the President, who is both Head of State and Head of Government.
  • Basic feature: a single executive; power is centralised.
  • The President appoints his Council of Ministers and cannot dissolve the Legislature.
  • Presidential Government is NOT a feature of the Indian Constitution.

Origin

  • The Presidential form first emerged in the United States of America — American Presidency is the oldest and purest Presidential Government; the President is elected indirectly by the people for a fixed period.

In the Indian Constitution

  • The Constitution has Parliamentary Government, independence of judiciary and federal government as characteristics; the Presidential form is not one.

Federal & Unitary Features

Federal vs Unitary

  • Unitary: all powers concentrated in a Central authority; States subordinate to it. Federal: powers distributed between Centre and States; States are not subordinates of the Centre.
  • India is called Federal because of the distribution of powers between Union and States in the 7th Schedule (and Arts 245–255) — not just a written/rigid Constitution, free judiciary, or residuary power at Centre.
  • Indian Constitution is federal in nature but unitary in spirit; the Constitution can be both unitary and federal according to circumstances — normally federal; in war or Emergency it works as a unitary system (Ambedkar).
  • The Constitution introduces a federal system as the basic structure of Government, with a strong admixture of unitary bias.
  • Reasons for federal adoption: large size of the country and its socio-cultural diversity.
  • The primary objective of India's federal design: to weave a nation out of diverse parts and protect national integration; accommodation of diversities has built a stronger, not weaker, nationhood.
  • India is not a complete federal state, because some provisions give it a unitary character — yet many experts accept it as federal given the division of powers between Union and States; so 'federalism is not practical in India / India is not a federal State' is false.
  • Indian federalism is NOT the result of an agreement among federating units; units cannot secede — Ambedkar used 'Union' to show this.

Federal Features

  • Written Constitution; rigid Constitution; distribution of powers; supremacy of the Constitution; independent judiciary (essential — judiciary interprets the Constitution); bicameral legislature; dual governments; unequal representation of States in Rajya Sabha.
  • Judicial Review rests on Arts 13, 32, 136, 137, 226, 227.

Unitary Features

  • Strong Centre; single Constitution; single citizenship; flexibility of Constitution; integrated judiciary; appointment of Governors by the President / Centre; All India Services; Emergency provisions; Parliament's power to implement international agreements; integrated audit machinery; integrated election machinery; veto over State Bills (President's approval on State bills); Parliament's authority over State List; one Supreme Court.
  • Art 250: Parliament can legislate on State List during Emergency; Art 249: legislate on a State matter in national interest.
  • Only the Union is sovereign, not the States (States have no say in Foreign and Defence matters); the judicial system has a hierarchy, not a division of judicial power.
  • Single citizenship is a unitary (not federal) feature; Decentralisation of powers and Judicial review are federal, not unitary.
  • "Ideal for large countries" is not a characteristic of unitary government — it depends on a country's will (swift decisions, flexibility, uniformity of laws are).
Source conflictThe source classifies "rigid Constitution" as federal in one place and "flexibility of Constitution" as unitary in another, and "unequal representation in Rajya Sabha" as a federal feature in one MCQ but a unitary element in another. Both versions appear in answer keys — read the option wording carefully.

Quasi-Federal

  • Quasi-federal means government generally belongs to the Federal system but the Constitution enables the Federation to convert into a unitary state, generally during an emergency.
  • Indian Federalism is called 'quasi-federal' for unitary features (not because of judicial review): the independent judiciary with Judicial Review (Arts 13, 32, 136, 137, 226, 227) is a crucial feature but not the explanation of the term.
  • 'Neither federal nor unitary' is the explanation of quasi-federalism given by D.D. Basu (for the Constitution as a combination).
  • Laski and Jawaharlal Nehru did NOT coin 'quasi-federal'.

Instruments of Unity in Diversity & Federal Evolution

Institutions

  • Institutions necessary to promote 'unity among diversity': Inter-State Council & National Development Council; Finance Commission & Regional Council; Unitary Judicial System & All India Services.
  • Inter-State Council (Art 263): discusses/investigates policies, subjects of common interest and disputes among States.
  • National Development Council: apex body for decision-making and deliberation on development matters; presided over by the Prime Minister.
  • Finance Commission (Art 280): defines the financial relations between the Central Government and the State Governments.
  • Unitary Judicial System: single integrated system with the Supreme Court at the top.
  • All India Services: Art 312 provides for their creation.

Phase of Federalism

  • The Parliamentary system in India shifted from Centralism to Federalism in the 1970s (1971–80), when the Congress Party lost power in many States.

Constitutional Government, Rule of Law & Concepts

Constitutionalism

  • Constitutional Government = government limited by the terms of the Constitution (a limited government); the Constitution is the source of power and imposes limits on government in the interest of people.
  • It places effective restrictions on the authority of the State in the interest of individual liberty — not restrictions on liberty in the interest of State authority.
  • Constitutionalism protects public interest and liberty from the autocratic power of Government; institutional mechanisms protect citizens' liberties.
  • The chief purpose of a Constitution is to define and limit the powers of government (it also creates offices, gives structure and defines roles of legislature, executive and judiciary).
  • Constitutionalism is sometimes treated as a synonym for limited government, but that is only one interpretation and not the most prominent one.
  • A constitutional government is not merely a government by the legislature, popular government, multi-party government, representative government of a federal nation, or one whose Head has nominal/real powers.

Rule of Law

  • Propounded by A.V. Dicey, a British jurist. Elements: limitation of powers (absence of arbitrary power; no punishment except for breach of law), equality before law (equal subjection to ordinary law, administered by ordinary courts), liberty and civil rights (primacy of individual rights, as defined and enforced by courts).
  • "People's responsibility to the government" is NOT a feature of Rule of Law.

Liberty & Equality

  • Liberty = absence of restraints on the activities of individuals + providing opportunities for the development of individual personalities — the positive conception (opportunity to develop oneself fully); 'protection against tyranny' and 'absence of restraint' = negative views; 'opportunity to do whatever one likes' = anarchy.
  • Constitutional liberty: reservation, protection of minority rights, prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth.
  • Law and liberty complement each other — if there are no laws, there is no liberty (violations occur where no laws protect freedom).
  • Equality implies absence of privileges (special privileges based on gender, colour, creed or religion are irrational prejudices); formal equality was first demanded to break the feudal hierarchy of ranks and orders.

Integration of Princely States & Constitutional Categories

Independence & Princely States

  • The Indian Independence Act 1947 created two independent and separate Dominions — India and Pakistan.
  • Princely States had three options: join India, join Pakistan, or remain independent.
  • More than 560 princely States lay in Indian territory; most joined India but three refused — Hyderabad, Junagadh, Kashmir.
  • How they were included:
  • Hyderabad
    • Included through military action.
  • Junagadh
    • Included through a referendum.
  • Kashmir
    • Included through the Instrument of Accession (a legal document) executed by Maharaja Hari Singh, ruler of Jammu & Kashmir.

Constitutional Provisions for the Union & its Territory

Article 1 — Name & Nature

  • Art 1(1): "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States" — a Union of States, not a Federation, a group, a confederation of States.
  • Country is mentioned in the Constitution as Bharat and India (not Hindustan).
  • The territory of India comprises (1) States, (2) Union Territories, (3) such other territories as may be acquired; names and territories of States are specified in the First Schedule.
  • Why 'Union': the States have no right of separation / secession (Ambedkar) — the federation is not the result of an agreement among units; States do not have territorial integrity either.
  • India is a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic with a Parliamentary system; the President is the Constitutional head of the executive.
  • Assertion "India is not a Union" is false; Reason (Parliament can alter area, boundary and name of any State without its consent) is true.

Present Strength

  • Presently 28 States and 8 Union Territories.
  • Count history: in 2002 — 28 States and 7 UTs (incl. National Capital Territory); Telangana became the 29th State on 2 June 2014; after Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh became UTs (2019) → 28 States and 9 UTs; merger of Dadra & Nagar Haveli with Daman & Diu → 28 States and 8 UTs.
Source conflictOne MCQ answers "number of States after Telangana's formation = 28", whereas the explanation of another says Telangana was the 29th State in 2014 — 28 is the number now (after J&K became a UT). Treat 28 as today's figure.

Power of Parliament — Articles 2, 3 & 4

Who Has the Power

  • The power to admit or establish new States and form States / alter areas, boundaries, names lies with the Parliament — not the President, Lok Sabha alone, Council of Ministers, Central Government, Prime Minister, Supreme Court, States' Reorganisation Commission, State Assembly, Governor's ordinance or political parties.
  • Art 2: admission or establishment of new States — NOT possible by Art 3 (Art 3 cannot be used for admission of new States).
  • Art 3: Parliament may by law:
    • (a) form a new State by separation of territory from any State, by uniting two or more States or parts of States, or by uniting any territory to a part of any State;
    • (b) increase the area of any State;
    • (c) diminish the area of any State;
    • (d) alter the boundaries of any State;
    • (e) alter the name of any State.
  • A State may not include a Union Territory by itself — no State has authority to include territory on its own.
  • Explanation I of Art 3: in clauses (a) to (e) 'State' includes a Union Territory, but in the proviso, 'State' does not include a UT.
  • The power to alter the name of a State belongs to Parliament, not the State Legislature.
  • Example: a separate Vananchal (Jharkhand) from Bihar could be made only through Parliament under Art 3 — not by State Assembly legislation or Governor's ordinance.

Procedure

  • A Bill under Art 3 can be introduced in Parliament only on the prior recommendation of the President.
  • Before recommending, the President must refer the Bill to the Legislature of the State(s) affected (area, boundary or name) to express its views within a specified period.
  • The President / Parliament is not bound by the State Legislature's views — may accept or reject them even if received in time; if the Legislature gives no views in the period, the Bill can still be presented.
  • Consent of the State is NOT required (not the Chief Minister's or Legislature's).
  • Parliament passes by simple majority (ordinary legislative process) — no ratification by two-thirds of States, no special/two-thirds/three-fourths majority.
  • Art 4(1): such law contains provisions amending the First Schedule (names/territories) and Fourth Schedule (Rajya Sabha seats).
  • Art 4(2): such law is not deemed an amendment of the Constitution for the purpose of Art 368 — amendments under Arts 2 and 3 are outside the scope of Art 368.
  • Option "law deemed an amendment under Art 368" is the wrong statement.

Secession & Territorial Integrity

  • States have no right to secede from the Union and no right to territorial integrity; Parliament can change area, boundaries or name by unilateral action with a simple majority.
  • Just advocacy of secession does not get protection of freedom of expression — Art 19(1) gives citizens six freedoms: speech & expression; assembly peaceably without arms; associations/unions/co-operative societies; free movement throughout India; residence and settlement anywhere; any profession/occupation/trade/business; the State may impose reasonable restrictions only on grounds in Art 19(2).

Related Rights

  • Equality before Law (Art 14) — guaranteed to both citizens and non-citizens; Equality of opportunity in public employment (Art 16) — only to Indian citizens.

Demand for Linguistic States — Dhar, JVP & Potti Sriramulu

Early Attitude

  • After the Nagpur Session of Congress 1920 (presided by C. Vijayaraghavachariar) provincial Congress Committees were constituted on a linguistic basis.
  • S.K. Dhar Commission (1948) investigated feasibility of reorganising States on linguistic basis — recommended administrative convenience / viability instead of language.
  • December 1948: the Congress constituted the JVP Committee — Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya — to scrutinise the Dhar Commission; its report in April 1949 also rejected reorganisation on linguistic basis.
  • Hence in 1948 Congress rejected the demand of linguistic provinces; Pattabhi Sitaramayya was a JVP member, NOT a member of the Fazal Ali Commission.

First Linguistic State

  • Death of Potti Sriramulu after a 56-day hunger strike forced the Government to form Andhra Pradesh in October 1953 (effective 1 October 1953) — the first State formed on a linguistic basis, by separating Telugu-speaking areas from Madras State (not Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, UP).
  • Large-scale reorganisation on linguistic basis started in 1956.

States Reorganisation Commission (Fazal Ali) & Act 1956

Commission

  • Constituted by the Central Government (29 December 1953); chairman Justice Fazal Ali (Syed Fazl Ali); members K.M. Panikkar and H.N. Kunzru (Hridayanath Kunzru); report submitted 30 September 1955.
  • Not members: K.T. Shah, Pattabhi Sitaramayya, Tej Bahadur Sapru.
  • It broadly accepted language as the basis of reorganisation but rejected 'one language–one State' and suggested abolition of the four-fold classification of States and territories.
  • It did not support splitting of Bombay and Punjab.
Source conflictOne question stem says the SRC was formed in August 1953; the explanation says it was established on 29 December 1953 (reported in September 1955). 29 December 1953 is used here.

Act & Effect

  • The Government accepted the recommendations with some modifications — enacted the States Reorganisation Act 1956 and the 7th Constitutional Amendment Act 1956.

Overview of Centre–State Relations

Nature

  • The Constitution is Federal in structure and divides all powers — legislative, executive and financial between the Centre and the States.
  • Centre–State relations are determined by Constitutional provisions, conventions and practices, judicial interpretations and mechanisms for dialogue; also influenced by the planning process, conflict of political interests and desire to dominate when different parties rule the Centre and State.
  • Part XI of the Constitution deals with relations between the Union and the States: Legislative relations Arts 245–255; Administrative relations Arts 256–263.
  • Part XII deals with financial relations (Finance, Property, Contracts and Suits, Arts 264–293).
  • Other Parts: Part III Fundamental Rights; Part IV DPSP; Part VI States; Part X Scheduled and Tribal Areas; Part XIII Trade, Commerce and Intercourse within India. Originally the Constitution had 22 Parts.
  • A federal polity involves: relations between Union and States; relations among States; mechanisms for cooperation; mechanisms for solving disputes.
  • Centre's control over States in the planning process is called 'Municipal Relations' (not legislative, financial, or administrative control).
  • Resources flow from Centre to States via tax sharing (Finance Commission, Art 280), grants-in-aid, and centrally sponsored schemes; transfer for plan implementation under Gadgil formula is NOT a means of resource allocation at present.
  • There is a growing demand for review of Centre-State relations because States lack adequate resources for development projects — Centre has more resources and States depend on it.
3

Legislative Relations

Territorial Extent

  • Art 245(1): Parliament can make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India; State Legislature for the whole or any part of the State.

Three-fold Distribution

  • Art 245 and Art 246 regulate the distribution of legislative power; the Seventh Schedule contains the three lists.
  • Art 246(1): Parliament has exclusive power over the Union List (List I).
  • Art 246(2): Parliament and (subject to clause 3) State Legislature have power over the Concurrent List (List III).
  • Art 246(3): State Legislature has exclusive power over the State List (List II) (subject to clauses 1 and 2).
  • Art 246(4): Parliament may legislate on any matter for any part of India not in a State even if it is a State List matter.
  • Number of subjects: Union List 97 originally (now 98); State List 66 (now 59); Concurrent List 47 (now 52).
  • On the Concurrent List, both Centre and State can legislate; in case of conflict Union law prevails. Economic and Social Planning and infants and minors are Concurrent List subjects.
  • Land revenue (State List entry 45) and taxes on agricultural income (entry 46) are State subjects.

Residuary Powers

  • Art 248: residuary powers of legislation — exclusive power over matters not in the Concurrent or State List — are vested in Parliament (not President, State, Cabinet); In the USA these are given to the States.
  • Residuary subjects arise from changing circumstances, technology or new policy needs.
Source errorThe source lists cyber laws, space research, atomic energy, AI regulation and foreign trade as examples of residuary subjects. Atomic energy and foreign trade are actually Union List entries [Outside source] — treat only newly arising matters (e.g. cyber laws, AI) as residuary examples.

Parliament's Power over State List & Special Legislative Provisions

Special Provisions

  • Art 247: Parliament may provide for establishment of certain additional courts.
  • Art 246A (101st Amendment Act 2016): Notwithstanding Arts 246 and 254, Parliament, and subject to clause 2, the State Legislature, have power to make laws on goods and services tax imposed by Union or State.
  • Art 249: Parliament can legislate on GST (Art 246A) or any State List matter in national interest if the Rajya Sabha declares by resolution it is necessary — supported by not less than 2/3 of members present and voting; resolution remains in force for not more than one year (Art 249(2)).
  • Art 250(1): Parliament can legislate on GST or any State List matter when a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation.
  • Art 251: if there is inconsistency between laws made under Arts 249/250 and State laws, Parliament's laws prevail.
  • Art 252: Parliament legislates for two or more States by consent and any other State may adopt such legislation.
  • Art 253: legislation giving effect to international agreements — Parliament may legislate on any State List matter to implement treaties, agreements and conventions so that the Centre can fulfil international obligations.
5

Administrative Relations (Arts 256–263)

Union Control over States

  • Arts 256 to 263 (Part XI) deal with administrative relations.
  • Art 256: obligations of States and the Union — the executive power of every State shall be exercised to ensure compliance with laws made by Parliament and existing laws applying in that State; the Union's executive power extends to giving directions to a State as appear necessary to the Government of India for that purpose.
  • Art 257: control of the Union over States in certain cases — the State's executive power shall be exercised so as not to impede or prejudice the Union's executive power; the Union can give necessary directions.
  • Art 258: power of the Union to confer powers on States — the President may, with the consent of the State government, entrust to that government functions relating to a matter within the Union's executive power.
  • Option Arts 355/356 (Union's duty / President's Rule), 365 (failure to comply with Union directions) and 360 (Financial Emergency) are different provisions.
6

Inter-State Water Disputes, Inter-State Council & Interstate Relations

Water Disputes

  • Art 262(1): adjudication of disputes relating to the waters of inter-state rivers or valleys; Parliament has power of adjudication.
  • Related legislation: Inter-State Water Disputes Act 1956; River Boards Act 1956 (not 1970); National Water Policy 1987.

Inter-State Council (Art 263)

  • Art 263: the President may establish an Inter-State Council to investigate and discuss subjects of common interest between the Centre and States, and also among States; co-ordinate the relationship; the source is the Constitution itself (not Parliamentary law, Planning Commission or CMs' resolution); established by the President's order, not Parliament, NDC or Zonal Councils.
  • Sarkaria Commission recommended a permanent Inter-State Council (also suggested the name Inter-Governmental Council); on this basis it was established on 28 May 1990 by the V.P. Singh (Janata Dal) Government; first ISC established May 1990, constituted December 1990.
  • Composition: Prime Minister — Chairman; Chief Ministers of all States; CMs of UTs having Legislative Assembly and Administrators of UTs without Legislative Assembly; Governors of States under President's rule; six Union Cabinet ministers (incl. Home Minister) nominated by the PM.
  • It is a recommendatory body on inter-state, Centre-State and Centre-UT relations.
  • Of the Inter-State Council, National Security Council and Zonal Councils, only the Inter-State Council is established per the Constitution; NSC was set by executive decision with the PM's approval; Zonal Councils are statutory bodies created by an Act of Parliament.
7

Zonal Councils, NDC & Other Coordination Bodies

Constitutional / Extra-Constitutional

  • National Development Council and Governors' Conference are extra-Constitutional and extra-legal bodies for cooperation between States; Zonal Councils are statutory; the Inter-State Council is constitutional (Art 263).

Zonal (Regional) Councils

  • Formed by the States Reorganisation Act 1956 (a Parliamentary law, not the Constitution/Government resolution/NDC) — five: Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Southern; Home Minister (Union) is Chairman of each; advisory bodies on matters of common interest.
  • Act as 'Sub-Federal Links' giving a platform to resolve disputes through consultation and enabling co-operation and integration among economically/politically/culturally interlinked States.
  • Chandigarh (not a State) is included; also Delhi, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, Puducherry.
  • North-Eastern States (Assam, Arunachal, Manipur, Tripura, Mizoram, Meghalaya, Nagaland) are not in Zonal Councils; their problems are looked after by the North Eastern Council (NEC) under the North Eastern Council Act 1971.
Source conflictOne explanation states a sixth regional council 'North East' formed in 1971 — the NEC (Act 1971) is a separate body.

North Eastern Council

  • NEC Act 1971 created it as an apex advisory body for balanced and coordinated development of seven NE States; amended in December 2002 — Sikkim became the 8th member and NEC became a statutory Regional Planning Body.
  • Present composition: Governors and Chief Ministers of constituent States + three members nominated by the President (not the Union Home Minister as a member); the President nominates the Chairman (need not be a member) and may nominate a Vice-Chairman.
  • Union Home Minister is ex-officio Chairman; the Minister of DoNER is ex-officio member and Vice-Chairman (notifications of 7 July 2018 and 24 August 2021).

Other Bodies

  • Jharkhand Area Autonomous Council formed on 8 August 1995 — 18 districts of Santhal Pargana and Chota Nagpur.
  • National Integration Council: concept involves political, economic, social, cultural and psychological dimensions and their inter-relations; formed in 1962 on 'Unity in Diversity' (one question stem said 1961, but the explanation says 1962; the reason statement was marked incorrect).

Funds, GST & Basic Financial Provisions (Part XII)

Arts 264–293 (Part XII)

  • Arts 264–293 deal with financial relations; Arts 268–281 specifically deal with distribution of revenues (duties and taxes levied by the Union, grants-in-aid, Finance Commission).
  • Art 265: no tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law.
  • Art 266(1): Consolidated Fund of India — all revenues received by Union, all loans, and all money received in repayment of loans.
  • Art 267(1): Contingency Fund of India; Art 267(2): State Legislature may create a Contingency Fund of the State in the nature of an imprest.
  • 101st Constitutional Amendment Act 2016: Art 246A (GST law-making), Art 269A (levy and collection of GST in inter-state trade or commerce), Art 279A (GST Council).
9

Tax Assignment & Grants

Articles 268–276

  • Art 268: duties levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by the States — e.g. stamp duty on financial instruments; stamp duty is imposed by Union but collected by State — so it is neither 'imposed and collected by the State' nor merely 'regulated by the Union'.
  • Art 270: taxes levied and collected by the Union but distributed between Union and States — income tax other than on agricultural income; presently all Union List taxes and duties except those under Arts 268, 269, 269A; taxes on stock exchange and futures-market transactions are levied by the Union (not collected by the State).
  • Taxes exclusively levied and collected by the States: Land Revenue (State List entry 45); Agricultural income tax given to States by the Constitution (State List entry 46) — not by FC, NDC or ISC; Income tax, Corporation tax and GST are not exclusive State taxes.
  • Art 274: provision for taxation in which States are interested.
  • Art 275: grants allocated by the Union to certain States.
  • Art 276(2): total tax on professions, trades, callings and employments payable by any person to a State or local body cannot exceed Rs 2,500 per annum (raised from Rs 250 by the 60th Amendment 1988) — the only tax limited by the Constitution (not corporation tax, estate duty or succession duty).
  • Art 282: Union or a State may make discretionary grants for any public purpose, even if outside its law-making subjects.
  • Art 281 and 228 are not about discretionary grants.

Finance Commission, Borrowing, Trade & Disputes

Finance Commission

  • Art 280(1): constituted by the President within 2 years of commencement of the Constitution and thereafter every 5th year or earlier if he considers necessary; recommends sharing of net proceeds of taxes between Union and States.
  • It is a quasi-judicial body (powers of a civil court).
  • Art 281: the President shall lay every recommendation before both Houses of Parliament with an explanatory memorandum on action taken.

Borrowing, Trade, Suits & Disputes

  • Art 293(1): executive power of a State extends to borrowing within India on the security of its Consolidated Fund within limits fixed by State law — so a State cannot take foreign loans directly.
  • Fiscal disputes between Centre and States: main agency is the Supreme Court (Art 131); the Finance Commission only recommends sharing of taxes.
  • Art 301: freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse throughout India (Part XIII, Arts 301–307; Art 19(1)(d–e) is movement/residence).
  • Art 300: Suits and proceedings — Union/State may sue or be sued by name; Union of India deemed substituted for the Dominion of India and States for Provinces/Indian States in pending proceedings at the Constitution's commencement. Subject to Acts of Parliament / State Legislature, the Union and States may sue or be sued in the like cases as the Dominion of India and the Provinces / Indian States could before the Constitution. Not attorney general or restrictions on trade.

Sarkaria Commission & Other Committees

Sarkaria Commission

  • Appointed by the Central Government in 1983 (formed 9 June 1983) under R.S. Sarkaria, a retired Judge of the Supreme Court; other members B. Shivaraman and S.R. Sen; to review Centre–State relations and suggest ways to strengthen them (not President-PM, Legislature-Executive or Executive-Judiciary relations).
  • Report submitted in January 1988 — 247 recommendations in 19 chapters.
  • Recommended the permanent Inter-State Council (Inter-Governmental Council), which was established under Art 263 in 1990; recommended that Art 356 be used sparingly since it had been misused (President's Rule declared without considering other options).
  • Not members: V. Shankar, K. Hanumanthaiah, O.V. Algesan.
