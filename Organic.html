1. What Are Organic Compounds?

  • Organic compounds = hydrocarbons and their derivatives, i.e. carbon compounds — with the exception of CO, CO₂, carbonates and bicarbonates.
  • Around 1780, it was believed organic compounds could only be synthesised by living organisms, never in a lab — hence the term "organic" (Anglo word meaning bio/living).
  • Berzelius (Swedish chemist) proposed the vital force theory — a "vital force" was thought responsible for forming organic compounds.
  • Wöhler accidentally synthesised the first organic compound — urea — from inorganic ammonium sulphate + potassium cyanate, while trying to prepare ammonium cyanate.
  • Kolbe synthesised acetic acid (1844) from its constituent elements; Berthelot synthesised methane (1856) — both proved organic compounds CAN be made from inorganic sources in the lab.
  • Carbon is the fundamental element of all organic (including bio-) compounds — a repeatedly tested one-liner.
  • Six elements primarily responsible for the origin of life on Earth: Hydrogen, Oxygen, Carbon, Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Sulphur; per one classic exam key the core trio is Carbon, Hydrogen, Nitrogen.
  • Correct sequence of appearance under simulated primitive-Earth conditions: Methane → Hydrogen Cyanide → Nitrile → Amino Acid.
  • Decreasing order of melting points: Acetic acid > Chloroform > Ethanol > Methane.
  • Methane is NOT an allotrope of carbon — graphite, diamond, and C-60 Buckminsterfullerene ARE; methane is a compound (CH₄), not an elemental form of carbon.

2. Petroleum as a Source of Organic Compounds & Petrochemicals

  • Petroleum = mixture of hydrocarbons, mainly aliphatic with lesser aromatic hydrocarbons, plus small amounts of organic S and N compounds.
  • Called rock oil (petra=rock, oleum=oil), black gold, or liquid gold.
  • World's first oil well drilled in Pennsylvania, USA, 1859.
  • Bitumen (a petroleum product) is now used instead of coal tar for metalling roads; stagnant water damages tar roads.
  • Biodiesel is obtained by transesterification.

3. Classification of Organic Compounds

Acyclic / Open-Chain (Aliphatic) Compounds

  • Straight or branched open chains — also called aliphatic compounds.
  • Methane = simplest aliphatic compound; Benzene = simplest aromatic compound — both considered the "father"/parent compound of their respective classes.
Fig. Structures of Methane, Ethane, and Iso-butane (branched-chain example).

Alicyclic / Closed-Chain (Ring) Compounds

  • Carbon atoms joined in a ring (homocyclic); sometimes non-carbon atoms are present in the ring (heterocyclic) — e.g. cyclopropane, cyclohexane, cyclohexene (homocyclic) and tetrahydrofuran (heterocyclic).
  • Alicyclic compounds resemble acyclic compounds more than they resemble aromatic compounds.

Aromatic Compounds

Word from "aroma" (fragrance). Includes benzene (six-carbon ring with alternate single/double bonds) and related benzenoid ring compounds; may have heteroatoms in the ring (heterocyclic aromatic compounds).

  • General formula for alkyl halide = CnH2n+1X (X=Cl, Br, I); general formula for primary aliphatic amine = CnH2n+1NH₂.
  • Functional group isomerism is NOT possible in alkyl halides — unlike alcohols (vs. ethers), aldehydes (vs. ketones), and cyanides (vs. isocyanides), which do show this isomerism.

5. Homologous Series & Isomerism

  • Homologous series: a series where the same functional group substitutes for H in a carbon chain; members are called homologues (the phenomenon = homology).
  • E.g. methane, ethane, propane, butane (straight-chain alkanes); methyl/ethyl/propyl/butyl/amyl alcohol (alcohol series).

Characteristics of a Homologous Series

  • Represented by the same general molecular formula, e.g. CnH2n+1OH for alcohols.
  • Successive members differ by a CH₂ unit.
  • Molecular weights of adjacent members differ by 14 u.
  • All members prepared by the same general methods.
  • Physical properties (m.p., b.p., density) show a gradation as molecular mass increases.
  • Chemical properties (determined by the functional group) remain similar across the series.

Isomerism

  • Isomers: compounds with identical molecular formula but different configuration/properties.
  • Structural isomerism: same molecular formula, different structures — 4 types: chain, position, functional group, metamerism. E.g. n-pentane, iso-pentane, neo-pentane all = C₅H₁₂ but different structures.
  • Stereoisomerism: differs due to relative spatial position of atoms — 2 types: geometrical and optical isomerism.
  • Glycine is NOT optically active — its central carbon is attached to 2 hydrogens (not 4 different groups), unlike alanine/serine, which are optically active.

Hydrocarbons

🟨 Concept   🟦 Fact/Number   🟩 Person/Place/Named Process   🟥 MCQ Trap   🟪 Rule/Reaction

  • Hydrocarbon = compound of hydrogen and carbon only; natural source = petroleum/crude oil.
  • Hydrocarbons minus one H = univalent hydrocarbyl groups, e.g. methyl, phenyl.
  • Serve as fuels, lubricants, and raw materials for plastics, fibres, rubbers, solvents, explosives, industrial chemicals.
  • Occur naturally in trees/plants too — e.g. β-carotene (C₄₀H₅₆) pigment in carrots & green leaves.
  • Classified structurally into open-chain (acyclic) and cyclic (closed-chain) — further into saturated (alkanes), unsaturated (alkenes, alkynes), aromatic, and alicyclic.

1. Saturated Hydrocarbons — Alkanes (Paraffins)

  • Carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen single bonds only; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (–ane suffix).
  • Also called paraffins (Latin parum=little + affinis=related → "little affinity") — chemically inert/unreactive under normal conditions with acids, bases, other reagents; but DO react with oxygen (combustion) and halogens under the right conditions.
  • Non-polar; weak van der Waals forces between molecules.
  • At 25°C: C₁–C₄ are gases, C₅–C₁₇ are liquids, C₁₈ onward are solids.

Methane (CH₄) / Marsh Gas

  • First member of open-chain saturated hydrocarbons; also called Marsh Gas — produced in wetlands by anaerobic decomposition of organic matter via methanogenic archaea (methanogens); also released from septic tanks, crude-oil production, and in termite/ruminant (cow) guts by rumen methanogens.
  • Lab prep: heating sodium acetate + soda lime. Commercial prep: reaction of aluminium carbide on water.
  • Shape: tetrahedral, 109°28′ bond angle; black in colour (per source text); mixture with air is explosive — a cause of coal-mine explosions.
  • Uses: fuel (light/energy), preparing methyl alcohol/chloroform, carbon black (printing ink), industrial hydrogen, motor tyre manufacture.
  • Methane is colourless, odourless, non-toxic but flammable; common name = Marsh Gas (NOT "laughing gas" — that's N₂O).
  • William Crookes was the first to isolate methane gas, discovering that methane mixed with air could be exploded using an electric spark.
  • Butane (C₄H₁₀), not methane, is the gas used in cigarette lighters — a common trap; butane is also a Natural Gas Liquid (NGL).
  • Methane is the largest component of biogas.
  • Methane + air is the classic cause of coal-mine explosions — not CO₂, N₂, or butane.
  • Carbon black (used in printing ink) is formed from the decomposition of methane heated in the absence of air — a frequently tested one-liner.
  • Paddy fields are a major source of atmospheric methane (est. 50–100 million tonnes/year) — NOT wheat, cotton, or groundnut fields.
  • Statement check: saturated hydrocarbons undergo substitution reactions (TRUE); carbon black is obtained when methane is heated in the absence of air (TRUE) — both statements correct together.
  • Match: Alkanes→CₙH₂ₙ₊₂; Alkenes→CₙH₂ₙ; Alkynes→CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; Alcohols→CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH.
  • Benzene, acetylene (alkyne), and pentene (alkene) are all unsaturated; butane is the odd one out — it is SATURATED.

2. Cycloalkanes

  • Carbon atoms form a closed ring; general formula CₙH₂ₙ; first member = cyclopropane (C₃H₆).
  • C–C bond length in ethane > that in ethyne, ethene, benzyne — distance between carbons joined by a single bond is always larger than between carbons joined by multiple (double/triple) bonds.

3. Unsaturated Hydrocarbons — Alkenes

  • Contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond; general formula CₙH₂ₙ (–ene suffix); also known as olefins ("oil forming" — ethylene was found to form an oily liquid on reacting with chlorine).
  • A hydrocarbon where two carbon atoms are joined by a double bond is, by definition, an alkene.

Ethylene / Ethene (C₂H₄)

  • Prepared by heating ethyl alcohol with conc. H₂SO₄ at 170°C (H₂SO₄ acts as a dehydrating agent): C₂H₅OH --H₂SO₄(conc.), 170°C--> C₂H₄ + H₂O.
  • Colourless, sweet-smelling, less water-soluble; high concentration can cause unconsciousness; molar mass 28; density 1.25 g/L at STP; boiling point –103.7°C.
  • Uses: mustard gas manufacture, artificial fruit ripening (as a natural plant hormone too), oxyethylene flame, fruit preservation, historically as an anaesthetic, manufacture of polythene. Trichloroethylene (a related compound) is used for dry cleaning.
  • Fruit ripening is promoted by ethylene. Ethephon (ethrel) is a commercial chemical that releases ethylene in water at a specific pH — used as a substitute for calcium carbide in fruit ripening.
  • Calcium carbide is used for artificial ripening of green fruit because it produces acetylene (not ethylene) gas — acetylene mimics ethylene's ripening action but carries harmful arsenic/phosphorus traces, so its use for fruit ripening is illegal in India.
  • Butene (CH₃CH=CHCH₃) is a constituent of LPG, used as a fuel.

4. Unsaturated Hydrocarbons — Alkynes

  • Contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; first member = acetylene/ethyne (C₂H₂).
  • Reaction of acetylene with silver nitrate demonstrates its acidic property.

Acetylene (C₂H₂)

  • First prepared by American scientist Wilson; formed by reaction of calcium carbide on water — NOT silicon carbide on water (a repeated trap).
  • Colourless, faint ethereal odour (garlic-like when impure), less soluble in water, lighter than air; sublimes at typical atmospheric conditions; boiling point –84°C.
  • Free acetylene at elevated pressure is unstable and explosive — shipped dissolved in acetone; its mixture with oxygen is explosive on ignition.
  • Uses: synthesis of benzene, oxy-acetylene welding & cutting (flame temp. ~3200°C), raw material for plastics, camphor manufacture, neoprene (artificial rubber), anaesthesia; carbide/acetylene lamps used in mines.
  • Welding gas mixture = Oxygen + Acetylene (not H₂+O₂, not O₂+ N₂).
  • Correct statement set on acetylene: used in the welding industry (✓), raw material for plastics (✓) — but it is easily obtained from CALCIUM carbide (not silicon carbide) + water (this third claim is false).

5. Aromatic Hydrocarbons (Arenes)

  • Also called arenes; most possess a pleasant odour (Greek aroma=pleasant-smelling); most contain a benzene ring, e.g. benzene, toluene, naphthalene, anthracene. General formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₆.
  • Aromatic hydrocarbons have alternate C–C and C=C bonds similar to benzene; alicyclic hydrocarbons lack this alternation and behave more like aliphatic compounds (e.g. cyclohexane, cyclopentane).
  • Heterocyclic compounds (not classed as hydrocarbons) have a ring of carbon plus other elements (N, O, S) — e.g. Furan, Pyridine, Pyrrole, Thiophene.

Benzene (C₆H₆)

  • First isolated by Faraday (1825) from cylinders of compressed illuminating gas (pyrolysis of whale oil); Hofmann obtained it from fractional distillation of coal tar; first synthesised by Berthelot by passing acetylene through a red-hot tube.
  • Uses: production of organic compounds, explosives manufacture, dry cleaning of woollen clothes, solvent, fuel additive (mixed with petrol).
  • Benzene has 12 sigma bonds and 3 pi bonds (6 C–C sigma + 6 C–H sigma = 12 sigma; 3 pi bonds from alternating double bonds) — NOT "6 sigma and 6 pi" or "6 sigma and 3 pi", a frequently repeated trap.

Toluene (C₆H₅CH₃)

First extracted from tolu balsam (a resin). Used in manufacturing TNT explosives, as antifreeze, solvent, in making chloramine-T drug, in artificial sweetener saccharin, and in dry cleaning.

  • Dry-cleaning chemicals: Benzene, Carbon tetrachloride, and Trichloroethylene ARE used — Sodium carbonate is NOT (of the four listed: I, II, IV correct; III incorrect).

Naphthalene (C₁₀H₈)

A polynuclear hydrocarbon; major source = coal tar; used as a germicide and moth repellent.

1. Alcohols: Methanol & Ethanol

  • Alcohols: hydroxyl (–OH) functional group on a saturated carbon (H of a hydrocarbon replaced by OH); general formula R–OH / CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH.
  • Classified by number of –OH groups: monohydric (methanol, ethanol), dihydric (glycol), trihydric (glycerol).
  • Presence of –OH identified by the esterification test — forms a pleasant-smelling compound with a carboxylic acid.

Methyl Alcohol / Methanol / Carbinol (CH₃OH)

  • Formerly produced by destructive distillation of wood → called wood spirit / wood alcohol / wood naphtha; today mainly by hydrogenation of carbon monoxide. Also obtainable by heating formaldehyde with KOH/NaOH (also forms sodium/potassium formate).
  • Light, volatile, colourless, flammable liquid, odour like ethanol; polar liquid at room temperature; boiling point 64.7°C, solidifies at –98°C.
  • Forms explosive mixtures with air, burns with a non-luminous flame → CO₂ + H₂O.
  • Highly toxic: ingestion in small amounts causes blindness (affects the optic nerve, oxidised in the liver to toxic methanal which coagulates cell protoplasm); large amounts cause death. The offending substance in liquor tragedies leading to blindness is methyl alcohol.
  • Increases the acidity of the body after ingestion; used to denature ethyl alcohol (making it undrinkable).
  • Cannot be dried by anhydrous CaCl₂ — forms the addition compound CaCl₂·4CH₃OH.
  • Uses: solvent for paints/celluloid/shellac/varnishes; fuel (mixed with petrol); making dyes, perfumes, medicines, formaldehyde, formic acid, acetic acid, methyl chloride, methyl salicylate; component of methylated spirit (methanol + ethanol mixture = denatured alcohol); older automobile antifreeze (40% methanol + 60% water) — modern coolants are mainly ethylene-glycol based.
  • Ethyl alcohol is made unfit for drinking by addition of methanol and pyridine.
  • Wood spirit = Methyl alcohol (not ethyl alcohol, ethylene glycol, or glycerol).

Ethyl Alcohol / Ethanol (C₂H₅OH)

  • Industrially prepared by fermentation of molasses (sugar-industry by-product) or starched foodgrains; also by treating ethene with steam + phosphoric acid catalyst: CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O --H₃PO₄--> CH₃CH₂OH.
  • Glucose is converted to ethyl alcohol by the enzyme zymase (naturally occurring in yeast). Related enzymes: invertase converts sucrose → glucose+fructose; maltase converts maltose → glucose; diastase converts starch → maltose.
  • Colourless, volatile, inflammable liquid; boiling point ~78.2–78.5°C, melting point ~–114.1°C; alcoholic odour, sharp taste; neutral compound (doesn't affect pH, neutral to acid-base indicators); soluble in water & many organic solvents; cannot be dried by anhydrous CaCl₂ (forms CaCl₂·3C₂H₅OH).
  • Also called "spirit of wines" (primary constituent of all wines) and "grain alcohol" (from fermentation of starched foodgrains).
  • Flavour of apple is mainly due to the presence of ethanol.
  • Uses: solvent in varnishes/paints; transparent soaps, perfumes, iodine tincture, polish; wine production; fuel in spirit lamps/stoves; wound cleaning; antiseptic/disinfectant; cough syrups/digestive syrups/tonics; sedative/hypnotic; preparing chloroform, iodoform; preserving biological specimens; used in spirit level & alcohol thermometers.
  • Mixed with petrol (+ minute aromatic/co-solvent amount) = power alcohol (motor fuel).
  • Large-quantity ingestion slows metabolic processes, depresses the CNS — the person may feel relaxed but judgement, timing, and muscular coordination are seriously impaired.
  • Breath/blood-alcohol test: person blows into a bag/filter paper containing potassium dichromate–sulphuric acid solution (acidic medium); on reaction with ethanol (oxidised to acetic acid), colour changes orange → green; degree of colour change ∝ alcohol level.

2. Ethylene Glycol & Glycerol

  • Ethylene Glycol [CH₂OH·CH₂OH]: dihydric alcohol, sweet taste, used as an antifreeze (depresses freezing point of water/petrol) in car radiators in cold regions — modern engine coolants are predominantly ethylene-glycol based.
  • Ethylene glycol, not ethanol/propyl alcohol/methanol, is the standard anti-freezing substance used in self-starting engine radiators.
  • Glycerol / Glycerine [CH₂OH·CHOH·CH₂OH]: extremely hygroscopic trihydric alcohol; found in fats/vegetable oils as esters; occurs free in fermented sugar solutions and human blood.
  • Treated with conc. HNO₃ (+ conc. H₂SO₄) → trinitroglycerine — used in making explosives (dynamite, etc.).
  • Other uses: stamp ink, boot polish, medicines, cosmetics, lubricants, antifreeze wine, transparent soaps; cleaning watch parts; fruit preservative.
  • Glycerol reacts with conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄ to give nitroglycerin (used in dynamite) — glycerol is thus a key raw material for explosives production, not methanol, urea, or oxalic acid.

5. Phenols, Ethers, Aldehydes & Ketones

Phenols (C₆H₅OH)

  • Formed when H of an aromatic hydrocarbon is replaced by –OH; phenol = simplest hydroxy derivative of benzene. Ferric chloride test indicates presence of phenols.
  • Commonly known as carbolic acid; prepared by Runge (1834).
  • Used to prepare: salol, bakelite, phenolphthalein (indicator), aspirin, salicylic acid, phenyl (household germicide), and explosives like picric acid.
  • Phenol (via condensation with formaldehyde) is used in manufacturing Bakelite — Bakelite was developed by Leo Baekeland (1907).
  • The organic compound used in making picric acid (explosive), phenolphthalein (indicator), bakelite, salol, aspirin, and salicylic acid is PHENOL.

Ethers

Formed when H of a hydrocarbon is replaced by alkoxy/aryloxy (R–O–/Ar–O–) group; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂O. Diethyl Ether (C₂H₅OC₂H₅): obtained by heating ethanol with conc. H₂SO₄ at 140°C; also via Williamson synthesis; used as an anaesthetic — considered better than chloroform.

Aldehydes

Contain the –CHO functional group; general formula CₙH₂ₙO — e.g. formaldehyde (HCHO), acetaldehyde (CH₃CHO). Identified by Tollen's reagent, Fehling solution, Schiff's reagent. Formaldehyde: tissue fixative, embalming agent, disinfectant; 40% aqueous solution = formalin, used as a preservative of biological specimens.

Ketones

Contain the C=O (ketonic) group; general formula CₙH₂ₙO. Acetone / Dimethyl ketone (CH₃COCH₃): common industrial solvent; used in cosmetics, artificial silk, synthetic rubber, nail polish remover, medicines. Acetone can be harmful to skin/nails — "acetone-free" removers use ethyl or butyl acetate instead.

6. Carboxylic Acids

  • Contain the –COOH (carboxyl) group; general formula R–COOH / CₙH₂ₙO₂ — e.g. formic acid, acetic acid, propionic acid.
  • Types by number of –COOH groups: monocarboxylic (acetic acid), dicarboxylic (oxalic acid, adipic acid), tricarboxylic (citric acid); tetra/polycarboxylic acids also exist.
  • Long-chain carboxylic acids = fatty acids (e.g. palmitic acid C₁₅H₃₁COOH, stearic acid C₁₇H₃₅COOH), mostly occurring esterified (triglycerides, phospholipids, cholesteryl esters) rather than free.
  • Tracking of people by trained dogs is based on recognising carboxylic acid in foot sweat.

Formic Acid (HCOOH) — Methanoic Acid

  • Simplest monocarboxylic acid; first obtained by distillation of (red) ants (Latin formica=ant) — hence the name; present in ant/insect venom and stinging-nettle secretions.
  • Formic acid is a stronger acid than acetic acid — acetic acid's electron-donating CH₃ group makes proton loss harder than in formic acid (which lacks such a group).
  • Colourless liquid, pungent smell, corrosive (blisters skin); freezes ~8.4°C, boils ~100.8°C; miscible with water, alcohol, ether.
  • Uses: rubber/leather/textile/dyeing/electroplating industries, arthritis medicines, insecticide, fruit-juice preservative, antibacterial food preservative.
  • Formic acid is present in ant bites/stings and in blood & tissues generally.
  • Match: Vinegar→Acetic acid, Tamarind→Tartaric acid, Sour milk/curd→Lactic acid, Nettle sting→Methanoic (Formic) acid.

Acetic Acid (CH₃COOH) — Ethanoic Acid

  • Most important carboxylic acid commercially; obtained from pyroligneous acid on commercial scale; common lab reagent.
  • Its 4–8% aqueous solution = vinegar (source text states 4–6%, other sources 4–8%) — vinegar's sour taste and pungent smell are due to acetic acid.
  • Anhydrous acetic acid = colourless, pungent liquid; b.p. ~117.9°C, m.p. ~16.6°C; "glacial acetic acid" = <1% water content, solidifies into crystals just below room temp (~16–17°C); corrosive; miscible with water/alcohol/ether.
  • Uses: making alcohol/other organic compounds, solvent, vinegar (food), photographic film, rayon (as cellulose acetate), source/jelly production.
  • Acetic acid is the major/dominant component of vinegar (called "ethanoic acid" in some option sets) — NOT lactic, citric, malic, or nitric acid, a very frequently repeated trap.
  • Vinegar is produced by fermentation and oxidation of natural carbohydrates / fermentation of ethanol by acetic acid bacteria (from sugarcane juice/molasses); used as cooking ingredient and preservative.
  • Odour of acetic acid resembles that of vinegar.
  • Souring of milk is due to LACTIC acid, NOT acetic acid or nitric acid — a classic mismatch trap.
  • A traditional battery contains sulphuric acid (not an organic acid, but a repeatedly paired MCQ fact in this topic cluster).

Oxalic Acid (C₂H₂O₄ / HOOC-COOH)

  • Dicarboxylic acid; as the potassium-hydrogen salt, present in plants of the oxalis and rumex family; in plant cells occurs as calcium oxalate; some calcium oxalate is also present in human urine — can deposit in the kidney/prostate as kidney/prostate stones.
  • Uses: metallurgy, cloth colouration/printing, cleaning ink spots (10% solution), leather bleaching, photography (as ferrous oxalate), ink and coal-tar manufacture.
  • Oxalic acid is used in photography.
  • Oxalic acid = HOOC-COOH, also called ethanedioic acid; acidic strength greater than acetic acid.

Lactic Acid [CH₃CH(OH)COOH]

  • Monohydroxy carboxylic acid; present in milk (gives it sour taste); also produced in muscles during exercise — its accumulation causes muscular fatigue.
  • More soluble in water than in organic solvents due to hydrogen bonding; used in yogurt production, detergents, pharmaceutical/cosmetic industries.
  • Accumulation of lactic acid in muscles after strenuous work causes the sensation of fatigue.
  • Milk turns sour (curd forms) due to lactic acid, produced when lactobacillus bacteria convert lactose (milk sugar) into lactic acid — curd-making is a bacterial biotechnological process.
  • Match tables: Lactic acid→Milk/Curd/Sour milk, Acetic acid→Vinegar, Citric acid→Lemon, Butyric acid→Rancid butter.
  • "Ascorbic acid–Lemon, Maltose–Malt, Acetic acid–Curd, Formic acid–Red Ant" — the Acetic acid–Curd pairing is WRONG (curd's acid is lactic, not acetic).

7. Esters

Contain the –COOR functional group and a characteristic fruity smell; general formula CₙH₂ₙO₂ / Cₙ₊₁H₂ₙ₊₁COOR — e.g. methyl formate, methyl acetate, ethyl acetate. Ethyl acetate is used for making artificial perfumes, scented colours, dyes, and is a classic example of a strong fruity-fragrance compound (a repeatedly tested MCQ answer over methyl chloride, methanoic acid, methanol).

1. Polymerization Basics

Polymerization: a process of reacting monomer molecules together in a chemical reaction to form polymer chains or 3-D networks. The small reacting molecules are monomers; the products are polymers.

4. Bulletproof & Speciality Polymers

  • Kevlar = a polyamide (para-aromatic, poly-paraphenylene terephthalamide); extremely strong due to many inter-chain bonds — used for bulletproof/stab-resistant vests, helmets, jackets.
  • Lexan (Polycarbonate) — along with laminated glass — used for bulletproof windows/CDs.
  • "Glyptal" is NOT used for bulletproof material — it's a condensation polymer used in paints and lacquers (protective coatings for wood/metal). Kevlar AND Lexan ARE correctly matched to bulletproof materials.
  • Laminated glass is also used in making bulletproof jackets/windows.
  • Correct statement bundle: Teflon and Dacron are polymers (✓); Neoprene is synthetic rubber (✓); Polythene is polyethylene polymer (✓); "Natural rubber is chloroprene" is FALSE (it's isoprene).
  • Caprolactam is NOT itself a polymer — it is the raw material (monomer) used to produce Nylon (Nylon 6).
  • Bakelite is formed by condensation of Phenol and Formaldehyde — not urea+formaldehyde or melamine+formaldehyde (those give other resins).

3. Explosives

  • An explosive = a chemical compound/mixture/device intended to explode, producing rapidly expanding gases; initiation via fire, friction, concussion, percussion.
  • A good explosive should: not be volatile, generally not be hygroscopic, and be stable.
  • Explosive reaction may be detonation (high explosives) or deflagration/burning (low explosives).
ExplosiveComposition / Facts
Trinitroglycerine (TNG) / Nitroglycerine / Nobel OilDense, colourless, oily; from nitrating glycerol with white fuming nitric acid + H₂SO₄; a high explosive but extremely shock-sensitive; used to make dynamite. "Nobel oil" is another name for TNG — not TNP, TNA, or TNT.
Trinitrophenol (TNP) / Picric AcidFormula (O₂N)₃C₆H₂OH; one of the most acidic phenols; light yellow crystalline solid; used as a lab reagent, explosive, antiseptic/burn treatment, and in dyes
DynamiteInvented by Alfred Nobel (1867); made by absorbing nitroglycerine in sawdust or kieselguhr (diatomaceous earth); used in mining, quarrying, construction, demolition
Blasting Gelatin~6–8% nitrocellulose + 91–93% nitroglycerine + ~1% acid acceptor
Trinitrotoluene (TNT)From toluene + nitric/sulphuric acid mix; formula C₆H₂(NO₂)₃CH₃; yellow solid; military shells/bombs/grenades; industrial deep-well/underwater blasting, dye-stuff & photographic chemical intermediate; quarrying/mining explosive
RDX (Cyclonite / Cyclotrimethylenetrinitramine)Discovered by George Friedrich Henning (Germany), patented 1898, widely used from WWII; formula (O₂NNCH₂)₃; white solid, high explosive in a pliable plastic matrix; also known as hexogen (Germany), T-4 (Italy), cyclonite (USA); primary explosive agent in C-4 plastic explosive. RDX is an explosive — unlike DDT (antibacterial - false claim), TNT (insecticide - false claim), LSD (antiviral - false claim), which are common wrong pairings.
Gun Cotton (Nitrocellulose)Highly flammable — from nitrating cellulose (cotton/wood fibres) via nitric acid + sulphuric acid; a mild explosive (combustion can be violent); used in rockets, propellants, printing ink bases, leather finishing, celluloid; mixed with camphor+alcohol, first used for denture plates

HMX (High Melting Explosive / Octogen)Powerful, relatively insensitive nitroamine high explosive, chemically related to RDX; used in solid rocket/missile propellants for high energy density (e.g. ICBMs)
CL-20 (HNIW)Polycyclic nitroamine explosive (C₆H₆N₁₂O₁₂); releases ~20% more energy than HMX; superior oxidiser-to-fuel ratio for rockets/missiles
LLM-105High-performance, insensitive military explosive; used in advanced warheads/solid propellants
  • Nitrochloroform (chloropicrin) is NOT an explosive — it's an antimicrobial/fungicide/herbicide/insecticide and tear gas, manufactured as a WWI poison gas; TNT, TNG (Trinitroglycerine), and RDX all ARE explosives.
  • Nitroglycerine is used as an explosive — not phosphorus trichloride, mercuric oxide, or graphite.
  • Glycerol (via nitration to nitroglycerin) is used in explosives production — not methanol, urea, or oxalic acid.
  • The chemical used in manufacturing dynamite is glycerol trinitrate (nitroglycerine), not glycerol triacetate or glycerol triiodate.
  • Methane + air mixture is the classic cause of most mine explosions (~5–15% methane in air is explosive range) — not H₂+O₂, O₂+acetylene, or CO₂+methane.
