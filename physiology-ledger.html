Digestion & Absorption — The Alimentary Ledger

Every organ of the gut, in the order food actually travels through it — theory + 45 solved MCQs compiled together, gaps explained.

Yellow — Concept Blue — Fact / Number Green — Person / Institution Red — MCQ Trap Green card — Glossary / Explainer

01The Alimentary Canal & Gut Wall

  • Digestion is the breakdown of large insoluble food molecules into small, water-soluble molecules so they can be absorbed into the watery blood plasma — a form of catabolism.
  • Two components: Mechanical digestion (physical breakdown of food into smaller pieces, e.g. chewing) and chemical digestion (enzymes breaking food into usable small molecules).
  • The alimentary canal (gastrointestinal tract) is a long tube running from mouth to anus, through which food is taken in and digested; the accessory/digestive glands (salivary glands, liver, pancreas, plus the tongue and gall bladder) support it but are not part of the tube itself.
  • Full path in order: Mouth → Buccal cavity → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small intestine (Duodenum → Jejunum → Ileum) → Large intestine (Caecum → Colon → Rectum) → Anus.
  • In humans, this passage is about 30 feet (9 metres) long from oesophagus to anus.

The Four Layers of the Gut Wall

  • From oesophagus to rectum, the gut wall has four layers, from outside in: Serosa → Muscularis → Sub-mucosa → Mucosa. All four layers show local modifications in different parts of the canal.
The four gut-wall layers, outermost to innermost

Glossary — Terms the source text uses but never fully explains
Mucosa vs "mucosal gland" / mucous gland
The mucosa is simply the innermost tissue layer lining the gut tube. A mucosal gland (or "gland of the mucosa") is any gland whose secretory cells sit within this mucosal layer itself, rather than being a separate large organ connected by a duct — e.g. the gastric glands in the stomach's mucosa, or the crypts of Lieberkuhn in the intestine's mucosa. This is different from an "accessory gland" like the liver or pancreas, which is a whole separate organ that empties its secretion into the canal via a duct.
Sub-mucosal gland
A gland whose secretory tissue sits one layer deeper, in the sub-mucosa rather than the mucosa. The only named example here is Brunner's glands in the duodenum.
Goblet cells
Individual mucus-secreting cells scattered within the mucosal epithelium (not a "gland" in the organ sense) — found lining the stomach, small intestine and large intestine; their mucus lubricates and protects the lining.
Villi vs Microvilli vs Crypts
Villi are finger-like foldings of the whole mucosa (visible to the naked eye) that increase surface area in the small intestine. Microvilli are much smaller projections on the surface of each individual villus cell, giving a "brush border" appearance and increasing surface area further still. Crypts of Lieberkuhn are pit-like glandular indentations found in between the bases of adjacent villi.
Chyme vs Chyle
Chyme is the acidic, semi-liquid mass of partly digested food leaving the stomach. Chyle is the later, milky-watery fluid formed once digestion is essentially complete in the ileum — it contains the soluble end-products of digestion, ready for absorption.

02Mouth — Buccal Cavity, Teeth & Tongue

  • The mouth leads into the buccal (oral) cavity, which contains the teeth and a muscular tongue, and performs two jobs: mastication (chewing) of food and facilitation of swallowing.
  • Each tooth sits in a socket of the jaw bone — this attachment type is called thecodont.
  • Most mammals, including humans, form two sets of teeth across life: temporary milk/deciduous teeth replaced later by permanent/adult teeth — this pattern of two successive sets is called diphyodont dentition.
  • An adult human has 32 permanent teeth of four types (heterodont dentition): incisors (I), canines (C), premolars (PM), molars (M). The dental formula (per half-jaw, upper/lower) is written as 2, 1, 2, 3 for each side.
  • The hard chewing surface of teeth is made of enamel, which aids mastication.
  • The tongue is a freely movable muscular organ attached to the floor of the oral cavity by the frenulum; its upper surface bears small projections called papillae, some of which carry taste buds.

03Salivary Glands & Saliva

  • Saliva is produced by three pairs of salivary glands, situated just outside the buccal cavity and secreting into it: parotids (cheek), sub-maxillary/sub-mandibular (lower jaw), and sub-lingual (below the tongue).
  • Human saliva is about 99.5% water; the remaining ~0.5% is electrolytes, mucus, white blood cells, epithelial cells (a source of DNA), enzymes (amylase/ptyalin, lipase), and antimicrobial agents (secretory IgA, lysozyme).
  • A healthy person produces roughly 0.75 to 1.5 litres of saliva per day.
  • Digestive functions of saliva: moistens food and helps form it into a bolus for easy swallowing; also begins carbohydrate digestion.
  • Salivary amylase (ptyalin) hydrolyses starch into the disaccharide maltose (and dextrin) at an optimum pH of 6.8; about 30% of starch is broken down at this stage — digestion of carbohydrate starts in the mouth, not protein or fat.
  • Lysozyme in saliva hydrolyses bacterial cell walls, acting as an antibacterial agent that prevents infection.
Starch —(salivary amylase, pH 6.8)→ Maltose
MCQ Trap In the buccal cavity, digestion of carbohydrate begins — not protein, not fat. Protein digestion begins later, in the stomach.
Fact Certain drugs placed under the tongue or against the cheek lining are absorbed directly into blood capillaries there — the mouth is a minor absorption site, mainly for such drugs.

04Pharynx & Oesophagus

  • The oral cavity opens into a short pharynx, a shared passage for both food and air — the oesophagus and the trachea (windpipe) both open into it.
  • A cartilaginous flap, the epiglottis, covers the glottis (the windpipe's opening) during swallowing, preventing food from entering the airway.
  • The bolus passes from mouth to pharynx to oesophagus by the act of swallowing, called deglutition.
  • The oesophagus (also called the gullet or food pipe) is a thin, muscular tube about 25 cm long, running through the neck, thorax and diaphragm to the stomach.
  • Food is pushed down the oesophagus by successive waves of muscular contraction called peristalsis — the oesophageal walls squeeze together and relax in sequence.
  • The gastro-oesophageal sphincter (a muscular valve) regulates and controls passage of food from the oesophagus into the stomach.

05Stomach — Anatomy

  • The stomach is a muscular, 'J'-shaped sac lying between the oesophagus and small intestine in the upper-left abdomen; it has strong muscular walls and acts as both a food store and a mixer/grinder.
  • Four regions: cardiac portion (where the oesophagus opens in), fundic region, body (main central region), and pyloric portion (opens into the small intestine).
  • The pyloric sphincter guards the opening from stomach into the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine.
  • The mucosa of the stomach is thrown into folds called rugae, and contains embedded gastric glands — a type of mucosal gland (see glossary above).

06Stomach — Gastric Digestion

Gastric Gland Cell Types

  • Gastric glands have three major cell types:
Cells of the gastric gland and their secretions
Cell TypeSecretesFunction

  • The stomach stores food for 4–5 hours, mixing it thoroughly with acidic gastric juice via churning movements of the muscular wall — this semi-digested acidic mixture is called chyme.
  • Roles of HCl: provides the acidic medium needed for digestion, kills bacteria, dissolves small foreign matter (stones/bone fragments) that entered with food, and activates pepsin and renin. Optimal pH for pepsin activity is around pH 1.8.
  • Mucin is a glycoprotein forming the protective mucus layer over the stomach's internal lining.
  • Pepsinogen, on exposure to HCl, converts into the active proteolytic enzyme pepsin, which converts proteins into proteoses and peptones (partially broken-down peptides) — this is where protein digestion begins.
  • Renin (also called rennin) is a proteolytic enzyme found in the gastric juice of infants specifically, which helps digest milk protein.
  • Small amounts of lipase are also secreted by gastric glands, breaking down some fat into glycerol and fatty acids even at this early stage.
  • Absorption already begins in the stomach: water, simple sugars, and alcohol are absorbed here, along with some medicines.
Glossary — Renin (stomach) vs Renin (kidney) — do not confuse
Gastric renin
A digestive enzyme in infant gastric juice that clots/curdles milk protein for easier digestion.
Kidney renin
A completely different enzyme, secreted by the kidneys, that converts angiotensinogen in the blood into angiotensin (blood-pressure regulation) — this is the "Renin" tested in the Ptyalin/Pepsin/Renin/Oxytocin match-list MCQ below.
MCQ Trap HCl is secreted by the parietal (oxyntic) cells of gastric glands, located in the stomach — not the intestine, liver, or pancreas. It is hydrochloric acid, not sulphuric, nitric, or phosphoric acid.
MCQ Trap Of the three enzymes released in the stomach for protein digestion — only Pepsin is correct. Trypsin and Chymotrypsin are secreted by the pancreas in inactive forms and act later, in the duodenum/small intestine — not in the stomach.

07Liver, Gallbladder & Bile

  • The liver is the largest gland/largest internal organ of the human body, weighing about 1.2–1.5 kg (roughly 22 cm long, 15 cm wide), located in the abdominal cavity just below the diaphragm, with two lobes.
  • The hepatic lobules are the structural and functional units of the liver, containing hepatic cells arranged in cords; each lobule is covered by a connective tissue sheath called Glisson's capsule.
  • Liver functions beyond digestion: making proteins and blood-clotting factors, manufacturing triglycerides and cholesterol, glycogen synthesis and storage, bile production, converting harmful ammonia to urea, breakdown of RBCs, and — critically — the liver produces no digestive enzymes of its own.
  • Bile secreted by hepatic cells passes through hepatic ducts and is stored/concentrated in the gall bladder, a thin muscular sac (~8 cm long, 4 cm diameter).
  • Duct pathway: hepatic duct (from liver) + cystic duct (from gall bladder) → common bile duct → joins the pancreatic duct → forms the common hepato-pancreatic duct, which opens into the duodenum, guarded by the sphincter of Oddi.
  • Composition of bile: about 97% water, 0.7% bile salts, 0.2% bilirubin, 0.51% fats (cholesterol, fatty acids, lecithin) and a small amount of inorganic salts; it also contains bile pigments (bilirubin and bili-verdin) — but no digestive enzymes at all.
  • About 800–1000 ml of bile is produced per day in adults.
  • Bile's main job is emulsification of fats — breaking large fat globules into very small micelles — and it also activates lipase; it acts to some extent as a surfactant.
MCQ Trap (Assertion–Reason) "The liver plays an important role in fat digestion" is true, but "the liver produces two important fat-digesting enzymes" is false — the liver produces no digestive enzymes at all; it aids fat digestion only indirectly, via bile-driven emulsification.
MCQ Trap Gallstones (usually cholesterol/bilirubin) block the bile duct, which primarily disrupts digestion of fats — since bile emulsifies fat, not carbohydrate or protein.
Fact Bile is produced in the liver but stored in the gallbladder, and finally received by the small intestine (duodenum) — three separate, commonly-confused MCQ answers about the "same" bile.

08Pancreas & Pancreatic Juice

  • The pancreas is both an endocrine and exocrine gland — hence called a "mixed gland". It is a compound, elongated organ situated between the limbs of the 'C'-shaped duodenum, behind the stomach.
  • It is the second-largest gland in the human body (after the liver), about 12–15 cm long, lobulated, salmon-coloured.
  • As an endocrine gland, it regulates blood sugar by secreting the hormones insulin, glucagon, and somatostatin, plus pancreatic polypeptide — this secretion goes directly into blood, not the gut.
  • As an exocrine gland, it secretes alkaline pancreatic juice containing enzymes, released into the duodenum.
  • Pancreatic juice contains enzymes mostly in inactive forms: trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, procarboxypeptidases, amylases, lipases, and nucleases.
  • Trypsinogen is activated into active trypsin by the enzyme enterokinase, which is itself secreted by the intestinal mucosa; active trypsin then activates the other pancreatic enzymes in turn.
  • Because pancreatic juice digests carbohydrates, proteins, and fats all together, it is called the "complete digestive juice".

MCQ Trap The pancreas is the second largest gland, with an alkaline secretion described as the "complete digestive juice" — this exact combination of three clues (2nd-largest + alkaline + complete digestive juice) is a favourite "identify the gland" question, and the answer is always the pancreas, not the liver or salivary glands.

09Duodenum

  • The small intestine is a long, narrow, coiled tube, about 6.7–7.6 metres (22–25 feet) long in adults, distinguished into three successive regions: duodenum → jejunum → ileum.
  • The duodenum is a short, 'C'-shaped structure (~20–25 cm long), adjacent to the stomach and surrounding the head of the pancreas — the shortest of the three regions.
  • Ducts from the liver, gallbladder, and pancreas all enter the duodenum, delivering juices that neutralize the acid arriving from the stomach and help digest proteins, carbohydrates and fats.
  • Digestion here occurs in an alkaline medium — a direct contrast with the acidic stomach.
  • Brunner's glands, located in the sub-mucosa (see glossary in Section 01), produce a mucus-rich alkaline secretion containing bicarbonate; combined with pancreatic bicarbonate, this neutralizes stomach acid in the chyme, providing the right pH (~7.8) for enzymes to work.
  • Proteolytic breakdown in the duodenum: Proteins/Proteoses/Peptones —(trypsin/chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase)→ Dipeptides.
  • Carbohydrate breakdown: Polysaccharides (starch) —(amylase)→ Disaccharides.
  • Fat breakdown: Fats —(lipases, aided by bile)→ Diglycerides.
  • Nucleic acid breakdown: Nucleic acids —(nucleases)→ Nucleotides and Nucleosides.
MCQ Trap Order of decreasing length among the three small-intestine parts: Ileum > Jejunum > Duodenum (approx. 3.5 m, 2.5 m, 0.25 m respectively) — the duodenum is by far the shortest despite doing much of the heavy enzymatic work.

10Jejunum & Ileum

Jejunum

  • The jejunum is the middle, second part of the small intestine, about 2.5 metres long.
  • Its lining is specialised for absorption by enterocytes — small nutrient molecules already digested in the duodenum are absorbed here.
  • Once absorbed, nutrients (except fat, which routes via the lymph) pass through the enterohepatic circulation and enter the liver for processing.
  • The jejunum is specifically involved in magnesium absorption.

Ileum

  • The ileum is the lower/final part of the small intestine and the largest section of the entire alimentary canal, about 3.5 metres long.
  • Its inner wall has secretory cells: some secrete mucus, others secrete an alkaline fluid known as succus entericus (intestinal juice) — the arrival of chyme in the ileum stimulates this secretion.
  • Succus entericus is formed from the combined secretions of brush border cells of the mucosa and the goblet cells; it contains disaccharidases (e.g. maltase), dipeptidases, lipases, nucleosidases, and more.

  • Digestion is completed in the ileum; the resulting watery substance is called chyle, containing soluble digestion products ready for absorption.
  • Absorption itself is described as occurring "through the mucosa of the ileum" — the inner epithelial lining is folded into villi, which greatly increase surface area for absorbing digested food.

Fact Zymase converts glucose into ethyl alcohol and carbon dioxide (fermentation), occurring naturally in yeast — it is not a human digestive enzyme, but is a frequent decoy option in "which enzyme does X" MCQs.

12Absorption Mechanisms

  • Absorption is the process by which end products of digestion pass through the intestinal mucosa into the blood or lymph, via passive, active, or facilitated transport.
  • Simple diffusion: small amounts of glucose, amino acids, and electrolytes like chloride ions pass into the blood, driven purely by concentration gradients.
  • Facilitated transport: glucose and amino acids are also absorbed with the help of carrier proteins.
  • Osmosis: water transport depends on the osmotic gradient.
  • Active transport: moves substances against the concentration gradient and therefore requires energy; used for amino acids, glucose, and electrolytes such as Na⁺.
  • Fatty acids and glycerol are insoluble and cannot be absorbed directly into blood. They are first packaged into small droplets called micelles, which move into the intestinal mucosa; there they are reformed into protein-coated fat globules called chylomicrons, which are transported into the lymph vessels (lacteals) inside the villi, eventually draining into the bloodstream.
Summary of absorption by region
RegionWhat Is Absorbed
MouthCertain drugs contacting the mucosa of the mouth/underside of tongue, absorbed directly into local blood capillaries.
StomachWater, simple sugars, and alcohol; also some medicines.
Small IntestinePrincipal site of absorption; digestion is completed here — final products (glucose, fructose, fatty acids, glycerol, amino acids) absorbed via the mucosa into blood and lymph. Maximum absorption occurs in the small intestine, overall.
Large IntestineAbsorption of some water, minerals, and drugs — no significant digestive activity occurs here.
  • Absorbed substances finally reach body tissues, which use them for cellular activity — this end process is called assimilation.
MCQ Trap "Most of the digestive process takes place in..." → the answer is the small intestine, since digestion begins in the mouth but is essentially completed there, using enzymes from the pancreas plus bile from the liver.

13Large Intestine, Caecum & Vermiform Appendix

  • Undigested, unabsorbed food passes from the ileum into the large intestine via the ileo-caecal valve, which prevents backflow of faecal matter.
  • The large intestine (colon) consists of the caecum, colon, and rectum. The colon itself has four parts: ascending, transverse, descending, and sigmoid colon.
  • No significant digestive activity occurs in the large intestine. Its two main functions are: (i) absorption of some water, minerals, and certain drugs; (ii) secretion of mucus, which helps bind undigested waste particles together and lubricates them for easy passage.
  • Water and salts are absorbed through the epithelial wall's many convolutions — most of the body's water reabsorption from waste happens here.
  • Wastes become semisolid faeces, developing a characteristic bad odour from bacterial decomposition of undigested food, which produces gases like methane, sulphur dioxide, ammonia, and carbon dioxide.
  • The caecum is a small, blind pouch-like sac at the junction of the small and large intestine, hosting symbiotic micro-organisms; it absorbs remaining fluids/salts and mixes contents with mucus.
  • The vermiform appendix is a narrow, finger-like, blind-ended tube arising from the caecum — a vestigial organ in humans (once useful for digestion, now largely redundant as humans evolved).
  • In herbivores, by contrast, the vermiform appendix is well-developed and actively helps digest cellulose — a sharp human/herbivore contrast worth remembering.
Explainer — Why can't humans digest cellulose? No enzyme capable of breaking cellulose down is found anywhere in the human digestive system. The starch-digesting enzyme diastase (an alternate name sometimes used for salivary/pancreatic amylase) is present in human saliva, but it acts only on starch, not on cellulose — the two are both polysaccharides of glucose but linked differently, and diastase cannot break cellulose's particular bond type.
MCQ Trap The appendix is anatomically attached to the large intestine (specifically the caecum), which is considered the beginning of the large intestine embryologically — not the small intestine, stomach, or gallbladder.

14Rectum, Anus & Defecation

  • The descending colon opens into the rectum, which opens out through the anus.
  • Digestive waste solidifies into coherent faeces in the rectum, which triggers a neural reflex causing the urge to defecate.
  • Egestion (defaecation) — passing faeces out through the anal opening — is a voluntary process, carried out by a mass peristaltic movement.

15Neural & Hormonal Control

  • Gastro-intestinal activity is coordinated by both neural and hormonal control.
  • The sight, smell, and/or presence of food in the oral cavity can stimulate saliva secretion by neural signalling — a reflex response even before eating starts.
  • Gastric and intestinal secretions are similarly stimulated by neural signals, both local and via the central nervous system (CNS).
  • Hormonal control of digestive-juice secretion is carried out by local hormones produced by the gastric and intestinal mucosa itself — these are the "mucosal glands" referenced in Section 01's glossary, now functioning as endocrine tissue.

16Calorific Values of Food

  • Energy content of food is measured in heat-energy units — calorie (cal) or joule (J): 1 calorie is the heat needed to raise 1 g of water by 1°C; physiologists commonly use kilocalorie (kcal) or kilojoule (kJ) instead. Nutritionists' "Calorie" (capitalised) = 1 kcal.
  • Gross calorific value = heat liberated from complete combustion of 1 g of food in a bomb calorimeter (a sealed metal chamber filled with O₂). The physiologic value is the actual energy the body extracts from combusting that food, which is somewhat lower.

17Disorders of the Digestive System

  • Inflammation of the intestinal tract is the most common ailment, caused by bacterial or viral infection, or by parasites such as tapeworm, roundworm, threadworm, hookworm, and pinworm.

18Protein-Energy Malnutrition (PEM)

  • Dietary deficiency of proteins and total food calories, widespread across parts of South and South-east Asia, South America, and West/Central Africa — worsened during drought, famine, and political turmoil (historical examples: Bangladesh's liberation war, Ethiopia's mid-1980s drought).
  • PEM chiefly affects infants and children, producing two distinct conditions: Marasmus and Kwashiorkor.

19Tongue & Taste — MCQ Facts

  • The tongue detects five true tastes: Sweet, Bitter, Salty, Umami, Sour — sensed by taste buds across all parts of the tongue, though the sides are somewhat more sensitive overall than the middle.
  • "Spicy" and "Pungent" are NOT tastes — they are pain/heat signals from touch-and-temperature nerves, not taste-bud signals; the compound capsaicin in chilli triggers this sensation.
  • Exception to the "all parts equally sensitive" rule: the posterior (back) of the tongue is specifically more sensitive to bitter taste — an evolutionary protection mechanism, helping reject spoiled or poisonous food before it's swallowed.

Breathing & Exchange of Gases — The Respiratory Ledger

The full airway in order, breathing mechanics, gas transport, and the cellular-respiration chemistry behind it all — 11 solved MCQs folded in, hard concepts explained.

Yellow — Concept Blue — Fact / Number Green — Person / Institution Red — MCQ Trap Green card — Glossary / Explainer

01Respiratory Organs Across Animals

  • Breathing (respiration) is the exchange of O₂ from the atmosphere with CO₂ produced by body cells — O₂ is used to indirectly break down glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids to release usable energy; the harmful by-product CO₂ must be continuously removed.
  • Mechanisms vary by habitat and organisation level:

MCQ Trap Mammals respire by lungs — not gills, trachea (the trachea is only a conducting passage, not itself the exchange organ), or skin.

02The Human Airway, In Order

  • Full pathway: External nostrils → Nasal chamber (via nasal passage) → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Primary bronchi → Secondary bronchi → Tertiary bronchi → Bronchioles → Terminal bronchioles → Alveoli.
  • A pair of external nostrils opens above the upper lips, leading via the nasal passage into the nasal chamber.
  • In the nasal cavity, a layer of mucous membrane filters the air, trapping pollutants and other harmful substances.
  • The nasal chamber opens into the pharynx (throat) — the same shared passage for food and air described in the Digestion tab, containing the intersection between oesophagus and larynx.
  • The pharynx opens through the larynx region into the trachea. The larynx is a cartilaginous box that also helps in sound production, hence called the "sound box".
  • During swallowing, the glottis (opening of the larynx) is covered by the thin elastic cartilaginous flap called the epiglottis, preventing food from entering the larynx/airway.
  • The trachea (windpipe) is a straight tube extending to the mid-thoracic cavity, dividing at the level of the 5th thoracic vertebra into right and left primary bronchi.
  • Each primary bronchus divides repeatedly: secondary (lobar) bronchi → tertiary (segmental) bronchi → many smaller bronchioles → terminal bronchioles.
  • The trachea, primary/secondary/tertiary bronchi, and initial bronchioles are all supported by incomplete cartilaginous rings, keeping the airway from collapsing while still allowing flexibility (e.g. for the oesophagus to expand behind it during swallowing).
  • Each terminal bronchiole gives rise to numerous thin, irregular-walled, vascularised bag-like structures called alveoli — roughly 15 crore (150 million) alveoli per lung.
  • The branching network of bronchi, bronchioles, and alveoli together comprise the lungs.
Nostrils → Nasal chamber → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Primary bronchi → Secondary bronchi → Tertiary bronchi → Bronchioles → Terminal bronchioles → Alveoli
Concept — Conducting Part vs Exchange (Respiratory) Part Everything from the external nostrils up to the terminal bronchioles is the conducting part — it transports atmospheric air to the alveoli, clears foreign particles, humidifies the air, and brings it to body temperature, but does no gas exchange itself. Only the alveoli and their ducts form the respiratory/exchange part — the actual site where O₂ and CO₂ diffuse between blood and air.
MCQ Trap The actual site of gaseous exchange in the lungs is the alveoli — not tracheoles (an insect structure), bronchioles (still part of the conducting zone), or the pulmonary vein (a blood vessel, not an exchange surface).

03Lungs, Pleura & the Thoracic Cavity

  • Humans have two lungs, situated in the thoracic cavity on either side of the heart. The right lung is bigger than the left (which shares chest space with the heart); the right lung has three lobes, the left has two.
  • Each lung is covered by a double-layered pleura, with pleural fluid between the two layers, reducing friction on the lung surface during breathing.
  • The outer/parietal pleura is in close contact with the thoracic lining (chest wall); the inner/visceral pleura is in contact with the lung surface itself. Together they enclose the pleural cavity, which holds the pleural fluid.
  • The thoracic chamber is anatomically an air-tight chamber, formed dorsally by the vertebral column, ventrally by the sternum, laterally by the ribs, and on the lower side by the dome-shaped diaphragm.
  • Because of this sealed anatomical setup, any change in thoracic-cavity volume is directly reflected as a matching change in pulmonary (lung) cavity volume — essential for breathing, since the lungs cannot alter their own volume directly.
  • At the base of the lungs sits the diaphragm, a sheet of skeletal muscle and the main muscle of respiration.
  • Between the ribs lie many small intercostal muscles (external and internal) that assist the diaphragm in expanding and compressing the lungs.
Explainer — Why is the thoracic cavity "airtight" but the lungs still fill with air? "Airtight" here means the thoracic cavity does not let outside air leak directly into the space around the lungs (the pleural cavity) — it is sealed by bone, muscle, and the diaphragm. The lungs themselves are the only path air can take into the chest, via the trachea. Because the pleural cavity is sealed and airtight, when the diaphragm/intercostals expand the rib cage, the whole airtight thoracic volume increases — and since the lungs are the only elastic, air-connected structures inside it, they expand to fill that larger sealed space, which is exactly what pulls air in.
MCQ Trap — Sequence Correct sequence of O₂ transport in the body: Lungs → Blood → Tissue (uptake at the lungs, transport by blood, delivery to tissues) — not blood-first or tissue-first orderings.

04Mechanism of Breathing — Inspiration & Expiration

  • Breathing has two stages: inspiration (atmospheric air drawn in) and expiration (alveolar air released out).
  • Air movement is driven by a pressure gradient between lungs and atmosphere — the body cannot alter pulmonary volume directly, so it changes thoracic volume instead, and the airtight setup (Section 03) transmits that change to the lungs.
Inspiration vs Expiration — mechanics compared
StageMuscle ActionPressure RelationshipResult
Inspiration (active process) Diaphragm contracts (flattens, moves down) — increases thoracic volume in the antero-posterior axis. External intercostal muscles contract — lift the ribs and sternum, increasing thoracic volume in the dorso-ventral axis. Intra-pulmonary pressure becomes less than atmospheric pressure (a "negative" pressure relative to outside). Air is forced from outside into the lungs.
Expiration Diaphragm and intercostal muscles relax, returning to their normal dome shape/position — thoracic volume decreases. Intra-pulmonary pressure rises to slightly above atmospheric pressure. Air is expelled from the lungs.
  • Additional abdominal muscles can be recruited to increase the strength/force of both inspiration and expiration (forced breathing).
  • On average, a healthy human breathes 12–16 times per minute at rest (one alternate source cites a broader range of 15–25 times/minute, and about 35 times/minute in babies — always use 12–16/min as the standard NCERT figure unless the question specifies "infant").
  • Breathing-movement air volumes are clinically measured with a spirometer — this measurement process itself is called spirometry.
  • In females, the diaphragm plays a comparatively smaller role in inspiration (to avoid injuring a foetus in the uterus) — the ribs take on the greater share of the work; in males, the diaphragm is the dominant driver.
Explainer — "Negative pressure" in plain terms "Negative intra-pulmonary pressure" does not mean a vacuum or pressure below zero — it simply means the air pressure inside the lungs has dropped to be slightly lower than the surrounding atmospheric pressure, purely because the lung's volume increased (the same amount of air molecules now occupy more space). Since gases always flow from high pressure to low pressure, outside air rushes in to equalize this small difference — that inward rush of air is inspiration.

Glossary — Dead space (an additional term worth knowing) Dead space refers to the volume of air (~150 mL) sitting in the nasopharynx, trachea, and bronchi — i.e. the conducting part of the airway — which never reaches the alveoli and is therefore not available for actual gas exchange, even though it is technically "inhaled."

06Exchange of Gases — Diffusion at the Alveolus

  • Alveoli are the primary sites of gas exchange; exchange also occurs between blood and tissues. In both places, O₂ and CO₂ move by simple diffusion, driven mainly by pressure/concentration gradients.
  • Other factors affecting diffusion rate: solubility of the gases and the thickness of the diffusion membrane.
  • Partial pressure is the pressure contributed by one individual gas within a mixture of gases — written as pO₂ for oxygen and pCO₂ for carbon dioxide.

  • This data shows a clear concentration gradient for O₂ from alveoli → blood → tissues (high to low), and an opposite gradient for CO₂, from tissues → blood → alveoli.
  • CO₂'s solubility is 20–25 times higher than that of O₂ — so for the same partial-pressure difference, far more CO₂ diffuses across the membrane than O₂ would.
  • The diffusion membrane has three layers: the thin squamous epithelium of the alveolus, the endothelium of the alveolar capillary, and the basement substance in between (a thin basement membrane supporting the epithelium, plus the basement membrane around the capillary's single-layer endothelial cells). Despite three layers, total thickness is much less than a millimetre — thin enough that diffusion is highly efficient.
Explainer — Partial pressure, in plain terms Air (or blood) is a mixture of several gases together. "Partial pressure" of one gas (say O₂) is simply the share of the total pressure that gas alone is responsible for — as if you separated out just the O₂ molecules and asked how hard they alone are pushing. A gas always diffuses from where its own partial pressure is higher to where it is lower, exactly like a concentration gradient — it does not care about the pressure of the other gases mixed in with it.

07Transport of Oxygen

  • Blood is the transport medium for both O₂ and CO₂. About 97% of O₂ is transported bound to RBCs (haemoglobin); the remaining 3% travels dissolved directly in plasma.
  • Haemoglobin is a red, iron-containing pigment in RBCs; O₂ binds to it reversibly to form oxyhaemoglobin. Each haemoglobin molecule can carry a maximum of four molecules of O₂.
  • Binding is primarily driven by pO₂; also affected by pCO₂, H⁺ concentration (pH), and temperature.
  • The oxygen dissociation curve is a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve plotting % saturation of haemoglobin with O₂ against pO₂ — it's the standard tool for studying how pCO₂/H⁺/temperature affect O₂ binding.
  • In the alveoli — high pO₂, low pCO₂, low H⁺, lower temperature — all factors favour oxyhaemoglobin formation (loading).
  • In the tissues — low pO₂, high pCO₂, high H⁺, higher temperature — all factors favour dissociation of O₂ from oxyhaemoglobin (unloading).
  • Every 100 mL of oxygenated blood delivers around 5 mL of O₂ to tissues under normal physiological conditions.
  • Normal haemoglobin concentration in a healthy person is about 12–18 g/100 mL of blood; approximately 1.34 mL of O₂ binds per gram of haemoglobin, so roughly 20 mL of O₂ is carried per 100 mL of blood.
Explainer — Why is the oxygen dissociation curve S-shaped, not a straight line? Each haemoglobin molecule has four O₂-binding sites, and binding the first O₂ molecule slightly reshapes the protein, making it easier for the next O₂ to bind (called "cooperative binding"). This creates a slow start, a steep middle rise, and a flattening near full saturation — the characteristic S/sigmoid shape. Practically, this means haemoglobin picks up O₂ very efficiently in the high-pO₂ lungs and releases it very efficiently in the lower-pO₂ tissues, rather than binding/releasing gradually and inefficiently throughout.
MCQ Trap — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Carbon monoxide (CO) binds to haemoglobin at the same site as O₂ but roughly 250 times more tightly, forming stable carboxyhaemoglobin (HbCO). This displaces oxygen and prevents haemoglobin from carrying it to tissues — so CO poisoning mainly affects the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood (leading to hypoxia), not digestive, liver, or kidney function directly. Recovery is slow because the HbCO complex is very stable.

08Transport of Carbon Dioxide

  • CO₂ is transported in three forms: ~20–25% bound to haemoglobin as carbamino-haemoglobin, ~70% as bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), and ~7% dissolved directly in plasma.
  • Carbamino-haemoglobin binding is related to pCO₂; pO₂ is a major factor affecting this binding too — when pCO₂ is high and pO₂ is low (as in tissues), more CO₂ binds; when pCO₂ is low and pO₂ is high (as in alveoli), CO₂ dissociates from carbamino-haemoglobin and is released.
  • RBCs contain a very high concentration of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (minute amounts are also present in plasma), which catalyses a reversible reaction in both directions.
CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ (carbonic anhydrase) ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺
  • At the tissue site — where pCO₂ is high due to ongoing catabolism — CO₂ diffuses into blood (RBCs and plasma) and the reaction runs forward, forming HCO₃⁻ and H⁺ (CO₂ "trapped" as bicarbonate).
  • At the alveolar site — where pCO₂ is low — the reaction runs in reverse, regenerating CO₂ and H₂O, so the CO₂ carried as bicarbonate from the tissues is released out through the lungs.
  • Every 100 mL of deoxygenated blood delivers approximately 4 mL of CO₂ to the alveoli.
MCQ Trap During normal breathing, the percentage of nitrogen in expired vs inspired air stays essentially unchanged — nitrogen is not consumed or produced by respiration. In contrast, exhaled air has less oxygen and more CO₂ and water vapour than inhaled air.

09Regulation of Respiration

  • Humans can maintain and moderate their respiratory rhythm to match the body's metabolic demands — this is done through the neural system.
  • The respiratory rhythm centre, in the medulla region of the brain, is primarily responsible for this regulation.
  • The pneumotaxic centre, in the pons region of the brain, can moderate the rhythm centre's function — its neural signals can reduce the duration of inspiration, thereby altering respiratory rate.
  • A chemosensitive area, situated adjacent to the rhythm centre, is highly sensitive to CO₂ and H⁺ ions; an increase in either activates this area, which signals the rhythm centre to adjust breathing so as to eliminate the excess.
  • Receptors in the aortic arch and carotid artery also detect changes in CO₂ and H⁺ concentration, sending signals to the rhythm centre for remedial action.
  • The role of oxygen itself in regulating respiratory rhythm is quite insignificant — regulation is driven mainly by CO₂/H⁺ levels, not by O₂ levels directly.
MCQ Trap When blood oxygen concentration decreases, the rate of breathing increases (via hypoxemia triggering compensatory faster breathing) — even though O₂ itself plays only an "insignificant" direct role in rhythm regulation compared to CO₂/H⁺, a drop in O₂ still indirectly drives the body to breathe faster to correct the deficit.

11Hypoxia, Hypoxemia & Body-Temperature Regulation

  • Hypoxia is a condition where the body, or a region of it, is deprived of adequate oxygen supply at the tissue level; it may be generalized (whole body) or local (one region).
  • Hypoxemia is abnormally low oxygen level specifically in the blood — this can cause hypoxia when blood fails to carry enough oxygen to meet tissue demand.
  • Both conditions are dangerous — without adequate oxygen, the brain, liver, and other organs can be damaged within minutes of symptom onset.
  • The lungs help maintain body temperature: energy is produced in tissues by oxidation of food, and body water is vaporised through breathing (inhaling O₂-rich air, exhaling CO₂-laden, moisture-laden air) — this process helps regulate core temperature.
MCQ Trap The organ that helps maintain body temperature through this oxidation/vaporisation mechanism is the lungs — not the heart, liver, or kidney.

12Cellular Respiration — ATP, Explained From Scratch

  • Cellular respiration is the biochemical pathway by which cells release energy from the chemical bonds of food molecules, making that energy available for life's essential processes. It can be aerobic (with oxygen) or anaerobic (without oxygen).
Explainer — What is ATP, and why does everything point to it?
The core idea
Food (glucose, fat, etc.) stores energy in its chemical bonds, but cells cannot use that energy directly to power things like muscle contraction. Instead, cells convert food energy into a single "universal currency" molecule called ATP (adenosine triphosphate) — think of digesting food as "earning money in a foreign currency," and ATP as the "local currency" every part of the cell can actually spend.
Why triphosphate?
ATP is adenosine attached to three phosphate groups chained together. The bond holding the outermost (third) phosphate on is a high-energy bond. When the cell needs energy, an enzyme breaks that one bond, releasing the stored energy and leaving behind ADP (adenosine diphosphate) — adenosine with only two phosphates — plus a free phosphate (Pi):
ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi + energy
Why is ADP → ATP the "maximum energy" answer in MCQs?
The conversion ATP → ADP is the step that releases stored energy for the cell to use — this is the reaction that actually powers muscle contraction, ion pumps, and biosynthesis, so it is the answer to "which biotransformation provides maximum energy to the body." The reverse step, ADP → ATP, is where the cell recharges the "battery" using energy harvested from food — this is what respiration (glycolysis + Krebs cycle) is doing at every step.
Where does the energy in respiration end up?
Respiration is not one single reaction — it is a stepwise pathway that gradually strips energy out of glucose and uses it to recharge many ADP molecules back into ATP. The ATP produced is then carried elsewhere in the cell and spent (converted back to ADP) to do actual work.

MCQ Trap The complete conversion of glucose, in the presence of oxygen, into CO₂ and water with release of energy is called aerobic respiration — not anaerobic respiration, glycolysis alone (glycolysis is only the first stage), or hydrolysis (a generic bond-breaking-by-water term, not specific to respiration).

13Glycolysis & Krebs Cycle

Glycolysis (the "EMP Pathway")

  • Glycolysis is the metabolic pathway that converts glucose into pyruvic acid (pyruvate); the free energy released is captured by forming the high-energy molecules ATP and NADH (reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide).
  • Also known as the EMP Pathway, named for its discoverers Embden, Meyerhof, and Parnas.
  • Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm, and does not itself require oxygen.
  • One molecule of glucose (6-carbon) yields two molecules of pyruvic acid — each pyruvate has three carbon atoms (not four).
  • During glycolysis, 4 ATP molecules are formed, but 2 ATP are consumed earlier in the pathway (phosphorylation steps) — so the net gain is 4 − 2 = 2 ATP per glucose molecule.
  • Depending on oxygen availability, pyruvate then follows different paths: with O₂ present, it proceeds into aerobic respiration (Krebs cycle) producing CO₂ and water plus energy; without oxygen, pyruvate converts to lactic acid in muscle, or to CO₂ and ethanol in yeast/plants (fermentation) — both are anaerobic routes, distinct from each other by organism/tissue type.

Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle)

  • Discovered by the British scientist Hans Krebs.
  • Takes place in the mitochondria of eukaryotic cells (in the cell membrane of prokaryotes, which lack mitochondria).
  • Here, pyruvic acid undergoes total oxidation in the presence of oxygen and various enzymes; end products are carbon dioxide, water, and released energy.
  • Net gain from the Krebs cycle: 36 ATP (from oxidation of the 2 pyruvic acid molecules produced per original glucose).
  • Total net ATP gain across all of aerobic respiration (glycolysis + Krebs cycle combined): 38 ATP per glucose molecule.
Glucose —(Glycolysis, cytoplasm)→ 2 Pyruvate (net +2 ATP)
2 Pyruvate —(Krebs cycle, mitochondria, +O₂)→ CO₂ + H₂O (net +36 ATP)
Total aerobic respiration: net +38 ATP per glucose molecule
MCQ Trap Regarding glucose breakdown: (i) is false — pyruvate has three carbons, not four. (ii) is true — lack of oxygen converts pyruvate to lactic acid (in muscle). (iii) is true — absence of oxygen converts pyruvate to CO₂ and ethanol (in yeast/plant cells). So the correct combination is (ii) and (iii) only.

14Respiratory Quotient (RQ)

  • Respiratory Quotient (RQ) = the ratio of the volume of CO₂ evolved to the volume of O₂ consumed, by an organism/tissue/cell, in a given time.
  • RQ indicates which macronutrient is being metabolised, since fats, carbohydrates, and proteins each use different energy pathways.
