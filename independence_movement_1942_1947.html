Background — Cripps Failure & the Wardha Resolution

July 5–14, 1942 · the resolution that set the movement in motion
Quit India Movement — identity card
AspectDetail

NatureDiffered from earlier campaigns by its spontaneous mass participation — it was effectively a leaderless movement once top leaders were arrested at the outset.
Slogan"Quit India" / "Bharat Chhodo". Yusuf Meher Ali is credited with coining the slogan (M.P. PCS 2018) — a distinct fact from who gave the "Do or Die" mantra (Gandhi — see Ch.2).
CharacterUnprecedented public courage and militancy; the British prioritised war efforts, imposed draconian measures, and declared most political activity illegal, framing the movement as revolutionary and threatening to colonial rule.

Factors responsible

  • Cripps Mission failure (1942): highlighted British reluctance to concede real progress, fuelling self-determination demands.
  • Inflation & shortages: discontent from essential-goods shortages; rumours of British scorched-earth policies added to panic.
  • British setbacks: losses in Southeast Asia and Burma sharpened fears of a Japanese invasion.
  • Economic insecurity: bank runs, gold hoarding.
  • Regional impact: strongest in Eastern U.P. and Bihar; comparatively weak in Madras.
  • Multi-part PYQ synthesis (Uttarakhand UDA/LDA 2003): the movement was a reaction to (1) disappointment against the Cripps Mission, (2) the threat of Japanese attack, and (4) the passing of the August 1942 AICC proposal — not to any incitement to violence by Gandhi's writings (he never provoked violent means in his articles).

The Wardha meeting (5–14 July 1942)

  • A meeting of the Congress Working Committee was held at Wardha, attended by Jawaharlal Nehru, Abul Kalam Azad, Vallabhbhai Patel, Mahatma Gandhi, Aruna Asaf Ali, Achyut Patwardhan, Dr. Anugrah Narayan Sinha, and Pattabhi Sitaramayya among others.
  • On 6 July 1942, at this CWC meeting, Gandhi discussed his "Quit India Policy" for the first time — the meeting was chaired by Congress President Maulana Abul Kalam Azad (43rd BPSC).
  • The 'Quit India' resolution was passed on 14 July 1942, again chaired by Azad as sitting Congress President.
  • The resolution — proposed by Jawaharlal Nehru, seconded by Sardar Patel — was later ratified at the All India Congress Committee meeting at Gowalia Tank, Bombay, on 8 August 1942.
  • The draft of the resolution was prepared by Mahatma Gandhi himself, with the help of Jawaharlal Nehru (and, per the Ghatnachakra explanation, also Maulana Abul Kalam Azad).
  • Gandhi, confident the moment was opportune, expressed impatience to proceed even if Congress itself was hesitant — but Congress followed his lead, marking unity in the decision for mass action.

'Kurnool Circular' (29 July 1942)

The Provincial Congress Committee in Andhra issued the 'Kurnool Circular', drafted by Kala Venkat Rao, and sent for CWC approval through Dr. Pattabhi Sitaramaiah. It outlined strategic directives for the movement — but was seized when the Kurnool Congress office was raided by police.

Context — Azad's presidency Maulana Abul Kalam Azad was Congress President throughout this entire phase. He was elected in the Ramgarh Session (1940), and — because no Congress session was held from 1941–1945 — he continued as President for six consecutive years, the longest tenure of any Congress President before independence, spanning the most politically fragile years of the freedom struggle. He negotiated with both the Cripps Mission and Lord Wavell.

The four demands of the Quit India Resolution

  • Immediate end to British rule in India.
  • Commitment of Free India to resist fascism and imperialism.
  • Formation of a Provisional Government after British withdrawal.
  • Sanction of Civil Disobedience against British rule.

"Do or Die" — Gandhi's mantra

Gandhi's rallying cry, "Do or Die" ("Karo Ya Maro"), underscored the movement's intensity and urgency, urging unity and non-violent resistance. Full sense of the statement: "I am not going to be satisfied with anything short than complete freedom... Today I give you a mantra 'Do or Die'. This mantra means that either we will free our country or will die fighting for Independence."

Repeatedly tested — attribution trap
  • "Do or Die" was said by Mahatma Gandhi — not Nehru, Azad, or Savarkar (71st BPSC 2025; 70th BPSC 2024 explicitly rules out Subhas Chandra Bose and Nehru).
  • It is associated specifically with the Quit India Movement — not the Dandi March, Non-Cooperation, Khilafat, Swadeshi, or Civil Disobedience movements (tested across UPPCS, I.A.S., Uttarakhand, Chhattisgarh papers repeatedly).
  • The statement "We shall either free India or die in the attempt" is the same Quit India slogan, occasionally quoted as a separate-sounding line (U.P. PCS 2009).

Pattabhi Sitaramayya remarked on Gandhi's speech that day: he was speaking as though "motivated with the power of Avatar and Paigambar."

Preparation, Operation Zero Hour & the arrests (9 August 1942)

  • Gandhi built momentum through civil disobedience and propaganda in the run-up.
  • The British pre-emptively arrested Congress leaders in the early hours of 9 August under "Operation Zero Hour"; Congress was declared unlawful under the Criminal Law Amendment Act (1908); Rule 56 of the Defence of India Rules banned public meetings.
  • Gandhi and Sarojini Naidu were kept at the Aga Khan Palace, Pune (Poona) — not Yerawada Jail or Naini Jail.
  • Members of the Congress Working Committee — Nehru, Abul Kalam Azad, Govind Ballabh Pant, Dr. Prafulla Chandra Ghosh, Dr. Pattabhi Sitaramayya, Dr. Syed Mahmud, Acharya Kripalani — were taken to Ahmednagar Fort.
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad, a CWC member, could not reach Bombay; he was arrested in Patna (under the Bihar Security Rules per one source) and sent to Patna (Bankipur) Jail.
  • Aruna Asaf Ali hoisted the tricolour at the Gowalia Tank ground on 9 August, becoming one of the movement's most celebrated symbols.
  • Baldev Sahai resigned from the post of Advocate General in 1942 in protest against Operation Zero Hour and other repressive acts.

Spread, Underground Leadership & Suppression

Sabotage, secret radio, parallel governments — and the price paid

Public rampage, sabotage & participation

  • Acts of defiance — hoisting flags, courting arrest — spread nationwide.
  • Sabotage in U.P. and Bihar: bridges, railway tracks, telegraph lines were targeted; in the first week alone, official assessment recorded ~250 railway stations damaged/destroyed and over 500 post offices and 150 police stations attacked.
  • Strikes in Ahmedabad, Bombay, Jamshedpur, Ahmednagar, and Poona; students led strikes and circulated illegal news sheets. The labour class, however, did not broadly engage (monetary constraints, fear of repression, effect of trade unions).
  • Movement of 1942 showed maximum impact in Bombay, Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, United Provinces, and Madras — though whole-country participation was needed (and largely occurred) for it to succeed.
  • Leadership went underground, coordinating activities across Bombay, Poona, Satara, Gujarat, Bihar, and Delhi.
Underground radio & the Congress Radio
Run by
Usha Mehta — a valued member of the small underground Congress group that operated the secret Congress Radio, broadcast from different parts of Bombay and audible up to Madras (U.P.PCS 1997; 67th BPSC Re-Exam 2022; I.A.S. 2011).
Regular broadcaster
Ram Manohar Lohia — directed the movement while underground and made regular broadcasts on Congress Radio (U.P. Lower Sub. 2008).
Not the broadcaster
Not Subhadra Kumari Chauhan, Sucheta Kripalani, or Annie Besant (67th BPSC Re-Exam distractors).

Jayaprakash Narayan's escape & the Azad Dasta

  • JP Narayan gained recognition as a national leader specifically through the Quit India Movement — the main leaders having been arrested at the outset, he (with Lohia and Aruna Asaf Ali) stepped up to consolidate public support secretly.
  • On 9 November 1942 — the day of Deepawali/Diwali — JP Narayan escaped from Hazaribagh Jail along with Yogendra Shukla and comrades.
  • Shukla was later arrested at Muzaffarpur and sent to Patna, where he was brought on 7 December 1942.
  • JP Narayan organised the All India Azad Dasta at Bakri Ka Tapu, in the Terai region of Nepal, to wage a guerrilla war against the British.

Louis Fischer & international solidarity

Louis Fischer, an American journalist and Gandhi's biographer, was with Gandhi at Sevagram Ashram, Wardha just before the movement — arriving in India in May 1942 and spending a week with him. Fischer, along with American intellectuals Pearl Buck, Edgar Snow, Fredrick L. Schuman, and Norman Thomas, publicly demanded India's independence.

Key underground/alternate leadership figures

Rammanohar Lohia, Jayaprakash Narayan, Aruna Asaf Ali, Usha Mehta, Biju Patnaik, Chhotubhai Puranik, Achyut Patwardhan, Sucheta Kripalani, and R.P. Goenka provided alternate leadership to the movement once the top leadership was jailed.

Government's response & the human cost

  • 9 August 1942: all top Congress leaders arrested and moved to undisclosed locations; CWC, AICC and Provincial Congress Committees declared unlawful under the Criminal Law Amendment Act (1908).
  • Martial law was not formally declared, but baton charges, tear gas and gunfire were used on crowds.
  • Death toll estimated at around 10,000.
  • Press freedom suppressed; military took control of numerous cities; police and secret service exercised significant authority.
  • Defiant villages faced heavy fines and mass floggings.
  • Riots were widest in Bihar and the United Provinces — centres of self-instilled mass revolt included Eastern U.P. (Azamgarh, Ballia, Gorakhpur) and Bihar's Gaya, Champaran, Saran, Purnia, Shahabad, Muzaffarpur, Bhagalpur districts.
  • Farmer groups worked their fields by day and rioted at night; their ferocity echoed 1857 — Viceroy Linlithgow, in a telegram to Churchill, termed it "by far the most serious rebellion" since the Revolt of 1857 (I.A.S. 1999 assertion-reason: both true, the peasant upsurge explains the rebellion label).
  • Munger district, Bihar was the district most affected by uprooted railway lines — cut off from the outside world for two weeks; Medinipur (Bengal) and Baliya (U.P.) were the other major centres of public anger (43rd BPSC 1999).

Bihar's own martyrs & leaders

  • Shri Krishna Singh, known as "Bihar Kesari," was an active Quit India participant and went on to become Bihar's first Chief Minister (69th BPSC 2023).
  • Rampyari Devi, wife of Jagat Narayan Lal, addressed the assembly at Bankipur and called on people to quit government jobs.

Women martyrs of 1942 — and a common trap

  • Matangini Hazra — 73 years old, fell to British bullets leading a protest march at Tamluk, Bengal.
  • Kanaklata Barua — an AISF leader who joined the "Mrityu Bahini" (Death Army), a youth death squad from the Gohpur sub-division, Assam, in line with the "Do or Die" call; shot dead in September 1942 at about age 17 while bearing the national flag, near Borangabari, Gohpur (Sonitpur district), Assam.
  • Aruna Asaf Ali — remembered for hoisting the flag at Gowalia Tank.
  • Gangaram and Kheemdev (also recorded as Gangadutt and Kheemananda) — two brothers martyred during police firing at Almora during the Quit India Movement.
U.P. PCS 2023 — trap Shanti Ghosh was not associated with the Quit India Movement — she belonged to the earlier revolutionary group Yugantar and, with Suniti, shot dead a District Magistrate on 14 December 1931.

Gandhi's fast (February 1943) & "Operation Rubicon"

  • While imprisoned at the Aga Khan Palace, Gandhi undertook a fast in response to the British government's demand that he condemn violence — he instead directed his protest against the state's own use of force.
  • The fast drew widespread public support: hartals, demonstrations and strikes at home and abroad.
  • Three members of the Viceroy's Executive Council resigned in solidarity.
  • Outcomes: boosted public morale, strengthened anti-British sentiment, allowed a surge in political activity, and exposed the government's authoritarian actions.
  • "Operation Rubicon" was the secret code word the British government proposed to use in India if Gandhi died during this fast unto death (R.O./A.R.O. 2017).

Pakistan Day & Gandhi's release

  • Pakistan Day was observed on 23 March 1943, marking a milestone in the demand for a separate Muslim state.
  • Gandhi was released on 5/6 May 1944 on account of poor health (the two source dates differ by a day across texts).
  • On his release, Churchill remarked: "When we are winning around the globe how can we be defeated by this old man who is our enemy for a long time."
Context — was Quit India really "non-violent"? I.A.S. 2011 and U.P. R.O./A.R.O. (Re-Exam) 2016 both test this: the claims "it was a non-violent movement" and "it was led by Mahatma Gandhi" are individually not fully correct — Gandhi never got to actually lead it (he was jailed before it began), and it was far from non-violent (railway/post-office/police-station attacks, Gandhi's own refusal to condemn the violence as "an answer to the huge violence of the rulers"). What is correct: it was spontaneous, and Congress was declared an illegal body. An assertion–reason pairing (U.P. PCS 2005) further affirms that the Quit India Movement marked the culmination of the national movement, after which a mechanism for the transfer of power had to be found — both statements true, reason correctly explains assertion.

Rajagopalachari Formula & the Gandhi–Jinnah Parleys

1944 · an individual attempt to bridge the Congress–League divide

The Rajagopalachari (CR) Formula

  • In 1944, C. Rajagopalachari (CR), a senior Congress leader, proposed a plan — later published as a pamphlet titled "The Way Out" — tacitly acknowledging the League's demand for Pakistan, aimed at solving the constitutional deadlock (I.A.S. 2010).
  • Gandhi endorsed the plan, signalling Congress's willingness to cooperate.
Key points of the CR Formula
ProvisionContent
IndependenceThe Muslim League would endorse Congress's demand for independence.
Provisional GovernmentLeague would collaborate with Congress to form a provisional central government.
PlebisciteAfter World War II, a plebiscite in Muslim-majority North-West and North-East India to decide on a separate sovereign state.
Post-Partition agreementIf partition occurred, a joint agreement would safeguard defence, commerce, and communications between the two entities.
Population transferAny transfer of population would be voluntary.
British handoverImplementation contingent on Britain transferring full powers to India.

Opposition

  • Jinnah: demanded Congress accept the two-nation theory outright; wanted only Muslims in the North-West/North-East (not the whole population) to vote in the plebiscite; opposed any common centre for defence/governance.
  • Hindu leaders — notably Vir Savarkar — condemned the plan as an acceptance of the League's Pakistan demand.

Gandhi–Jinnah Parleys (1944)

After Gandhi's release on 5 May 1944, he proposed negotiations with Jinnah based on the CR Formula. Jinnah rejected it, accusing Rajagopalachari of "mutilating" the original Lahore Resolution. Gandhi opposed the two-nation theory and advocated independence for all of India; Jinnah insisted on Muslim self-determination as a separate nation. Key sticking points: the plebiscite (League rejected it) and a common centre for defence/commerce/communications. Talks collapsed after two weeks over these ideological differences.

Desai–Liaquat Pact

1945 · a provisional-arrangement attempt that also failed
  • Objective: establish a provisional central-governance arrangement and ease Congress–League tension.
  • Equal Representation: proposed an equal number of Congress and League representatives in the central legislature for an interim government.
  • Minority Representation: reserved 20% of seats for minorities.
  • Outcome: no final agreement — but its emphasis on equal representation signalled a potential shift toward parity between Congress and the League, and its failure underscored the persistent divide over India's future.

Shimla Conference (June 1945)

  • Objective: finalise the Wavell Plan, focusing on self-governance for India.
  • Muslim League's position: demanded that Muslim council members be League nominees (to avoid Congress-nominated minority influence); sought a two-thirds-majority veto for decisions affecting Muslim interests.
  • Congress's position: opposed the plan as limiting Congress to caste Hindus; insisted on the right to nominate members from all communities.
  • Breakdown: Wavell's mistake was declaring the talks a failure, inadvertently giving the Muslim League de facto veto power in council matters — this strengthened Jinnah's influence, visible in the 1945–46 elections.

Subhas Chandra Bose — Life, Ideology & the Forward Bloc

1897–1939 · from the ICS to open revolt against Gandhian strategy
Early life
AspectDetail
Birth23 January 1897, in Cuttack, Odisha (Orissa) division of Bengal province.
FamilyFather Janakinath Bose was a prominent lawyer; brother Sarat Chandra Bose was a notable freedom fighter.
EducationSchooling in Cuttack and Calcutta; graduated from Scottish Church College (Calcutta University) in 1919; passed the Indian Civil Services exam in 1920 — he later resigned from the ICS to join the freedom struggle.
Political guruC.R. Das (R.O./A.R.O. 2017).

Congress career & break

  • Joined the INC, advocating immediate independence and opposing Gandhi's non-violence strategy.
  • Elected President of the INC in the 1938 Haripura session (Gujarat) and again the 1939 Tripuri session (Madhya Pradesh) — 71st BPSC 2025 clarifies 1938 was Haripura, not Tripuri, as sometimes mis-stated.
  • Resigned the Congress presidency in 1939 after the Tripuri crisis, over ideological conflicts with Gandhi.
  • Founded the Forward Bloc on 3 May 1939 to advocate a more aggressive anti-colonial stance (I.A.S. 2005; UP Lower Sub. 2008; ruling out Sardar Sinhji Rana, JP Narayan, Lakshmi Sahgal as founders).
  • Escaped house internment/arrest in January 1941, disguised, successfully leaving Calcutta (71st BPSC 2025 confirms this escape date; note the assembly's provisional-government date is Oct 1943, not 1943's own escape year — commonly conflated).

Titles & ideology

  • Rabindranath Tagore gave him the title "Desh Nayak" (National Leader) in 1938 at Shantiniketan, after Bose became Congress President.
  • Gandhi called him "Patriot of Patriots."
  • The honorific "Netaji" was first applied to him in Germany in early 1942, by Indian soldiers of the Indische Legion and by German/Indian officials of the Special Bureau for India in Berlin (67th BPSC).
  • Bose advocated complete independence, anti-imperialism, and a socialist, secular India; he supported armed resistance and sought alliances with Germany and Japan during WWII.

Germany, the Free Indian Legion & the Birth of the INA

1941–43 · Mohan Singh's INA before Bose's arrival

Bose in Germany

  • Reached Germany on 3 April 1941; with German government help, founded the "Free India Centre," printing pamphlets and giving speeches for India's independence.
  • Adolf Hitler approved and permitted Bose to organise an army for the independence war in late 1941.
  • Bose recruited about 10,000 Indian war prisoners captured under General Erwin Rommel in North Africa, forming the "Free Indian Legion" (also called the Indische Legion) — the correct answer over Lala Hardayal, Ras Behari Bose, and V.D. Savarkar as founders (I.A.S. 2008).

The Indian National Army — Mohan Singh phase

  • The INA's brainchild is credited to Giani Pritam Singh (Dhillon), working with Japanese Major Iwaichi Fujiwara, who had reached an agreement of collaboration with the Japanese at Bangkok in December 1941 — this predates the actual formation of the army (U.P. PCS 2001: the "brainchild" answer is Pritam Singh + Fujiwara, not Mohan Singh or Bose).
  • When the British Army retreated in the war with Japan, Captain Mohan Singh (a British Indian Army officer) joined hands with the Japanese; Japan handed over captured Indian soldiers to him to recruit into an Indian army.
  • In December 1941, in the Malay forests, the Punjab battalion led by Mohan Singh was defeated by the Japanese army; Lt. Col. L.V. Fitz Patrick (British) was among the POWs.
  • In February 1942 (also recorded as 15 July 1942 in one source), under Mohan Singh's leadership, the Indian National Army was formed from defeated Indian soldiers of the British Army's Malay expedition.
  • The INA was officially constituted in September 1942; Captain Mohan Singh became its first Commander — he was the first operational commander, not the originator of the idea.
  • After the fall of Singapore, Singh had enlisted about 40,000 men by the end of 1942, in response to the potential Japanese occupation of India.
  • The unit was dissolved in December 1942 after disagreements/distrust between Mohan Singh and the INA leadership, and between the League leadership (notably Rash Behari Bose) — but most volunteers later rejoined the INA's second incarnation under Subhas Chandra Bose.

Rash Behari Bose (1886–1945) — and a name-confusion trap

  • Fled to Japan in 1915 after revolutionary activities in India; founded the Indian Club in Tokyo, opposing Western imperialism.
  • Established the Indian Independence League in 1942, unifying independence efforts.
  • On 4 July 1943, handed over INA leadership to Subhas Chandra Bose (Supreme Commander) at Singapore; had earlier offered to resign the presidency to Subhas at their meeting in Tokyo (June 1943).
  • Died on 21 January 1945, after a lung collapse in February 1944.
Trap — do NOT confuse the two "Bose"s Ras Behari Ghosh (1845–1921) is a completely different person: Bengal-born, London-educated lawyer, Congress President in 1907 and 1908, key figure in the Surat Split (1907), promoted women's education/social upliftment for lower castes. He has no INA connection.

International recognition

The Azad Hind Government was recognised by 9 countries (70th BPSC 2024): Japan, Germany, Italy, Thailand, Burma (Japanese-controlled), the Philippines (Japanese-sponsored Second Republic), Manchukuo (Japanese puppet state in Manchuria), the Independent State of Croatia, and the Japanese-controlled government in Nanking, China.

Japan handed the Andaman & Nicobar Islands to the INA on 6 November 1943. Bose renamed Andaman as "Shaheed" (Shahid) Dweep and Nicobar as "Swaraj" Dweep (67th BPSC Re-Exam 2022).

  • "Give me blood, I'll give you freedom" ("Tum Mujhe Khoon Do, Mai Tumhe Azadi Dunga") — said by Bose in Burma while motivating soldiers; his slogan endures as a symbol of resistance (M.P. PCS 2000).
  • "Chalo Delhi" / "Delhi Chalo" — the battle cry Bose gave on reorganising the Azad Hind Fauj (71st BPSC 2025).

Address to Gandhi & other associates

  • Sheel Bhadra Yajee, a Bihar freedom fighter, joined Bose in founding the All India Forward Bloc and was actively associated with the INA movement (60th–62nd BPSC 2016).

Post-War National Scenario

June–September 1945 · Britain's attitude shifts

Change in government attitude

  • June 1945: ban on Congress lifted, leaders released.
  • Wavell Plan failed to resolve the constitutional deadlock.
  • July 1945: Labour Party took power in Britain — Clement Attlee as Prime Minister (26 July 1945), Pethick Lawrence as Secretary of State for India.
  • August 1945: elections announced for central and provincial assemblies, followed by a plan to convene a constituent assembly in September, aligned with the Cripps Offer's principles.

Why Britain's attitude changed

  • Global power shift: UK's decline, rise of USA and USSR, both sympathetic to India's independence.
  • Labour sympathy: the new government was more sympathetic to Indian demands.
  • Resource strain: UK owed £1.2 billion to India by 1945.
  • Anti-imperialist sentiment from resistance to colonial rule in Vietnam and Indonesia.
  • Fear of revolt: threat of agrarian uprisings and disaffection among the army and INA veterans.
  • Inevitability of elections: post-war elections became unavoidable, prompting British retreat.

Three Upsurges & the Naval Mutiny

Winter 1945–46 · the final straw for British colonialism

The three upsurges

DateEvent
21 November 1945Calcutta protest over INA trials — students (Forward Bloc, SFI activists) protested at Dalhousie Square, faced police lathi charges, retaliated with stones; two deaths.
11 February 1946Calcutta demonstrations against the seven-year sentence handed to INA officer Rashid Ali; Muslim League students defied Section 144, faced arrests/lathi charges.
18 February 1946Bombay Naval Strike initiated by Royal Indian Navy ratings — around 1,100 ratings from HMIS Talwar struck against racial discrimination and poor treatment, raising the tricolour, crescent, and hammer-and-sickle flags together.

Three-stage pattern

  • Stage I — Defiance of authority: the three flashpoints above.
  • Stage II — Citywide participation: Calcutta and Bombay faced near-paralysis; strikes and attacks on police, shops, and transport hubs.
  • Stage III — National solidarity: students organised boycotts/hartals, sparking sympathetic strikes in military establishments across India; the Royal Indian Air Force also joined the protests.
  • On 23 February 1946, the ratings surrendered after assurances from Patel and Jinnah against victimisation.

The Naval Mutiny's significance

  • Sailors protested poor conditions and racial discrimination, seeking independence; the mutiny gained widespread support from the populace, political leaders, and military factions (including the RIAF).
  • Exposed British vulnerability; attracted international pressure, particularly from the U.S. and Soviet Union, urging independence.
  • Fostered rare unity between Congress and the Muslim League; highlighted the impracticality of continued British rule; accelerated the transfer of power.

Evaluation & Congress's response

  • Militant spirit: the upsurges, including the RIN revolt, symbolised the looming end of British rule.
  • British concessions: Dec 1946 — trials limited to serious offences; Jan 1947 — pardons for initial INA prisoners; Feb 1947 — withdrawal of Indian soldiers from Indo-China/Indonesia; finalisation of the Cabinet Mission and a parliamentary delegation to India.
  • Limited participation: mainly militant groups, urban-focused; British repression mechanisms remained largely effective.
  • Congress strategy: leftist concern over loss of control and discipline in an independent India; viewed the upsurges as extensions of earlier nationalist movements but refrained from endorsing violent uprisings; favoured negotiation — Gandhi opposed the mutiny, urging resolution through established leadership.

The Cabinet Mission

Cabinet Mission — identity card
AspectDetail

Recommendations (announced 16 May 1946)

  • A Union of India, embracing British India and the princely states, controlling only foreign affairs, defence, and communications; all other subjects to remain with the provinces/states.
  • The Muslim League's demand for a full-fledged Pakistan was rejected.
  • The Union to have an Executive and Legislature drawn from British India Provinces and Princely State representatives.
  • A Constituent Assembly to be formed from Provincial Legislative Assembly, Chief Commissioners' Provinces, and Princely State representatives.
  • Reconstitution of the Viceroy's Executive Council, with all portfolios (including War) held by Indians (I.A.S. 2008).
  • An interim National Government to be formed with leaders of Indian parties.
  • Reasons for rejecting full Pakistan: non-Muslim populations of 38% in the North-West and 48% in the North-East; risk of splitting Hindu-majority Bengal and Sikh/Hindu-majority Punjab; disruption of regional ties and military security.

Grouping of provinces

GroupProvinces
Group A (Hindu-majority)Madras, Bombay, Central Provinces (& Berar), United Provinces, Bihar, Orissa — plus Delhi, Ajmer-Marwar & Coorg (Chief Commissioners' Provinces).
Group B (Muslim-majority)Punjab, North-West Frontier Province, Sindh — plus Baluchistan.
Group C (Muslim-majority)Bengal, Assam.
  • Congress: preferred optional grouping (feared it paved the way for Pakistan); opposed removal of veto power.
  • Muslim League: insisted on compulsory grouping as a step toward Pakistan.
  • Provinces could exit their groups after elections; communal issues in the central legislature to be decided by simple majority of both communities present.

Federal structure & the Constituent Assembly (as proposed)

  • Proposed a three-tier government — provincial, sectional, and union levels (a distinctive Cabinet Mission innovation, testable against Nehru Committee/Simon Commission/Cripps Mission distractors).
  • Constituent Assembly of 389 members: 292 from provincial assemblies, 93 from princely states, 4 from Chief Commissioners' provinces.
  • Seats allotted roughly at one representative per 10 lakh population, on a communal basis, across three voter classes: General, Muslims, and Sikhs (Sikhs only in Punjab).

Objections, acceptance & withdrawal

  • Congress objections: opposed mandatory grouping of NWFP/Assam; disagreed with the absence of elected princely-state representatives.
  • League objections: insisted on compulsory grouping; expected to be invited to form the interim government after Congress's initial rejection.
  • Muslim League accepted the plan on 6 June 1946.
  • Congress accepted on 24 June 1946.
  • Provincial elections for the Constituent Assembly held July 1946: 208 Congress members elected, 73 Muslim League candidates won, 15 others elected (total 296 British-India seats). M.A. Jinnah was not a member of the Constituent Assembly.
  • Nehru's statement (10 July 1946): suggested grouping might not be necessary, given objections from NWFP and Assam.
  • On 29 July 1946, the League withdrew its acceptance and called for "Direct Action" from 16 August 1946, demanding Pakistan — triggering violent communal riots in Calcutta, Bombay, Noakhali, Bihar, and Garhmukteshwar with thousands of deaths.
I.A.S. 2002 — "last opportunity" trap The rejection of the Cabinet Mission Plan (not the Cripps Mission, CR Formula, or Wavell Plan) is identified as the last opportunity lost to avoid partition.

Socialist-Party dissent

The National Executive of the Indian Socialist Party (1946) called for dissolution of the (indirectly elected) Constituent Assembly and its re-election by universal adult suffrage — leaders JP Narayan and Ram Manohar Lohia argued it did not adequately represent common Indians (Rajasthan PCS 2024).

"...still wanted a united India. There was to be the Federal Union composed of British provinces."
Describes the Cabinet Mission (I.A.S. 1999) — not the Simon Commission, Gandhi-Irwin Pact, or Cripps Mission.

The Constituent Assembly

Idea to institution

Genealogy of the idea

  • Earliest non-official draft: the Swaraj Bill of 1895, influenced by Bal Gangadhar Tilak's thinking.
  • Gandhi first raised the idea in 1922 — that the Indian Constitution should be made per India's needs.
  • 8 February 1924: Motilal Nehru officially moved the "National Demand" resolution in the Central Legislative Assembly, calling for a round-table conference to establish a constitution-making body.
  • M.N. Roy then proposed the demand; Jawaharlal Nehru reiterated the basic concept.
  • The Congress Faizpur Session (December 1936) officially reaffirmed and elaborated the idea of a Constituent Assembly (U.P.UDA/LDA 2001 — over the Swaraj Party 1935 / Muslim League 1942 / All Parties Conference 1946 distractors).
  • The Constituent Assembly was formed on the recommendation of the Cabinet Mission Plan (U.P.PCS Mains 2008; U.P.UDA/LDA 2010) — the idea's genealogy is older, but its formal creation traces to the Cabinet Mission.

Wavell's "Breakdown Plan" & Attlee's February Statement

May 1946 – February 1947

Wavell's Breakdown Plan (May 1946)

Lord Wavell proposed this to the Cabinet Mission as a compromise between "repression" and "scuttle":

  • Withdrawal of British Forces: British Army/officials would retreat to Muslim provinces in the North-West and North-East.
  • Transfer of power over the rest of India to Congress.
  • Recognition of reality: suppressing a Congress-led rebellion was increasingly untenable.
  • Desire for partition: some British officials sought a division resembling "Northern Ireland" within Pakistan.

Independence with partition — the interpretive frame

  • Imperialist view: Britain viewed independence as fulfilling its "mission" to facilitate Indian self-government; partition seen as a consequence of the Hindu-Muslim divide and failed consensus on power transfer.
  • Radical view: independence resulted from mass actions of 1946–47, in which Communists emerged as leaders; but bourgeois Congress leaders, fearing revolutionary upsurge, opted to negotiate with the imperialists — compromising national unity.
  • The Hindu Mahasabha explored establishing a separate Hindu province in West Bengal.
Attlee's declaration & the path to Mountbatten
DateEvent
20 February 1947Attlee announced in the House of Commons that British rule would end by 30 June 1948 at the latest, regardless of a constitutional agreement — power could go to a central authority or directly to provincial governments in Muslim-majority areas; British responsibility over princely states would also lapse, hinting at partition (similar to the Cripps Offer). This decision aimed to resolve deadlock and demonstrate British sincerity, per Lord Wavell's own assessment of Britain's declining control.
—The announcement triggered violence in Punjab, collapsed Khizr Hayat Khan's coalition, and pushed Congress toward accepting partition, while the League saw it as validation.

Mountbatten arrives as Viceroy

  • Lord Mountbatten served as Viceroy 12 Feb 1947 – 15 Aug 1947 — more decisive than his predecessors, with delegated powers for on-the-spot decisions.
  • He came with specific instructions to keep India united if possible (I.A.S. 1998) — not to Balkanise it, accept Jinnah's demand outright, or simply persuade Congress toward partition — but was authorised to act per Indian circumstances and minimise British loss.
  • He aimed to explore unity/division options until October 1947, but quickly recognised the Cabinet Mission Plan's futility, with Jinnah adamant on a sovereign state.
  • Between 24 March – 6 May 1947, across 133 recorded talks with Indian politicians, Mountbatten concluded the Cabinet Mission Plan was impractical, drafting an alternative he called "Plan Balkan" — the brainchild of Lord Mountbatten himself (I.A.S. 2000).
  • Gandhi's suggestion (31 March 1947, first Gandhi–Mountbatten meeting): as an alternative to partition, Gandhi suggested inviting Jinnah to form/lead the government — Congress leaders (including Patel and Nehru) and the CWC did not support this (I.A.S. 2000).
  • The "Dickie Bird Plan," floated before the final Mountbatten Plan, suggested provinces become independent successor states instead of forming an Indian Union or two dominions; Nehru rejected it, warning of Balkanisation and conflict — it was cancelled.

  • Congress conceded the demand for a unified India while securing: princely states could not remain independent (must join India or Pakistan); independence for Bengal ruled out; accession of Hyderabad to Pakistan ruled out (Mountbatten supported Congress here).

The Radcliffe Line & the Indian Independence Act

June–August 1947

Boundary Commissions

  • Mountbatten set up the Punjab Boundary Commission and Bengal Boundary Commission on 30 June 1947, to determine the India–Pakistan division in Punjab and Bengal.
  • Cyril Radcliffe chaired both commissions — the correct answer over A.P. Moon and A.V. Alexander (68th BPSC 2022). Each commission had 4 members (judges): 2 from Congress, 2 from the Muslim League.
  • The commissions demarcated boundaries based on contiguous majority areas of Muslim/non-Muslim population, among other factors — and were tasked with delineating borders (not solving the minorities problem broadly, effecting the Independence Bill itself, or enquiring into East Bengal riots — I.A.S. 2014).
  • The Radcliffe Award, ready by 12 August 1947, was deliberately delayed by Mountbatten and published on 17 August 1947 — two days after Independence, absolving the British of responsibility for the ensuing disturbances.

Execution of the Mountbatten Plan

  • Legislative assemblies in Bengal and Punjab voted for partition: East Bengal and West Punjab became part of Pakistan; West Bengal and East Punjab remained with India.
  • A referendum in Sylhet led to its incorporation into East Bengal (Pakistan).

Integration of Princely States

1946–48
  • The State People's Movement (1946–47) demanded political rights and representation in the Constituent Assembly; Nehru chaired the All India State People's Conference at Udaipur (1945) and Gwalior (April 1947), warning non-participating states would be treated as hostile.
  • Phase I — Instrument of Accession: by 15 August 1947, all states except Kashmir, Hyderabad, and Junagadh had signed, granting central control over defence, external affairs, and communication.
  • Phase II — Integration: complex integration into Kathiawar, Vindhya, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh; Hyderabad, Mysore, and Travancore-Cochin retained their boundaries; privy purses were offered, and some princes became Governors or Rajpramukhs.
  • On 15 February 1948, 222 princely states (217 from the Kathiawar Agency, 5 others) merged to form the Saurashtra Sangh (M.P. PCS 2023).
