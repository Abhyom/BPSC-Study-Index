Heat, Temperature & Temperature Scales

🟨 Concept   🟦 Fact/Number   🟩 Person/Institution   🟥 MCQ Trap   🟪 Rule/Formula

1. What Exactly Is Temperature?

If heat is energy on the move, temperature is the property that decides which way it will move. Temperature is a measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of a body, and at the microscopic level it is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the random motion of the particles.

AVERAGE KINETIC ENERGY PER MOLECULE
\(\overline{KE} = \dfrac{3}{2}\,k\,T\)
\(k\) = Boltzmann's constant, \(T\) = absolute temperature. Note that no property of the gas — mass, size, chemical nature — appears here. Only \(T\).

Temperature decides the direction of heat flow. Heat flows hot → cold, always.

3. Understanding Thermal Equilibrium

If two objects with different temperatures touch, energy migrates from the warmer to the cooler one. As the hotter body loses heat and the colder one gains it, they eventually settle at the same thermal state. Once this occurs, all overall energy transfer ceases, and the system is described as being in thermal equilibrium.

The defining concept is net flow. Even at this balanced state, microscopic energy exchanges persist; but because the rate of giving and receiving is identical, there is no net heat flow.

EPFO (AO/EO) 2017

4. Temperature Scales

(i) Celsius Scale (°C)

Ice point 0°C, steam point 100°C; the gap divided into 100 equal parts.

(ii) Fahrenheit Scale (°F)

Ice point 32°F, steam point 212°F; gap divided into 180 equal parts.

Designed by Gabriel Fahrenheit in 1717.

(iii) Kelvin Scale (K)

Ice point 273 K (exactly 273.15 K), steam point 373 K (373.15 K).

This is the absolute scale: its zero is the theoretically lowest temperature in the universe, so Kelvin never takes a negative value.

(iv) Reaumur Scale (°R)

Ice point 0°R, steam point 80°R, i.e. 80 equal divisions.

One degree Reaumur = the 80th part of the interval between the fixed points. Designed by R. A. Reaumur in 1730.

(v) Rankine Scale (°Ra)

Ice point 460°Ra, steam point 672°Ra; 1°Ra = the 212th part of the difference between the two fixed points.

5. Conversion Formulas

CELSIUS ↔ FAHRENHEIT
\(\dfrac{C}{5} = \dfrac{F-32}{9}\)
Rearranged: \(F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32\) and \(C = \frac{5}{9}(F-32)\).
CELSIUS ↔ REAUMUR
\(\dfrac{C}{5} = \dfrac{R}{4}\)
Combined master relation: \(\dfrac{C}{5}=\dfrac{F-32}{9}=\dfrac{R}{4}=\dfrac{K-273}{5}\).
CELSIUS ↔ KELVIN
\(K = C + 273.15\)
The Kelvin degree and the Celsius degree are the same size; only the zero differs.

8. Absolute Zero

At this point the volume would theoretically become zero and all molecular motion would cease. This is absolute zero — the theoretically lowest possible temperature, which can be approached but never actually attained. It is the zero of the Kelvin scale.

Thermometers, Thermal Control & Thermal Expansion

Chapters 3–4 · Measuring instruments, the liquid choice, and how matter grows with heat

1. Types of Thermometers

(i) Clinical Thermometer

A mercury-in-glass thermometer for human body temperature. Because body temperature varies over a short range, its scale is marked only from 96°F to 110°F (or 35°C to 42°C).

Placed under the tongue, mercury expands and is pushed past the constriction.

Never sterilise it in hot water — the mercury would expand too much and shatter the glass.

(ii) Electronic Thermometer

Uses the fact that electrical resistance of a conductor depends on its temperature. The key component is a thermoresistor / thermistor; a circuit measures that resistance and converts it into a digital temperature display.

(iii) Optical Pyrometer

For very high temperatures — a blast furnace, molten metal, a star — no liquid thermometer can survive contact.

It measures temperature from a distance by comparing the brightness and colour of radiation emitted by the hot body with a calibrated filament. Used for 1000°C and above.

NDA (I) 2009

2. Choosing the Liquid: Mercury versus Alcohol

Boiling point about 357°C about 78°C

Wets the glass? No — does not stick to the tube Yes — clings to the tube

Why alcohol in cold countries?

  • Mercury freezes at about −39°C. In polar or high-altitude regions the air can be colder than this, so a mercury thermometer would simply be a solid lump and would read nothing.
  • Alcohol stays liquid down to about −112°C, so it can record extremely low temperatures.
  • Alcohol also expands far more than mercury for the same temperature rise, giving a longer, more easily read column.

4. The Thermostat

A thermostat is not a measuring device — it is a controlling device. It automatically senses temperature and switches a heating or cooling appliance on and off so the temperature stays within a set range. Refrigerators, air conditioners, geysers, electric irons and ovens all contain one. Its function is to maintain a constant temperature, and in doing so it also saves energy.

5. Thermal Expansion of Solids — Three Types

1. LINEAR EXPANSION — increase in length
\(\alpha = \dfrac{\Delta L}{L_0 \,\Delta T}\)
Change in length per unit original length per °C rise. Unit: \(^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\) or \(\text{K}^{-1}\).
2. SUPERFICIAL EXPANSION — increase in area
\(\beta = \dfrac{\Delta A}{A_0 \,\Delta T}\)
Change in area per unit original area per °C. Also called areal expansion.
3. CUBICAL EXPANSION — increase in volume
\(\gamma = \dfrac{\Delta V}{V_0 \,\Delta T}\)
Change in volume per unit original volume per °C.

6. The Golden Ratio 1 : 2 : 3

FOR THE SAME MATERIAL
\(\alpha : \beta : \gamma = 1 : 2 : 3\)
Equivalently \(\beta = 2\alpha\) and \(\gamma = 3\alpha = \tfrac{3}{2}\beta\). This single line solves most numerical MCQs on expansion.

At ordinary temperatures, gases expand far more than liquids and solids.

NDA 2018

7. Anomalous Expansion of Water — A Special Case

Water refuses to follow the ordinary rule. Watch what happens as water is warmed from 0°C:

0°C → 4°C

The volume of water decreases instead of increasing.

Beyond 4°C

The volume increases normally with temperature.

Consequence

The density of water is maximum at 4°C — one of the reasons aquatic life survives on this planet.

Water also expands on freezing.

  • When water turns to ice, the molecules lock into an open hexagonal lattice with wide gaps. The volume increases by about 9%, which is why ice is less dense than water and floats.
  • The reverse is equally true: when ice melts, its volume decreases.
  • This is why a sealed water bottle bursts in the freezer, and why metal water pipes crack in a hard winter.
RAS/RTS (Pre) 2007

Q21. When a piece of ice floating in a glass of water melts, the level of water will

(a) Fall   (b) Rise
(c) Remain the same   (d) Fall or rise depending on the temperature of water
Show Answer
(c) Remain the same. A floating ice cube displaces exactly its own weight of water; on melting it produces exactly that weight of water, filling precisely the volume it had displaced.

Specific Heat, Thermal Capacity, Calorimetry & Latent Heat

Chapters 5–6 · The two heats: one that raises temperature, one that hides from the thermometer

1. Specific Heat

Specific heat \(s\) is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of unit mass of a substance through 1°C.

SPECIFIC HEAT
\(s = \dfrac{Q}{m\,\Delta t} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad Q = m\,s\,\Delta t\)
\(Q\) = heat supplied, \(m\) = mass, \(\Delta t\) = rise in temperature. SI unit: \(\text{J kg}^{-1}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\).

Key facts about specific heat

  • It is a property of the material alone — completely independent of the mass and shape of the body.
  • Specific heat of water at 15°C is 4200 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹ (i.e. 1 cal/g°C) — remarkably high compared with almost every other common substance.
  • Car radiators use water as coolant, because a given mass of water can carry away a large quantity of heat from the engine.
  • Deserts are scorching by day and freezing at night, because sand has a very low specific heat — it absorbs heat readily and its temperature shoots up during the day, then radiates it away just as readily at night.
  • Oceans moderate climate. Because of water's high specific heat, the sea warms up much more slowly than land in summer and cools much more slowly in winter. The temperature contrast between continents and oceans is therefore greater in summer than in winter.
NDA (I) 2018

Q23. Body A of mass 2 kg and body B of mass 4 kg, of the same material, are kept in the same sunshine for some interval of time. If the rise in temperature is equal for both bodies, which statement is correct?

2. Heat Capacity (Thermal Capacity)

Thermal capacity is the heat required to raise the temperature of a given amount of a substance through 1°C or 1 K. Unlike specific heat, it does depend on how much substance you have.

THERMAL CAPACITY
\(C = m \times s\)
Unit: \(\text{J K}^{-1}\) or \(\text{cal }^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\). Depends on both the nature of the body and its mass.

Specific heat = material property, mass-independent. Thermal capacity = body property, mass-dependent. Examiners swap these two constantly.

3. Molar Specific Heat of Gases

For gases we count in moles rather than grams. Molar specific heat is the heat required to raise the temperature of 1 mole of gas by 1°C. Because a gas can be heated in two very different ways, there are two such values.

C_V — at constant volume

Heat needed to raise 1 mole through 1°C while volume is held fixed. The gas cannot expand, so it does no work, and all the heat goes into internal energy.

C_P — at constant pressure

Heat needed to raise 1 mole through 1°C while pressure is held fixed. Here the gas expands and does external work, so extra heat is needed. Hence C_P is always greater than C_V.

MAYER'S FORMULA
\(C_P - C_V = R\)
\(R\) = universal gas constant \(\approx 1.99 \approx 2\ \text{cal mol}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}\).
RATIO OF SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITIES
\(\gamma = \dfrac{C_P}{C_V}\)
Always greater than 1, since \(C_P > C_V\).

4. Principle of Calorimetry

Calorimetry is the practical method of determining specific heat. The instrument is a calorimeter — a cylindrical copper vessel fitted with a stirrer and a lid.

When two substances at different temperatures are brought into contact, heat flows from the hotter to the colder until both reach a common temperature. Assuming no heat escapes to the surroundings:

PRINCIPLE OF CALORIMETRY
\(Q_{\text{lost}} = Q_{\text{gained}}\)
Heat lost by the hotter substance = heat gained by the colder substance. Valid only when there is no change of state.
CISF-LDC 2018

(a) T = (T₁ + T₂ + T₃)/3   (b) T = (M₁T₁ + M₂T₂ + M₃T₃)/(M₁ + M₂ + M₃)
(c) T = (M₁T₁ + M₂T₂ + M₃T₃)/[3(M₁ + M₂ + M₃)]   (d) T = (M₁T₁s + M₂T₂s + M₃T₃s)/(M₁ + M₂ + M₃)

5. Change of State

Process Change Heat is
Melting (Fusion) Solid → Liquid Absorbed
Freezing (Solidification) Liquid → Solid Released
Boiling / Vaporisation Liquid → Gas Absorbed
Condensation Gas → Liquid Released
Sublimation Solid → Gas directly Absorbed
Deposition Gas → Solid directly Released
Melting Point

Temperature at which a solid turns liquid at a given pressure. Ice 0°C, wax about 60°C, iron about 1538°C.

Boiling Point

Liquid turns to vapour throughout the bulk. Ethanol 78°C, water 100°C, mercury 357°C.

Freezing Point

For a pure substance, the freezing point of the liquid and the melting point of the solid are exactly the same temperature — water freezes at 0°C and ice melts at 0°C.

Sublimation

Some solids skip the liquid stage entirely: ammonium chloride, iodine, camphor, naphthalene (moth balls). The reverse is deposition.

Supercooling

Cooling a liquid below its freezing point without it actually solidifying.

6. The Triple Point

There is one unique combination of temperature and pressure at which the solid, liquid and gaseous phases of a substance can all coexist in equilibrium. That point is called the triple point.

By international agreement the triple point of water is fixed at 273.16 K (0.01°C) at a partial vapour pressure of 611.657 pascals (about 0.006 atm). This point was formerly used to define the kelvin, the SI base unit of thermodynamic temperature.

Do not confuse 273.15 K (ice point, 0°C) with 273.16 K (triple point, 0.01°C).

7. Latent Heat — Heat That Does Not Raise Temperature

Latent heat is the heat absorbed or released per unit mass during a change of state, at constant temperature. The word latent means "hidden" — the heat is hidden from the thermometer.

LATENT HEAT
\(Q = m\,L\)
\(L\) = latent heat. Unit: cal/g or J/kg.
LATENT HEAT OF FUSION (ICE)
\(L_f = 80\ \text{cal/g}\)
\(= 3.34 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/kg}\). Heat to change unit mass of solid into liquid at constant temperature.
LATENT HEAT OF VAPORISATION (WATER)
\(L_v = 536 \approx 540\ \text{cal/g}\)
\(= 22.6 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/kg}\) at 100°C. Heat to change unit mass of liquid into vapour at constant temperature.

Points examiners love

  • Latent heat is fixed at a given temperature for a given substance, and is independent of the volume of the substance.
  • It does depend on the temperature (and hence on pressure) at which the change of state occurs.
  • \(L_f\) of ice (80 cal/g) is NOT equal to \(L_v\) of water (540 cal/g) — a favourite trap in statement-based questions.
  • On a temperature-versus-heat graph, latent heat shows up as the flat, horizontal portions of the curve — temperature constant while heat is being supplied.

8. Numerical Drill — The Staged Method

Every mixed-state numerical is just stages added together: sloping stages use \(Q = ms\Delta t\), flat stages use \(Q = mL\). Remember specific heat of ice = 0.5 cal/g°C and of water = 1 cal/g°C.

Boiling Point & Pressure, Evaporation, Humidity and Cooling

Chapters 7–8 · Why cookers work, why sweat cools, and why a cooler fails in the monsoon

1. Why Pressure Controls the Boiling Point

A liquid boils when the pressure of the vapour escaping from it becomes equal to the pressure of the atmosphere pressing down on it. This single sentence explains everything in this unit.

External pressure reduced

The vapour needs to reach only a smaller pressure to escape, so the liquid boils at a lower temperature.

External pressure increased

The vapour has to work harder to escape, so the liquid boils at a higher temperature.

2. Boiling at High Altitudes

As you climb a mountain, the column of air above you gets shorter and thinner, so atmospheric pressure falls. Consequently water boils below 100°C — perhaps 90°C or less at a high hill station.

This creates a practical problem. Cooking depends on temperature, not on the mere fact of boiling. Water at 90°C simply cannot cook rice or pulses properly, so food takes far longer at high altitudes — or never cooks at all. Two remedies are used:

Add salt to the water

A dissolved solute raises the boiling point (elevation of boiling point), so the water gets hotter before it boils.

Use a pressure cooker

This is why pressure cookers are far more common in mountain households.

3. Effect of Dissolved Salt in Water

Boiling point ↑Elevation of boiling point — dissolved salt lowers the vapour pressure of the solution, so a higher temperature is needed to match atmospheric pressure
Freezing point ↓Depression of freezing point — dissolved particles interfere with the formation of the ice crystal lattice, so the solution must be cooled further before it freezes

Summary in four words: boiling point up, freezing point down.

4. The Pressure Cooker

A pressure cooker is a sealed vessel. As the water inside boils, the steam produced cannot escape, so it accumulates and the pressure inside rises well above one atmosphere. Because the pressure is higher, the boiling point of the water rises to about 120°C. Food therefore cooks at 120°C instead of 100°C, and cooking is much faster.

Note the exact logic — it is not that "steam cooks faster", but that the boiling point of water is raised.

Atmospheric pressure is measured by a barometer.

5. Evaporation versus Boiling

Evaporation Boiling
Occurs at any temperature, even room temperature Occurs only at one fixed temperature — the boiling point
A surface phenomenon — only molecules at the surface escape A bulk phenomenon — bubbles form throughout the liquid
A slow, quiet process A rapid, vigorous process
Causes cooling of the remaining liquid Temperature stays constant during boiling

What makes evaporation faster?

  1. Higher temperature — molecules move faster and more of them have enough energy to escape.
  2. Lower humidity — dry air can accept more water vapour, so evaporation is rapid. In humid air the rate falls sharply.
  3. More wind / moving air — the vapour is carried away instead of accumulating above the liquid.
  4. Larger surface area — more molecules are exposed at the surface.

6. Cooling by Evaporation

This is the single most productive idea in the unit. When a liquid evaporates, the molecules that escape are the most energetic ones. They take their energy — the latent heat of vaporisation — away with them, drawing it from the liquid and from whatever surface the liquid is sitting on. The result is that evaporation always produces cooling. Evaporation of sweat is therefore an endothermic process — heat is absorbed from the body.

  • Sweating. Sweat evaporates by absorbing latent heat from the skin and the blood beneath it, so body temperature falls. This is the body's chief temperature-regulating mechanism.
  • A fan makes you comfortable. The fan does not supply cold air — it simply moves air across the skin, which makes the sweat evaporate faster, and faster evaporation produces more cooling.
  • The earthen pot (surahi). The pot wall has countless tiny pores. Water seeps through them and evaporates from the outer surface, drawing the latent heat needed from the water still inside. The water in the pot therefore becomes cool.
  • Desert (evaporative) cooler. Air is drawn through wet pads; the evaporating water cools the air. It works best on hot, dry days, because high temperature and low humidity both speed up evaporation. It is useless in humid weather.
  • Cotton clothes in summer. Cotton absorbs sweat readily and exposes it for easy evaporation. Synthetics such as polyester do not absorb sweat, so they give no cooling.

7. Humidity

Absolute Humidity

The actual amount of water vapour present in a unit volume of air. Measured in grams per cubic metre (g m⁻³).

Relative Humidity

The ratio of the vapour actually present in a given volume of air at a given temperature to the amount required to saturate that same volume at that same temperature. Expressed as a percentage.

Why relative humidity falls when temperature rises: warmer air can hold more vapour, so the denominator of the ratio grows. If the actual vapour content (the numerator) does not change, the ratio must fall.

Transmission of Heat & Thermodynamics

1. The Three Modes of Heat Transfer

Mode What moves Medium needed? Occurs mainly in
Conduction Energy passes molecule to molecule; particles stay in place Yes Solids
Convection The heated particles themselves move in bulk Yes Liquids and gases
Radiation Electromagnetic waves No All — even vacuum

2. Conduction

In a solid, the molecules are locked into fixed positions. When one end is heated, those molecules vibrate more vigorously and pass the extra energy on to their neighbours by collision, which pass it on further, and so on down the line. Energy travels along the rod, but the molecules themselves do not move from their positions. Everyday proof: heat one end of a metal rod and the other end soon becomes hot.

Thermal Conductivity

Thermal conductivity measures how well a solid conducts heat through it. Copper and silver are excellent conductors; wood, glass, cork, air and saw dust are poor conductors (insulators).

\[\text{Steel} \;>\; \text{Water} \;>\; \text{Wood} \qquad \text{(order of thermal conductivity)}\]

Why air is the secret ingredient in every winter garment

  • Air is a very poor conductor of heat. Trapping a layer of still air around the body is the most effective way to stop body heat escaping.
  • Woollen clothes keep us warm because wool fibres have large spaces between them in which air is trapped. (The wool protein is itself a poor conductor too, but trapped air is the main reason.)
  • A new quilt is warmer than an old one because it encloses a larger amount of air.
  • Two thin blankets are warmer than one thick blanket of the same total thickness, because a layer of air is trapped between the two.
  • Birds swell their feathers in winter to trap a thicker layer of air between body and feathers.
  • Saw dust conducts worse than the wood it came from, because of air trapped between the particles — which is why ice is packed in saw dust.

Why iron feels colder than wood in winter: both are at exactly the same room temperature — a thermometer would read the same for each. In winter the body is warmer than the room. Iron is a good conductor, so it rapidly draws heat away from your hand, and you register that rapid loss as "cold". Wood is a bad conductor, so it takes heat away only slowly and feels far less cold.

4. Radiation and Its Three Laws

Radiation is the transfer of heat from one body to another without any actual movement of the particles involved and without heating the intervening medium. Because it is electromagnetic in nature, radiation needs no medium at all — it travels perfectly well through a vacuum. This is how the sun's energy reaches the earth across 150 million kilometres of empty space. It travels in a straight line and at the speed of light.

Thermal radiations belong to the infrared region of the electromagnetic spectrum, and are therefore also called infrared radiations.

(i) KIRCHHOFF'S LAW
\(\dfrac{e_\lambda}{a_\lambda} = E_\lambda\)
At a given temperature and wavelength, the ratio of emissive power to absorptive power is the same for all substances and equals the emissive power of a perfectly black body. In plain language: a good absorber is a good emitter, and a poor absorber is a poor emitter.
(ii) STEFAN'S LAW
\(E \propto T^{4} \quad \Rightarrow \quad E = \sigma T^{4}\)
Heat radiated by unit area of a surface in one second is directly proportional to the fourth power of the absolute temperature of the body.
(iii) WIEN'S DISPLACEMENT LAW
\(\lambda_m \times T = b\)
\(b = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m·K}\). At a given absolute temperature of a black body, the product of the wavelength of maximum emission and \(T\) is constant.

Kirchhoff's law in action: the silvered surface of a thermos flask is a bad absorber, so ice inside does not melt easily. By Kirchhoff's law it is also a bad emitter/radiator, which is why hot liquids inside do not cool quickly.

Wien's law in action: hotter objects glow at shorter wavelengths — a cool star is red, a hotter one white, a very hot one blue. Wien's law is used to compute the temperature of the sun and the stars.

  • White and light-coloured clothes are good reflectors and therefore poor absorbers of heat. They reflect most of the radiation falling on them, so we stay cooler — hence white clothes in summer.
  • Black and dark-coloured clothes are good absorbers of heat, so they are preferred in winter.

5. Newton's Law of Cooling

NEWTON'S LAW OF COOLING
\(-\dfrac{dQ}{dt} \propto (T_{\text{body}} - T_{\text{surroundings}})\)
The rate of cooling of a body is directly proportional to the temperature difference between the body and its surroundings.

The practical meaning is that a hot body cools quickly at first and then more and more slowly as it approaches the temperature of its surroundings. The temperature therefore falls off exponentially with time, not linearly.

The Refrigerator

A refrigerator does not "make cold". It removes heat from inside the cabinet and dumps that heat into the room, by circulating a refrigerant through a closed loop.

Evaporator coils (inside the cabinet)

The liquid refrigerant evaporates, absorbing latent heat from the food and air inside. This is what makes the inside cold.

Compressor

The vapour is compressed, which raises its temperature.

Condenser coils (black grille at the back)

The hot vapour condenses back to liquid, releasing that heat into the room.

Expansion valve

The liquid passes through and the cycle repeats. A thermostat switches the compressor on and off to hold the interior at a constant temperature.

Cooling is therefore produced by the evaporation and expansion of a compressed gas.

The open-door paradox

  • If you leave the refrigerator door open in a closed room, the room does not get cooler — it gets slightly warmer.
  • The fridge takes heat from the air in front of it and throws that heat out of the condenser at the back. Those two cancel out.
  • But the compressor motor is also consuming electrical energy, and that energy ends up as heat in the room. So the room gains heat overall.
  • Safest temperature for keeping food fresh: about 4°C (roughly 36–38°F) — slows bacterial growth without freezing the food.
Antifreeze

In cold countries, water in a car radiator would freeze and burst the engine block. Ethylene glycol is added as antifreeze — a mixture of 60% ethylene glycol and 40% water freezes only at about −45°C.

Cold Storage of Food

Even after harvesting, fruits continue to respire, which ripens and eventually spoils them. Respiration, like all biochemical processes, slows at lower temperature — so cold storage decreases the rate of respiration and extends storage life.

7. Thermodynamics — Zeroth and First Law

ZEROTH LAW OF THERMODYNAMICS
\(T_A = T_C,\; T_B = T_C \;\Rightarrow\; T_A = T_B\)
If two bodies A and B are separately in thermal equilibrium with a third body C, then A and B will be in thermal equilibrium with each other. This is what makes a thermometer meaningful.
FIRST LAW OF THERMODYNAMICS
\(\Delta Q = \Delta U + \Delta W\)
\(\Delta Q\) = heat supplied to the system, \(\Delta U\) = change in internal energy, \(\Delta W\) = work done by the system. Heat given to a system is used up in two ways: raising internal energy, and enabling external work.

9. The Second Law

The first law tells you that energy is conserved, but it says nothing about direction. It would be perfectly happy with a cup of tea spontaneously growing hotter while the room grows colder — energy would still be conserved. Hence a second law is needed.

(i) Kelvin–Planck Statement

It is impossible for an engine operating in a cyclic process to extract heat from a reservoir and convert it completely into work. Some heat must always be rejected to a sink.

Air conditioning is based on this statement.

(ii) Clausius Statement

It is impossible for a self-acting machine, unaided by any external agency, to transfer heat from a colder to a hotter reservoir.

The refrigerator is based on this — it does move heat from cold to hot, but only because external work is supplied by the compressor.

The second law in one sentence each

  • Kelvin–Planck: you cannot build an engine that is 100% efficient.
  • Clausius: heat will not flow uphill on its own.
  • Every energy transformation dissipates some energy in a less useful form. This is why, in an ecosystem, only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next — the rest is lost as metabolic heat.
