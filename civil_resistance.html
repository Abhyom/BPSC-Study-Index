Sanyasi Revolt — extended detail

  • Participants: landless peasants, displaced zamindars, disbanded soldiers, impoverished masses, plus active Sanyasis and Fakirs.
  • Character: attacks on British factories, seizing goods, cash, arms and ammunition — a unified Hindu-Muslim monastic resistance.
  • "Vande Mataram" is derived from Anandamath by Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, whose plot is based on the Sanyasi Revolt; it was first sung at the Calcutta Congress session of 1896 (primarily by Rabindranath Tagore, per one account).

Wahabi Movement — extended detail

  • Proponent: Syed Ahmad Barelvi of Rae Bareli — inspired by Abdul Wahab of Arabia, but in India the movement was chiefly inspired by Shah Waliullah of Delhi.
  • Spread to the North-West Frontier Provinces, Bengal, Bihar, Kabul and Central Provinces.
  • Captured Peshawar in 1830, issued coins in his own name; killed at the Battle of Balakot (1831).
  • After his death, leadership passed to Maulavi Qasim, Vilayat Ali, Inayat Ali, Ahmadullah; Patna became the movement's chief centre, later branches in Hyderabad, Madras, Bengal, UP and Bombay.
TrapChief centre of the Wahabi Movement in the 19th century was Patna, not Lahore, Amritsar or Pune (UPPCS 1994).

Namdhari / Kuka Movement — socio-religious reform among Sikhs

  • Initiated by Bhagat Jawahar Mal (Sai Sahib) and Baba Balak Singh — devotion to one God, spiritual purity, revival of Sikh values.
  • Developed under Baba Ram Singh (1816–1885), who organised the Namdhari sect; movement called "Kuka" because followers shouted ecstatic cries ("Kuk") during devotion.
  • Baba Ram Singh preached monotheism, Naam Simran, moral discipline; opposed caste system, female infanticide, early marriage, exchange of daughters in marriage; popularised inexpensive Anand marriages.
  • The 1872 Kuka uprising in Punjab against cow slaughter & British authority was severely suppressed; Ram Singh died in 1885 in exile as a state prisoner at Mergui, Burma (Myanmar), where he had been held since 1872.
TrapKuka Movement was founded/organised by Guru Ram Singh — not Guru Ram Das, Guru Nanak, or Guru Gobind Singh (45th BPSC 2001). It originated in Punjab, not Bengal, Bihar or Maharashtra.

Pagal Panth & the Faraizis

  • Pagal Panth: semi-religious group founded by Karim/Karam Shah; son Tipu Shah (leader from 1813 after his father's death, formally from 1852) organised tribal peasants (of the Garo tribe) against zamindar oppression in Sherpur (Mymensingh, earlier Bengal), 1825–1833.
  • Tipu Shah declared land belonged equally to all as God's creation; with his mother Chandi Bibi (Pir-Mata), established an independent administration at Gar-jaripa (a mud fort) for over two years, appointing a judge, magistrate and collector.
  • Cause worsened after the 1820 division of Sherpur zamindari — litigation and Company financial pressure raised tax burdens.
  • Tipu Shah arrested multiple times (Dec 1824–Jan 1825), tried and imprisoned; leaders Janku Pathor and Dubraj Pathor continued the movement afterward.
  • Government eventually responded with rent reduction/concessions; the movement divided and subsided.
  • Faraizis: followers of Haji Shariatullah of Faridpur, Eastern Bengal — radical religious, social, political change. Son Dudu Miyan organised followers against oppressive landlords and Indigo planters; revolt ran 1818–1862, later merging into the Wahabi Movement.
TrapPagalpanthi rebellion belonged to the Garo tribe — not Bhils, Gonds or Koliyas (UPPCS 1999).

Vasudev Balwant Phadke & the (later) Ramosi Uprising

  • Vasudev Balwant Phadke (1845–83) — one of the earliest Indian revolutionaries; organised the Ramoshi tribes into a fighting force and founded the Ramosi Krishak Jatha / Ramoshi farmers' group (39th BPSC 1994).
  • Protested the British government's oppressive land-tax policies and its neglect during the Deccan famine of 1876–1878.
  • Lacking funds, turned to raiding government treasuries — first raid at Dhamari (Pune district) targeting stored income tax; further treasury raids at Shirur and Khed talukas to support starving villagers; strategically disabled British communication lines.
  • Uprising weakened when British forces ambushed and shot a leader, Naik; Phadke was eventually arrested, sentenced to life, and died in 1883 after a hunger strike against colonial rule in the Andamans (Kala Pani).
Context — distinguish from the earlier tribal Ramosi UprisingA separate, earlier tribal Ramosi Uprising (1822–1829) in the Western Ghats (Karnataka/Maharashtra/MP) was led by Chittur/Chittar Singh, Umaji Naik and Bapu Trimbakji Sawant — former Maratha soldiers resisting British annexation of Satara (see Ch. 3). Phadke's 1877–87 movement is a distinct, later event with the same community name.
TrapGeographical location of the (tribal) Ramosi Uprising was the Western Ghats — not Eastern Ghats, Eastern India or Western India (56th–59th BPSC 2015).

Chronological ordering — pre-1857 revolts (high-yield PYQ pattern)

Trap — correct chronological order
  • Bengal Sepoy Mutiny (1764) → Vellore Mutiny (1806) → Kutch Rebellion (1816–32) → Kol Uprising (1831–32) → Santhal Revolt (1855–56) (R.A.S./R.T.S. 2013).
  • Revolt sequence — Sanyasi (1763–1800) → Indigo Revolt (1859–60) → Kuka/Namdhari Rebellion (peak 1871–72) → Pabna Movement (1873–76) (UPPCS 2025).
  • Also: Sanyasi (1763–1800) → Santhal (1855–56) → Indigo Revolt (1859–60) → Deccan Riots (1875) — Indigo Revolt is the one that occurred in Bengal immediately after the Revolt of 1857 (IAS Pre 1994).

Military Uprisings Before 1857

Sepoy discontent that foreshadowed the Great Revolt
Causes
Discrimination
Sepoys faced unequal pay and limited promotions compared to British soldiers.
Mistreatment
Frequent abuse by British officials fuelled resentment.
Foreign Service Allowance
Sepoys were not compensated when sent on distant postings (denial of allowance).
Religious concerns
The General Service Enlistment Act (1856) required sepoys to serve overseas, conflicting with high-caste Hindu beliefs; replacement of the turban with a leather cockade (1806) and orders to cross the sea (loss of caste) were other flashpoints.
Alignment with civilians
Sepoys shared the wider population's social, religious and economic discontent.

Santhal Rebellion — full picture

  • Took place in the Damin-e-Koh area between Bhagalpur and the Rajmahal hills; moneylenders and the British exploited Santhals together, charging interest ranging from 50 to 500%.
  • Four brothers — Sidhu, Kanhu, Chand, Bhairav — led the revolt; over ten thousand Santhals assembled at Bhognadih near Barhait, Sahibganj district, on 30 June 1855, resolving to fight the "Dikus" (corrupt government officials/outsiders), not the "Sahibs" as such.
  • Sidhu reportedly told officials: "God said to me that this country is not of bosses. God will fight from our side..."
  • Santhals defeated a British contingent under Major Burroughs at Pirpainati; Gokko was an important leader of Godda, and Mahajan Deen Dayal Rai is another notable name in this context — but Santhals never held sovereignty over the Ganga Valley near Muzaffarpur.
  • Finally suppressed by Commissioner of Bhagalpur Brown and Major General Lloyd; Sido was arrested and hanged, Kanu was killed in action.
  • Aftermath: a separate territory called Santhal Parganas was created; it became illegal for a Santhal to transfer land to a non-Santhal (Santhal Pargana Tenancy Act) — both measures confirmed (I.A.S. 2018).
  • The Santhal Pargana region was called Narikhand in ancient times (also "Kajangala" in Buddhist literature); during the Mahabharata period it was part of Anga Mahajanapada.
TrapChief leader of the Santhal Revolt is usually named as Sidhu (also accept Sidhu-Kanhu jointly) — not Birsa Munda, Bhima Nayak or Swami Govindgiri, who belong to different revolts. Santhal Hool took place 1855–56, not 1857–58.

Bhil Uprising — extended detail

  • Bhils were concentrated in the hill ranges of Khandesh (Deccan table land); British occupation of Khandesh in 1818 enraged them over outsider incursion.
  • Officials blamed Peshwa Bajirao II and his representative Trimbakji Dengle for inciting the Bhils, but agricultural hardship and fear of the new English government were the real reasons.
  • General insurrection in 1819; under Sevaram, Bhils revolted again in 1825 — ruthlessly suppressed a second time.
  • Later, in the second half of the 19th century, Surji Bhagat and Govind Guru (Lasodhiya) brought reforms among Bhils of Mewar, Dungarpur, Banswada, Pratapgarh, Sirohi and Pali; Govind Guru established the Samp Sabha (1883) to organise Bhils — considered the founder of the Lasodhiya Movement.

Chuar & Koli — trap notes

TrapChuar Revolt belongs to Bengal (Bankura & Midnapore) — not Madhya Pradesh (UPPCS Mains 2015 flags this as the incorrect pairing). Koli revolt correctly pairs with Gujarat.

Ho & Munda Revolt — Birsa Munda's Ulgulan

  • 1765: Chotanagpur came under British control as part of Bengal, Bihar & Orissa; the Raja of Singhbhum sought British protection in 1767, acknowledged British feudal control in 1820.
  • Ho Revolt (1820–21): centred in the then Santhal subdivision of Bihar (now Singhbhum district, Jharkhand). Hos, initially British allies, eventually broke away and united with the Mundas, leading to the Kol Uprising (1831–33).
  • Rebellion targeted dikkus (non-Adivasi landowners) — burning houses, casualties. British land policies undermined the traditional Khuntkatti system (joint tenures); missionary activity undermined traditional culture; moneylender exploitation of land intensified suffering.
  • Birsa Munda (working area Ranchi, extending to Bhagalpur, area of influence between Ranchi Khunt and the Singhbhum side including Chakradharpur): the earlier agrarian agitation was the Sardari fight (Sardari Larai); Birsa's later revolt is the Munda Rebellion / Ulgulan ("Great Tumult"), aiming to establish an independent Munda Raj — not a modern state like present-day Jharkhand.
  • In 1895, Birsa proclaimed himself a prophet/Messenger of God with healing powers; promoted abstinence from alcohol, renunciation of animal sacrifice, wearing the sacred thread, worship of one god Sing Bonga in the Sarna groves — a revivalist movement to purge foreign elements from Munda society (also blended Hindu–Christian idioms).
  • Called for tribals to "colour this land red with their blood like a Red Flag" against non-tribals — earned him the title Jagat Pita / Dharti Aaba ("Father of the Earth").
  • On Christmas Eve 1899, Mundas shot arrows and burned churches across six police stations in Ranchi & Singhbhum districts; in January 1900 police stations were targeted; rumours of an attack on Ranchi (8 Jan) caused panic; rebels defeated 9 January 1900.
  • Birsa was captured while asleep at Jamkopai forest, Chakradharpur (West Singhbhum) on 3 February 1900; died ambiguously in jail on 9 June 1900. Nearly 350 Mundas were tried — 3 hanged, 44 transported for life.
  • Impact: the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908) was enacted to protect tribal rights; Birsa is revered as a tribal hero and freedom-struggle symbol.
  • Thakkar Bapa (Amritlal Vithaldas Thakkar) — General Secretary of the Harijan Sevak Sangh — was the first to use the word "Adivasi" for tribal people (I.A.S. 1995), deeply influenced by Gandhi.
Traps
  • "Ulgulan"/Great Tumult refers to Birsa Munda's Revolt (1899–1900), not the 1857 Revolt, Mappila Rebellion, or Indigo Revolt (I.A.S. 2020).
  • Working region of Birsa Munda was Ranchi — not Champaran or Ballia.
  • Kol Revolt (1831–32) was centred on Singhbhum — not Kutch, Western Ghats or Satara (66th BPSC 2020).

Kol Mutiny — extended detail

  • Led by Buddho Bhagat in Chotanagpur, region covering Ranchi, Singhbhum, Hazaribagh, Palamau, Manbhum.
  • New land laws (1831) brought in outsiders — Hindu, Sikh, Muslim farmers and moneylenders — causing massive exploitation of tribes.
  • Kol rebels killed about a thousand outsiders; order restored only after large-scale military operations.

Bhumij Revolt

  • Led by Ganga Narain; occurred 1832–33 in the Jungle Mahal region of East India, beginning as an inheritance dispute in the Zamindari of Barabhum (West Bengal), which snowballed into armed conflict against British rule — persecution of the Bhumij tribe by both the king and exploitative British policies (Jharkhand PCS 2021).

Context — two different "Rampa" revolts, do not merge
  • An earlier Rampa Uprising (1879–80) was a forest-rights protest against the 1882 Madras Forest Act's restriction on shifting cultivation.
  • The Rampa/Manyam Rebellion (1922–24) led by Alluri Sitarama Raju is a separate, later, better-known event against forced labour and forest-produce restrictions — despite the shared name, PYQs test them as distinct episodes.

Rani Gaidinliu / Jadonang — extended detail

  • British authorities, fearful of the Heraka movement's growing influence, arrested and executed Jadonang in 1931; Gaidinliu was captured in 1932 and sentenced to life imprisonment.
  • The movement had reformist religious objectives but strong political undertones challenging British rule; lasting cultural and political impact on Naga resistance.
  • Haipou Jadonang is credited as the first tribal leader inspired by Mahatma Gandhi and his philosophy — pioneer freedom fighter for the Naga community of Manipur (Jharkhand PCS 2013).

Part III · Peasant Movements
From scattered agrarian revolt to organised Kisan Sabhas

Peasant movements surged due to economic hardship and rising political awareness, focused on tenancy rights, fair rents, and debt relief.

Sanyasis, Faraizis, and the Pagal Panthis

Peasant Movements — Second Phase (After 1857)

From Indigo to Tebhaga — the long organised struggle

Indigo Revolt (1859–60), Nadia, Bengal

  • Leaders: Bishnucharan Biswas & Digambar Biswas (the "Biswas brothers") of Nadia — two former employees of an indigo producer; also Rafique Mondal of Malda and Kader Molla of Pabna.
  • Began at Govindpur village, Nadia district, West Bengal; peasants resisted coerced indigo cultivation for European planters under fraudulent contracts, coercion and threats.
  • In September 1859 cultivators revolted against landlords; peasants attacked indigo factories with spears and swords, staged rent strikes; movement spread to Nadia, Pabna, Khulna, Dhaka, Malda, Dinajpur; all indigo factories shut down.
  • Bengal's intelligentsia supported the movement through public meetings, journals and newspaper articles; Harishchandra Mukherjee, editor of Hindu Patriot, played a major role.
  • Dinabandhu Mitra's play Neel Darpan (1858–59/1860) visualised the atrocities of indigo planters on farmers.
  • British response: the Indigo Commission (1860) addressed abuses — a victory for peasants, as indigo cultivation declined by 1860, and further declined by the early 20th century due to its unprofitability in the world market from new (synthetic-dye) inventions — not primarily due to peasant resistance, national leaders' opposition, or government control (I.A.S. 2020).
Traps
  • The Indigo Rebellion was fundamentally about peasants not wanting to grow indigo but being forced to grow it at an unacceptable price — not a simple refusal, not a flag-colour protest (B.P.S.C. 2018).
  • Neel Darpan was written by Dinabandhu Mitra — not Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, Rabindranath Tagore, Sharat Chandra Chatterji, or Akshay Kumar Datta (tested repeatedly: UK PCS 2024, 42nd/66th BPSC, UPPCS 2015).
  • The Indigo Revolt occurred in Bengal immediately after the Revolt of 1857 — not the Santhal Rebellion or Pabna Disturbances (I.A.S. 1994).

Deccan Peasants' Uprising (1875), Poona, Satara & Ahmednagar (64th BPSC)

  • Leaders: Maratha Peasants (no single named leader).
  • The Ryotwari system favoured moneylenders (Vanis) over cultivators (Kunbis); high taxation, increased land revenue, cotton-price collapse and poor harvests compounded the hardship.
  • Nature: social boycott of moneylenders (particularly Marwari & Gujarati outsiders); violent attacks on moneylenders' homes/shops; destruction of debt bonds; spread across Poona, Ahmednagar, Sholapur, Satara.
  • Outcome: the Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act (1879); support from Maharashtra's intelligentsia.
TrapThe immediate cause of the Deccan Riots of 1875 was both the shadow of famine and high interest rates charged by Mahajans — a "more than one of the above" answer, not any single option alone (64th BPSC 2018).

Pabna Movement (1873–1885), Pabna, East Bengal

  • Leaders: Shah Chandra Roy, Shambhu Nath Pal, Khodi Mollah.
  • Illegal property seizure, arbitrary rent hikes, coercive eviction despite Act X of 1859 (which granted immunity from eviction); oppression via forcible evictions, crop seizures, prolonged litigation.
  • Formation of the Agrarian League (1873) in Yusufshahi Pargana — organised rent strikes, raised funds for legal battles, held mass meetings; armed resistance plus legal challenges.
  • Movement mainly intense 1873–1876 against zamindari oppression, illegal abwabs (forced levies), and rent enhancement.
  • Outcome: the Bengal Tenancy Act (1885) clarified tenant rights on land — after this, the movement became mainly a legal dispute; intellectual support came from Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, R.C. Dutt, and the Indian Association under Surendra Nath Banerjee.

Ramosi Uprising (1877–87), Maharashtra — Vasudev Balwant Phadke

See Ch.1 for full detail (Dhamari/Shirur/Khed raids, disabled communication lines, death by hunger strike 1883). Listed here as the peasant-movement dimension of the same 1877–87 episode — protest against oppressive land tax and neglect during the Deccan famine of 1876–78.

Punjab Peasants' Revolt (last decade of 19th c., Punjab)

  • The Punjab Land Alienation Act (1900) (63rd BPSC) aimed to restrict land-ownership transfers, especially targeting alienation of land from peasants to non-agricultural classes.
  • Imposed a 15-year limit on land transactions, prohibiting urban professionals and traders from buying land from peasants.

Champaran Satyagraha (1917), Champaran, Bihar

  • Leaders: Mahatma Gandhi, J.B. Kripalani, Babu Brajkishore Prasad, Babu Rajendra Prasad.
  • The "Teen Kathia" system forced tenant farmers to cultivate indigo on three-twentieths of a bigha of their land for British planters, causing severe financial losses; peasants could endure the system or pay higher rent to abandon indigo cultivation.
  • Gandhi initiated satyagraha (peaceful civil disobedience); the government invited Gandhi for talks and appointed him to a committee, whose findings led to abolition of the Teen Kathia system.

Kheda Satyagraha (1918), Kheda, Gujarat

  • Leaders: Mahatma Gandhi, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Mohanlal Pandya.
  • Poor harvests in 1917–18 and rising prices of essential goods worsened peasants' economic strain; revenue-remission demands were ignored by the colonial government.
  • Local leaders such as Mohanlal Kameshwar Pandya were arrested for defying government orders.
  • Outcome: government instructed restraint in revenue collection and refrained from land confiscation — relief for peasants, recognising the need for Indian support during British war efforts.

The Kisan Sabha Movement (1918–1921)

  • Background: after 1857, Awadh taluqdars regained control and imposed high rents and oppressive practices; the Home Rule Movement and activists Gauri Shankar Mishra and Indra Narayan Dwivedi led formation of Kisan Sabhas in UP.
  • The United Provinces Kisan Sabha was established in February 1918, with support from Madan Mohan Malaviya; by June 1919, it had expanded to 450 branches.
  • Awadh Kisan Sabha (1920): urged peasants to refuse till bedakhali land, not offer unpaid labour, resolve disputes through panchayats. Repression by government led to decline; the Awadh Rent (Amendment) Act further contributed to its end.

Eka Movement (1921), Awadh, UP

  • Leaders: Madari Pasi and Baba Ramchandra (68th BPSC).
  • Economic distress from high rents, forced labour, corruption by intermediary thekedars; sparked by drought, food shortages, and the effects of the Spanish flu.
  • Started by farmers of Hardoi, Behraich/Bahraich, Sitapur and other UP districts; the movement's main contention was the collection of 50% more tax than the accepted rate.
  • Initially aligned with Congress & Khilafat campaigns, followed Gandhian methods (social boycotts, picketing, peaceful protest); as it grew more militant, Congress and Khilafat leaders distanced themselves and it became independent.
  • Outcome: repression led to the movement's collapse by March 1922.

Mappila / Moplah Uprising (1921), Malabar region, Kerala

  • Leaders: Mappila tenants (no single named leader identified).
  • Oppression by upper-caste Hindu landlords and British land laws stripped Mappilas of customary land rights — high rents, oppressive fees, insecure tenures; the Khilafat movement and Congress support for tenants' grievances laid the groundwork.
  • Mappila tenants were predominantly Muslim; Hindus were zamindars — centred at Tirurangadi.
  • Initially anti-government and anti-landlord, the rebellion evolved into a communal conflict after the arrest of key leaders like Ali Musaliar, becoming increasingly violent, targeting Hindus perceived as supporting authorities.
  • British imposed martial law and created the Malabar Special Police; by December 1921 the movement ended, marked by its communal turn.
TrapMoplah Revolt (1921) took place in Malabar, Kerala — not Telangana, Vidarbha, Marathwada, Kashmir, Assam or the B.N.W.F.P.

Bardoli Satyagraha (1928), Surat, Gujarat (67th BPSC Re-exam)

  • Leaders: Vallabhbhai Patel, Chunnilal Mehta and the Mehta brothers.
  • Cause: peasant protest against a 30% tax hike imposed by the Bombay Presidency amid famine and flood; demands — an independent tribunal for fresh assessment, acceptance of the previous tax as full payment.
  • January 1926: authorities proposed the 30% revenue increase, provoking unrest. February 1926: Vallabhbhai Patel took charge, earning the title "Sardar" from Bardoli's women.
  • Formation of the Bardoli Inquiry Committee, publication of the Bardoli Satyagraha Patrika, worker's camps, social boycotts of opponents; women's role was significant — bhajan mandalis, holy imagery, communication with Adivasis.
  • Political support: K.M. Munshi and Lalji Naranji resigned from the Bombay Legislative Council in solidarity.
  • Outcome: by June 1928, a settlement mediated by Chunnilal Mehta saw the government commit to a smaller tax increase of 6.03%.

All India Kisan Sabha (1936) — Lucknow Session of INC (60th–62nd BPSC)

  • Leader: Swami Sahajananda Saraswati.
  • Formed at the Lucknow Session (61st–62nd BPSC) to protect tenants' rights and advocate abolition of zamindari.
  • Manifesto called for abolition of zamindari and recognition of tenants' occupancy rights.
  • Impact: nationwide agrarian reforms after the 1937 elections — fixing interest rates, reducing rent increases, granting tenant status in UP, restoring bakhast lands in Bihar.

Bakasht Movement (1936), Bihar

  • Leader: Swami Sahajananda Saraswati.
  • Resistance against forced displacement of tenants from Bakasht lands (lands previously settled by peasants) and oppressive landlords.
  • 1938: the Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha passed a resolution at its annual conference at Waini protesting zamindars.
  • Impact: the Bihar Tenancy Act and Bakasht Land Tax were enacted; Sahajanand Saraswati published the newspaper "Lok Sangraha" to raise awareness and mobilise peasants (70th BPSC).
  • The Barhiyataal resistance in Munger had the specific purpose of demanding restoration of Bakasht land (39th BPSC 1994) — not abolition of zamindari custom, protecting Muslim peasants, or class struggle per se.

Tebhaga Movement (1946–47), Bengal

  • Participants: poor peasants, tenants, and bargadars/share-croppers (66th BPSC Re-Exam); prominent leaders Kampram Singh and Bhuvan Singh.
  • Demand: a two-thirds share of the harvest for sharecroppers, one-third for landlords, based on the Flood Commission report; started under the leadership of the Bengal Provincial Kisan Sabha (66th BPSC Re-Exam 2020).
  • Slogan: "Nij khamare dhan tolo" ("Take your produce to your own threshing floor").
  • Widespread participation, especially among Rajbanshis (a low-caste tribal group) and Muslims in Bengal.
  • Outcome: 40% of peasants gained the two-thirds share; faced repression and limited success in East Bengal.

Telangana Insurrection (1946–51), Hyderabad

  • Leadership: Communist Party of India.
  • Exploitation of peasants by Jagirdars and Deshmukhs under the Nizam's rule; oppressive forced labour (Vetti) and high taxes.
  • 1940: formation of Jagir Ryotu Sangham; 1946: guerrilla-style armed struggle emerged, supported by the CPI, attacking landlords and Razakars (the Nizam's stormtroopers).
  • Outcomes: abolition of Vetti, land distribution, and debt settlement.

Part IV · Working-Class Movement
From mill hands to the AITUC

The late 19th-century rise of modern industry created India's working class — coal, cotton, jute — whose struggle intertwined with the fight for independence.

9

Working-Class / Labour Movements

Factory Acts, trade unions, and the AITUC

Foundation of modern industries (1850–1870)

  • Railway Minute of 1853: machinery introduced, developing railways, coal, cotton, jute, tea industries.
  • First Cotton Mill: Bombay, 1854; first Jute Mill: Calcutta, 1855.
  • By 1901, 584,000 factory workers in India.

Early initiatives to improve working conditions

  • Sasipada Banerjee (1870): founded the Bharat Shramjeevi and a workingmen's club to advocate for labour rights.
  • Sorabjee Shapoorji Bengalee (1878): proposed a bill in the Bombay Legislative Council to improve working conditions.
  • Narayan Meghajee Lokhande (1880) — full name Narayanji Meghaji Lokhande (1848–1897) — founded the Deenbandhu newspaper and the Bombay Mill and Millhands Association; considered the first leader to organise the labour movement in India (M.P. PCS 2017).
  • Great Indian Peninsular Railways Strike (1899): workers struck, receiving strong support from nationalist newspapers — Tilak's Kesari and Maharatta.
Major Factory Acts under British rule
Act Provisions
Factory Act of 1881 Max working hours 11 hrs/day for children; prohibited employment under age 7, minimum age raised to 12; dangerous machinery required fencing; 13-hour rest period between shifts for women/children; inspections and employment records mandatory.
Factory Act of 1891 Children's hours cut to 7 hrs/day, minimum working age 9, maximum 14; women/children workday capped at 11 hrs; overtime compensation mandated; night work for children banned; safety standards & inspection system established.
Factory Acts for the Jute Industry (1909 & 1911) Regulated working hours and improved safety standards specifically in jute mills.

Emergence of political consciousness

  • 1908 Political Strike, Bombay: workers organised a nearly week-long political strike in response to the conviction and imprisonment of Lokamanya Tilak; attracted attention from Lenin, who recognised the Indian proletariat's class-consciousness and potential for mass political struggle.
  • During the Swadeshi Movement: leaders Ashwini Coomar Banerjea, Prabhat Kumar Roy Chaudhuri, Premtosh Bose, Apurba Kumar Ghosh organised strikes in government printing presses, railways and jute — linked to broader nationalist objectives; early trade unions formed with limited initial success.
  • Subramaniya Siva and Chidambaram Pillai led significant strikes in Tuticorin and Tirunelveli; both were arrested for their activism.

Building the trade-union movement

  • Chennai Labour Union (1918): established by B.P. Wadia — first Indian trade union operating on modern lines.
  • All-India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) founded 31 October 1920, to organise growing worker discontent; Lala Lajpat Rai (President of INC) became its first president, emphasising the link between capitalism and imperialism; Dewan Chaman Lal was the first general secretary.
  • Influential leaders across AITUC sessions: C.R. Das, V.V. Giri, Sarojini Naidu, J.L. Nehru, Subhas Chandra Bose.
  • Early orientation: initially influenced by the British Labour Party's social democratic ideals — N.M. Joshi advocated economic improvement over political activity. Over time, Gandhian philosophy (non-violence, trusteeship, class collaboration) shaped the movement toward fewer strikes and more cooperative efforts.
  • Gandhi organised the Ahmedabad Textile Labour Association (1918), securing a 27.5% wage increase (later raised to 35%).

Key developments & legislative history

  • Gaya Congress Session (1922): Congress welcomed formation of AITUC, forming a support committee.
  • C.R. Das argued Congress should include workers and peasants in the freedom struggle to prevent their alienation; C.R. Das authored "India for Indians" (65th BPSC).
  • Trade Union Act of 1926: legalised trade unions with recognition/regulation; required minimum 7 members for registration; granted immunity from civil & criminal liability for legitimate actions; allowed unions to own property — but restricted funds from political purposes, limiting activity to economic grievances.
  • Late 1920s — communist influence: the Girni Kamgar Union led a six-month strike at the Bombay Textile Mills (1928); key communist figures Sohan Singh Joshi, P.C. Joshi, Muzaffar Ahmed, S.A. Dange; the 4th Congress of the Communist International urged AITUC to move beyond wage demands to overthrowing capitalism/imperialism; AITUC affiliated with the Pan-Pacific Secretariat and the Third International in Moscow.
  • Moderate response: N.M. Joshi formed the All India Trade Union Federation (AITUF), 1931, withdrawing from AITUC.
  • Government legislative restrictions: Trade Disputes Act (TDA), 1929 — compulsory Courts of Inquiry & Consultation Boards; illegal strikes in public utilities unless one-month notice given; prohibited coercive/political and sympathetic strikes. Public Safety Ordinance (1929) — introduced 1928, enacted 1929, provided for tribunals and a strike ban.
  • 1935: communists rejoined AITUC; the left front now included communists, Congress socialists and leftist nationalists like Nehru and Bose; AITUC supported Congress candidates in the 1937 elections.

Trade unions under Congress ministries (1937–1947)

  • 296 unions by 1938.
  • Kanpur Workers' Strike (1937): 10,000 workers, a 55-day strike.
  • Labour Enquiry Committee chaired by Babu Rajendra Prasad.
  • Legislative reforms: Bombay Industrial Disputes Act (1938), Bombay Shop Assistants Act (1939), C.P. Maternity Act (1939); Congress-led provincial governments were generally sympathetic to workers' demands.
The Bombay Plan (1944) — 70th BPSC
What
A blueprint for India's economic development proposed by prominent Indian industrialists, presenting a vision for post-independence progress focused on industrialisation, infrastructure and active state involvement in economic planning.
Main contributors
J.R.D. Tata, G.D. Birla, Lala Sri Ram
Other contributors

Second World War & post-Independence

  • Workers initially opposed the war; communist support shifted after Russia's involvement.
  • Communists did not support the Quit India Movement, advocating a policy of industrial peace instead.
  • Workers participated in post-war national upsurges: in 1945, dock workers of Bombay and Calcutta refused to load ships carrying supplies to warring troops in Indonesia; they went on strike in support of the Naval Ratings in 1946.

1909 & 1911 Factory Acts for the Jute Industry Hours & safety in jute mills.
1910 Bastar Revolt (Jagdalpur) Against feudal exploitation & forest levies.
1913 Mangarh Massacre Associated with Govind Guru's Bhil movement.
1914–1920 Tana Bhagat Movement Jatra Bhagat; satyagraha methods pre-dating Gandhi's Non-Cooperation.
1917 Champaran Satyagraha Ended the Teen Kathia system.
1917–1919 Kukis Revolt (Manipur) Opposed WWI labour recruitment.
1918 Kheda Satyagraha Gandhi, Patel, Mohanlal Pandya.
1918 United Provinces Kisan Sabha established 450 branches by June 1919.
1918 Ahmedabad Textile Labour Association Gandhi secures 27.5% (later 35%) wage rise.
1918 Chennai Labour Union founded B.P. Wadia — first modern Indian trade union.
1920 AITUC founded (31 Oct) Lala Lajpat Rai first president, Dewan Chaman Lal first general secretary.
1920 Awadh Kisan Sabha Refuse-labour & panchayat-dispute-resolution campaign.
1920s Zeliangsong Movement (Manipur) Reaction to British failure during Kuki revolt.
1921 Eka Movement (Awadh, UP) Madari Pasi, Baba Ramchandra; collapsed March 1922.
1921 Mappila/Moplah Uprising (Malabar) Turned communal after Ali Musaliar's arrest.
1921–1922 Chenchu Tribal Movement Venkattappaya, K. Hanumanthu; forest satyagraha.
1922 Gaya Congress Session Congress welcomed AITUC formation.
1922–1927 Peasant/labour organisations founded Labour Swaraj Party, Congress Labour Party, Kirti Kisan Party, Labour Kisan Party of Hindustan.
1922–1924 Rampa/Manyam Rebellion Alluri Sitarama Raju captured & executed 1924.
1926 Trade Union Act Legalised unions; barred political use of funds.
1926 (Jan–Feb) Bardoli tax hike proposed / Patel takes charge Patel earns the title "Sardar."
1928 Girni Kamgar Union strike, Bombay Textile Mills Six-month strike; communist-influenced militancy.
1928 Bardoli Satyagraha settled (June) Tax increase reduced to 6.03%.
1929 Trade Disputes Act & Public Safety Ordinance Restricted political/sympathetic strikes.
1930s Rani Gaidinliu's Naga (Heraka) Movement Jadonang executed 1931; Gaidinliu captured 1932.
1931 AITUF formed N.M. Joshi withdraws from AITUC.
1933 India Peasants' Institute founded (N.G. Ranga) Andhra Pradesh peasant mobilisation.
1935 Communists rejoin AITUC Left-front consolidation ahead of 1937 elections.
1935 Bihar Provincial Kisan Conference Adopted anti-zamindari slogans.
1936 All India Kisan Sabha formed (Lucknow) Swami Sahajananda Saraswati.
1936 Bakasht Movement (Bihar) Bihar Tenancy Act & Bakasht Land Tax followed.
1937 Kanpur Workers' Strike 10,000 workers, 55-day strike.
1937–1947 Trade unions under Congress ministries 296 unions by 1938; favourable labour legislation.
1938 Malabar Tenancy Act amendment push (Kerala) Congress Socialist Party, Karshak Sanghams.
1938 Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha conference, Waini Protest against zamindars over Bakasht lands.
1939 (Aug) Bihar Kisan Sabha loses momentum Precursor to future agrarian struggles.
1940s Gond Uprising United followers of Gond Dharma against British rule.
1940 Jagir Ryotu Sangham formed Foundation of Telangana peasant organisation.
1943 Bengal famine relief work by Kisan Sabha Continued despite the AIKS/pro-war split.
1944 Bombay Plan proposed Tata, Birla, Lala Sri Ram, and other industrialists.
1945 Bombay & Calcutta dock workers' refusal Refused to load supply ships for Indonesia-bound troops.
1946 Strikes in support of the Naval Ratings Post-war working-class upsurge.
1946–47 Tebhaga Movement (Bengal) Slogan "Nij khamare dhan tolo"; 40% of peasants gained the two-thirds share.
1946–51 Telangana Insurrection CPI-led guerrilla struggle vs. Jagirdars/Deshmukhs/Razakars.
