Governors-General & Viceroys of British India

British Policy Framework Towards Indian Princely States

1. Struggle for equality with Indian provinces — 1740–1765

2. Ring Fence policy (a.k.a. 'Security Cell') — 1765–1813

3. Subordinate Isolation policy — 1813–1857

4. Subordinate Union policy — 1858–1935

5. Equal Federation policy — 1935–1947

Quick Chronology Anchor (frequently asked sequence)

Battle of Buxar → 22/23 Oct 1764

Clive's re-arrival in India → 3 May 1765

Treaty of Allahabad → August 1765

Warren Hastings becomes Governor of Bengal → 1772

1. Robert Clive1757–60, 1765–67
  • Widely regarded as the Founder of British Empire in India; joined East India Company as a clerk in Madras, 1744 — reaffirmed in the 66th BPSC Re-Exam.
  • Defeated Nawab of Bengal at Battle of Plassey, 1757 — foundation of British political power in India.
  • Battle of Buxar, 1764 — fought during the gap between Clive's two terms; Clive returned 3 May 1765 to negotiate the resulting Treaty of Allahabad, August 1765.
  • First British Governor of Bengal Presidency.
  • Civilian reforms: banned gifts to officials and private trade by Company servants.
  • Military reforms: ended double batta (double allowance); from 1766 only soldiers serving outside Bengal–Bihar received it.
  • Introduced the Dual Government System (Diwani–Nizamat split) in Bengal, 1765.
⚠ TRAP: Clive was NOT the first Governor-General — that title (of Bengal) belongs to Warren Hastings under the Regulating Act, 1773. Also, Buxar (1764) is often loosely clubbed with Clive's "achievements," but it was fought while he was in England.
2. Warren HastingsGov. of Bengal 1772; Gov-Gen of Bengal 1774–85
  • Wars: Rohilla War (1774); First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782), ended by the Treaty of Salbai, 1782; Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780–1784).
  • Ended the Dual Government System, 1772, on orders of the Court of Directors; revenue management taken over by the President & Council; Board of Revenue established; treasury shifted from Murshidabad to Calcutta; Nawab's allowance cut; payments to Emperor Shah Alam stopped.
  • Made first Governor-General of Bengal by the Regulating Act, 1773; headed a 5-member Executive Council. Act of 1781 further clarified jurisdictional conflicts between the Supreme Court and Company courts. Pitt's India Act, 1784 (passed in London) introduced dual control via a Board of Control.
  • Asiatic Society of Bengal founded 15 January 1784 (by William Jones); also established the Calcutta Madrasa.
  • Revenue reforms: tried a five-year land revenue settlement, replaced corrupt Collectors, then reverted to annual settlements favouring zamindars — aimed at centralisation but led to poor collection.
  • Judicial reforms: established Diwani & Faujdari Adalats and a Supreme Court; jurisdiction conflicts persisted; codified Hindu and Muslim personal laws.
  • Commercial reforms: reduced custom houses, standardised duties, regulated dastaks, protected weavers, and fostered trade with Bhutan & Tibet.
  • Banaras Rebellion, 1781, led by Raja Chet Singh; Hastings fled and took refuge in Chunar Fort.
  • Faced impeachment proceedings 1788–1795; charges dropped in light of his service to the empire.
⚠ TRAP: He was Governor-General of Bengal only, never "Governor-General of India" — that office was created later, in 1833, for William Bentinck.
3. Lord Cornwallis1786–1793; 30 Jul–5 Oct 1805
  • Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790–92) → Treaty of Seringapatam, 1792 — Tipu Sultan ceded ~half his territory.
  • Cornwallis Code, 1793 — judicial reform based on Separation of Powers; separated revenue from judicial administration.
  • Permanent Settlement of Bengal, 1793 (Istamrari Bandobast).
  • Europeanised the administrative machinery; emphasised capability and integrity; abolished low wages and excessive perks in favour of merit-based appointments; separated commercial, judicial, and revenue services.
  • Reduced revenue districts from 35 to 23; Collectors gained magisterial powers; abolished Indian-judge Faujdari Courts and created 4 Circuit Courts (3 in Bengal, 1 in Bihar/Patna), headed by Europeans and assisted by Indian Qazis/Muftis.
  • Police reforms: created the Daroga system, 1793 — removed police powers from zamindars, empowered District Judges.
  • Founded the Covenanted Civil Service (Indian Civil Service).
  • Died at Ghazipur (U.P.), 5 October 1805; his grave is there.
⚠ TRAP: Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–18) is wrongly paired with Cornwallis in some MCQs — it actually belongs to Lord Hastings.
4. Sir John Shore1793–1798
  • Battle of Kharda, 1795 — fought between the Nizam of Hyderabad and the Maratha Confederacy; Shore followed a policy of non-intervention, watching the battle without engaging.
  • Charter Act of 1793 — renewed the East India Company's charter and regulated its administration.
⚠ TRAP: The Policy of Non-Intervention is associated with Shore, the opposite of Wellesley's later forward, Subsidiary-Alliance approach — the two are sometimes mixed up.
5. Lord Wellesley1798–1805
  • Refined (did not invent) the Subsidiary Alliance system; Dupleix was the first European to lend an army to an Indian ruler for fixed payment.
  • First such arrangement: 1765 treaty with Awadh (boundary protection for payment).
  • Order of states accepting Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance: Nizam of Hyderabad (1798 & 1800 — FIRST) → Mysore (1799) → Tanjore (Oct 1799) → Awadh (Nov 1801) → Peshwa Bajirao II (Dec 1802) → Bhonsle of Berar (Dec 1803) → Sindhia (Feb 1804).
  • Treaty of Bassein, 31 December 1802, with Peshwa Bajirao II — first Maratha chief to accept; Company gave 6,000 soldiers, Peshwa paid 26 lakh/year.
  • Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, 1799 — Tipu Sultan killed.
  • Second Anglo-Maratha War, 1803–05.
  • Formed the Madras Presidency; established Fort William College, Calcutta, to train British civilians for administration in India.
⚠ TRAP: UPSC 2018: securing a fixed income for the Company was NOT a stated aim of the Subsidiary Alliance — its real aim was British paramountcy. Also, 'Subsidiary Alliance — 1863' is a deliberately wrong date pairing seen in MCQs.
6. Sir George Barlow1805–1807
  • Vellore Mutiny, 1806 — sepoy protest against interference in religious/social practices; rebels raised the flag of the ruler of Mysore.
⚠ TRAP: Barlow's short tenure comes after Wellesley (1798–1805) and before Minto I (1807–13) — a frequently scrambled chronology point.
7. Lord Minto I1807–1813
  • Treaty of Amritsar, 1809, with Ranjit Singh — fixed the River Sutlej as the boundary between British territory and the Sikh kingdom; first formal British–Sikh agreement.
⚠ TRAP: Do not confuse Lord Minto I (1807–13, Treaty of Amritsar) with Lord Minto II (1905–10, Morley-Minto Reforms) — a common name-clash trap.
8. Lord Hastings (Marquess of Hastings)1813–1823
  • Anglo-Nepal War, 1814–16; ended with Treaty of Sugauli/Sagauli, signed 2 Dec 1815, ratified 4 Mar 1816 — Nepal ceded Kumaon, Garhwal, Shimla districts.
  • Third Anglo-Maratha War, 1817–19 — abolished the title of Peshwa; merged Peshwa territory into Bombay Presidency; forced settlements on the Peshwa and Scindia via a Treaty with Sindhia, 1817.
  • 1817–18 Subordinate alliances with Rajputana princely states negotiated by Charles Metcalfe.
  • Adopted an interventionist policy (reversal of the earlier Ring-Fence/non-intervention stance); introduced the Ryotwari Settlement in Madras, 1820 — noted in the 67th BPSC.
9. Lord Amherst1823–1828
  • First Anglo-Burmese War, 1824–1826 — first major British war against Burma.
  • Capture of Bharatpur, 1826 — reasserted British "invincibility" after an earlier failed siege in 1805.
10. Lord William Bentinck1828–1835 (1st Gov-Gen of India from 1833)
  • Became first Governor-General of India after the Charter Act, 1833 merged the office — noted in the 66th BPSC Re-Exam.
  • Abolished Sati — Bengal Sati Regulation, 1829; extended to Madras & Bombay, 1830. Made female infanticide illegal and punishable.
  • Suppressed Thuggee via Captain Sleeman — 1,562 thugs arrested 1826–35; organised thuggee ended by 1837.
  • Annexed Mysore (1831), Coorg/Kurg (1834), Cachar (1832), Jaintia (1835) due to extreme mismanagement; signed a Treaty with Ranjit Singh, 1831.
  • Did NOT interfere in Jaipur (despite an attack on the British Resident), or in Jodhpur, Bundi, Kota, Bhopal.
  • Regarded as the "Father of Modern Western Education in India"; formed the Committee of Public Instruction; endorsed Macaulay's English-medium recommendation; established Calcutta Medical College, 1835.
  • Charter Act, 1833 attempted total administrative centralisation and paved the way to end slavery by law; allowed Indians to hold public office.
  • Financial reforms: reduced military salaries, regularised the opium trade, achieved a Rs. 1.5 million budget surplus.
  • Administrative: abolished several Cornwallis-era provisions; introduced local languages in lower courts, English in higher courts.
  • Macaulay's Minute on Education approved 7 March 1835.
⚠ TRAP: The 'Hill Assembly Plan' for tribal development is wrongly attributed to Bentinck in some options — it was actually by Cleveland. Also, removal of disqualification due to change of religion was 1850 (Dalhousie era), not under Bentinck.
11. Charles Metcalfe1835–1836 (acting Governor-General)
  • New press law, 1835, removing restrictions on the press in India — known as the "Liberator of the Indian Press".
  • Earlier (under Lord Hastings) negotiated the 1817–18 subordinate alliances with Rajputana states.
12. Lord Auckland1836–1842
  • First Anglo-Afghan War, 1838–1842.
  • Death of Ranjit Singh, 1839 — Sikh kingdom weakened, later leading to the Anglo-Sikh Wars.
13. Lord Ellenborough1842–1844
  • Sindh fully annexed into the British Empire, 1843, by Charles Napier.
  • War with Gwalior, 1843.
  • Slavery abolished by Act V of 1843.
  • Started the Masterly Inactivity policy toward Afghanistan, continued till Lord Northbrook.
⚠ TRAP: Ellenborough annexed Sindh, NOT Awadh — Awadh was annexed later by Dalhousie (1856). A frequently swapped pair.
14. Lord Hardinge I1844–1848
  • First Anglo-Sikh War, 1845–1846 → Treaty of Lahore, 1846.
  • Carried out social reforms against female infanticide and human sacrifice.
⚠ TRAP: Tenure sits between Ellenborough and Dalhousie — frequently tested purely as a chronological-order question.
15. Lord Dalhousie1848–1856 (real name James Andrew Broun-Ramsay)
  • Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–49) → annexation of Punjab, 1849; annexation of Lower Burma, 1852.
  • Doctrine of Lapse first applied to Satara, 1848 — its first victim. Also applied to Jaitpur & Sambalpur (1849), Baghat (1850), Udaipur (1852), Jhansi (1854), Nagpur (1854).
  • Awadh annexed February 1856 — on grounds of alleged maladministration of Nawab Wajid Ali Shah, based on a report by Resident James Outram (appointed 1854, replacing Sleeman).
  • First railway line: Bombay–Thane, 1853, run by the Great Indian Peninsula Railway Company; Ganges Canal opened, 1854.
  • Administrative: appointed a Lieutenant Governor for Bengal, centralising administration; designated commissioners for newly acquired territories.
  • Military: established Simla as army headquarters; integrated Sikh and Gurkha forces into the army.
  • Educational: Wood's Despatch, 1854 — blueprint for state education, including universities at Calcutta, Madras, and Bombay; Thomsonian system for vernacular education adopted, 1853.
  • Established the Public Works Department, 1854; restored the Grand Trunk Road; enacted the Post Office Act, 1854.
  • Annexed part of Sikkim (incl. Darjeeling) on the pretext of mistreatment of two English doctors.
⚠ TRAP: Awadh annexation is NOT Doctrine of Lapse — it was annexed on the separate ground of maladministration. A separate note also credits the Hindu Widow Remarriage Act, 1856 to Dalhousie's tenure since it was signed right at the transition — but the standard, more commonly examined attribution is to Canning (with Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar). Treat this as a contested attribution across sources.
16. Lord Canning1856–1862 (1st Viceroy from 1858)
  • First Viceroy of India under the Government of India Act, 1858.
  • Hindu Widow Remarriage Act, 1856 — due to Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar; legalised remarriage and legitimised children from it (Section 15).
  • Managed the Revolt of 1857; also faced the White Mutiny by European troops, 1859.
  • Passed the War Rearmament Act, 1856; enacted the Act of 1858, dissolving the East India Company; Indian Councils Act, 1861; introduced income tax for the first time in India.
  • Universities established at Calcutta, Madras, and Bombay, 1857 (building on Dalhousie's Wood's Despatch blueprint).
  • Read out the Queen's Proclamation at a Durbar in Allahabad, 1 November 1858 — ended Company rule, transferred India to the Crown, restored rulers' right to adopt heirs.
17. Lord Elgin I1862–1863
  • Wahabi Movement — a series of revolts against British rule — continued during his short tenure.
18. John Lawrence1864–1869
  • Most strongly associated with Masterly Inactivity toward Afghanistan — had the fullest opportunity to apply it; the term is traced to an article by J.W.S. Wylie in the Edinburgh Review.
  • Bhutan War, 1865.
  • Set up High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras, 1865; improved telegraphic communication with Europe; expanded canals and railways.
19. Lord Mayo1869–1872
  • Opening of Rajkot College; established the Department of Agriculture and Commerce; introduced state railways.
  • Financial Decentralisation / Mayo's Resolution, 1870 — first split of Central & Provincial finances; founded Mayo College; organised the Statistical Survey of India — noted in the 67th BPSC Re-exam.
  • First Census in India, 1872 (regular decennial census began 1881 under Ripon).
  • Assassinated in the Andaman & Nicobar Islands by convict Sher Ali Afridi — the only Viceroy murdered in office.
20. Lord Northbrook1872–1876
  • Visit of the Prince of Wales, 1875.
  • Trial of the Gaekwar of Baroda.
  • Kuka Movement — a Namdhari Sikh reform-and-rebellion movement.
21. Lord Lytton1876–1880
  • Followed an aggressive Forward Policy toward Afghanistan (opposite of Masterly Inactivity); appointed by PM Disraeli; led to the Second Anglo-Afghan War, 1878–1880.
  • Royal Titles Act, 1876 — Queen Victoria titled Empress of India.
  • Famine of 1876–78 (Great Famine) — Lytton controversially held a Grand Durbar in Delhi during the famine.
  • Vernacular Press Act, 1878 ('Gagging/Silencing Act') — S.N. Banerjee called it 'lightning from the sky'.
  • Arms Act, 1878 — discriminatory; Europeans/Anglo-Indians exempt from licence requirement.
  • Abolished duties on British goods; the titles of "Rai Bahadur" and "Khan Bahadur" began to be conferred on Indians during his tenure — noted in the 60th–62nd BPSC.
⚠ TRAP: The Ilbert Bill is NOT linked to Lytton — that was Lord Ripon, 1883.
22. Lord Ripon1880–1884
  • Regarded the most pro-Indian / liberal Viceroy; called the 'Father of Local Self-Government'.
  • Local Self-Government Resolution, 18 May 1882 — created rural local boards and urban municipalities.
  • Repealed the Vernacular Press Act, 1882 (originally imposed by Lytton, 1878).
  • First Factory Act, 1881 — banned employment of children below 7 years; capped 7–12 age group to 9 hrs/day; 4 holidays/month.
  • Ilbert Bill debate, 2 February 1883 (introduced by Sir C.P. Ilbert) — aimed to let Indian judges try European offenders; faced fierce British opposition and was reintroduced in modified form, 1884 — noted in the 69th BPSC.
  • Education: Hunter Commission, 1882.
  • Land & Tenancy: Rent Commission amid peasant unrest, leading to the Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885.
  • First regular decennial Census conducted, 1881; continued financial decentralisation with revenue categorisation and greater provincial control.
23. Lord Dufferin1884–1888
  • Indian National Congress founded 1885 by A.O. Hume during his tenure.
  • Third Anglo-Burmese War, 1885–1886 — oversaw the annexation of Burma.
24. Lord Lansdowne1888–1894
  • Second Factory Act, 1891.
  • Indian Councils Act, 1892.
  • Formation of the Durand Commission, 1893 — demarcated the Durand Line boundary with Afghanistan.
25. Lord Elgin II1894–1899
  • Famine, 1896–1897, and Plague, 1897.
  • Assassination of Rand by the Chapekar brothers — a reaction to harsh plague-control measures in Pune.
26. Lord Curzon1899–1905
  • Traditionally cited as the Longest-serving Viceroy of India — 7 years (1899–1905).
  • ASI integrated & centralised, 1901; John Marshall appointed Director-General 1901, took charge 1902; Ancient Monuments Preservation Act, 1904 (£50,000 allocated for protection).
  • Imperial Cadet Corps established, November 1901, to impart military training to Indian subjects — noted in the 63rd BPSC.
  • Calcutta Corporation/Municipal Act, 1899 ('Mackenzie Act') — reduced elected Indian members.
  • Agriculture: Punjab Land Alienation Act, 1900 (63rd BPSC) — limited land transfer to money-lenders; Co-operative Credit Societies Act, 1904; Scott-Moncrieff Commission, 1901 (₹4.5 crore for irrigation).
  • Economic: British currency made legal tender (₹15 = £1); salt tax reduced; Commerce & Industry Department created.
  • Judicial: expanded the Calcutta High Court with more judges and higher salaries.
  • Army: reorganised into Northern/Southern Commands; introduced the Kitchener Test for assessment; the resulting Curzon–Kitchener clash over military authority led to Curzon's resignation, 1905.
  • Railways: Railway Commission, 1901 — extended lines, established the Railway Board.
  • Famine response: MacDonnell Commission — employment for wages, tax relief, and preventive measures.
  • Police Commission, 1902; Universities Commission, 1902; Indian Universities Act, 1904 (government control over universities).
  • Younghusband Expedition, 1904, to Tibet.
  • Partition of Bengal, 1905 — created Eastern Bengal & Assam province; faced strong Hindu resistance; reversed 1911.
  • Gopal Krishna Gokhale compared his rule to Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb; Curzon famously said Congress was "tottering to its fall" (1900).
⚠ TRAP: Curzon did NOT introduce the Vernacular Press Act (that's Lytton) or the Ilbert Bill (that's Ripon). Also, the "Longest-serving Viceroy" label is contested: by exact tenure length, Lord Linlithgow (1936–43) served longer than Curzon — different sources round this differently.
27. Lord Minto II1905–1910
  • Replaced Curzon, November 1905; John Morley made Secretary of State for India.
  • Foundation of the Muslim League, 1906; Surat Split of Congress, 1907.
  • Morley-Minto Reforms, 1909 (Indian Councils Act 1909) — introduced separate electorates for Muslims.
  • Along with Curzon, associated with the Divide and Rule strategy (Partition of Bengal deepened Hindu-Muslim divide).
⚠ TRAP: Do not confuse with Lord Minto I (1807–13, Treaty of Amritsar).
28. Lord Hardinge II (Charles Hardinge)1910–1916
  • Capital of India shifted from Calcutta to Delhi, announced at the Third Delhi Durbar, 12 December 1911, by King George V — noted in the 70th BPSC.
  • Bomb attack on the Viceroy, 1912.
  • Komagata Maru incident, 1914.
29. Lord Chelmsford1916–1921
  • Lucknow Pact, 1916 — Congress–Muslim League unity pact.
  • Champaran Satyagraha, 1917 — Gandhi's first Satyagraha in India; Montagu Declaration, 1917, promising gradual self-governing institutions.
  • Kheda Satyagraha, 1918.
  • Rowlatt Act, 1919 → Jallianwala Bagh massacre, 1919.
  • Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms, 1919 (Government of India Act 1919) — introduced dyarchy in the provinces.
30. Lord Reading1921–1926
  • The only Jewish Viceroy of India (born Rufus Isaacs); earlier Solicitor-General, Attorney-General, Lord Chief Justice of England.
  • Moplah Rebellion, Kerala, 1921 — noted in the 63rd BPSC.
  • Chauri-Chaura incident, 1922, and formation of the Swaraj Party.
  • Kakori Train Robbery, 1925.
31. Lord Irwin1926–1931
  • Simon Commission, 1927.
  • Nehru Report, 1928.
  • Deepavali/Irwin Declaration, 1929 — promised eventual Dominion Status.
  • Civil Disobedience Movement launched, 1930; First Round Table Conference, 1930.
32. Lord Willingdon1931–1936
  • Second Round Table Conference, 1931, and Third Round Table Conference, 1932.
  • Communal Award, 1932, and Gandhi's protest fast, leading to the Poona Pact, 1932 (between Gandhi and Ambedkar over separate electorates for the Depressed Classes).
  • Formation of the Congress Socialist Party, 1934.
33. Lord Linlithgow1936–1943
  • Often described as the longest-serving Viceroy, holding office 1936–1943.
  • Declared India at war (Second World War, 1939) without consulting the Congress.
  • Lahore Resolution, 1940 — Muslim League's demand for a separate Muslim state.
  • August Offer, 1940; Cripps Mission, 1942; Quit India Movement, 1942.
⚠ TRAP: The "longest-serving Viceroy" title is contested between Linlithgow (1936–43) and Curzon (1899–1905, sometimes rounded to "7 years" in notes) — check which framing the specific source/question uses.
34. Lord Wavell1943–1947
  • End of the Second World War, 1945; INA Trials, 1945.
  • Simla Conference, 1945, and the resulting Wavell Plan.
  • Cabinet Mission, 1946.
  • Direct Action Day, 16 August 1946 — noted in the 70th BPSC Prelims.
35. Lord Mountbatten1947–1948
  • Last Viceroy of India; facilitated the Partition of India and transfer of power.
  • Independence of India, 15 August 1947.
