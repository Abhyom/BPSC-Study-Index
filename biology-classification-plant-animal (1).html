Biological Classification — The Five Kingdoms

Every topic of the chapter under its own heading — history, Whittaker's system, each kingdom in turn, then the acellular agents.

Yellow — Concept / DefinitionBlue — Fact / Number / ExampleGreen — Person / ScientistRed — MCQ Trap

01History of Classification

Early Attempts

  • Since the dawn of civilisation there have been many attempts to classify living organisms; at first it was done instinctively, not on scientific criteria, but out of a need to use organisms for food, shelter and clothing.
  • Aristotle was the earliest to attempt a more scientific basis for classification, using simple morphological characters.
  • Aristotle classified plants into trees, shrubs and herbs.
  • Aristotle divided animals into two groups: those with red blood and those without.

Two Kingdom System (Linnaeus)

  • In Linnaeus' time a Two Kingdom system was developed with kingdoms Plantae and Animalia, including all plants and all animals respectively.
  • It did not distinguish between: eukaryotes and prokaryotes; unicellular and multicellular organisms; photosynthetic (green algae) and non-photosynthetic (fungi) organisms.
  • Classifying into plants and animals was easy to do and understand, but a large number of organisms fell into neither category — so the two kingdom classification, used for a long time, was found inadequate.

Why Classification Kept Changing

  • Besides gross morphology, a need was felt to include: cell structure, nature of wall, mode of nutrition, habitat, methods of reproduction, evolutionary relationships.
  • Plant and animal kingdoms have been a constant under all systems, but what groups/organisms are included under them has kept changing.
  • The number and nature of the other kingdoms has been understood differently by different scientists over time.
  • Over time an attempt has been made to evolve a classification that is also phylogenetic — i.e., based on evolutionary relationships — not only on morphological, physiological and reproductive similarities.

02Whittaker's Five Kingdom Classification

The System

  • R.H. Whittaker proposed the Five Kingdom Classification in 1969.
  • The five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
  • Main criteria used: cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction and phylogenetic relationships.

Three-Domain System

  • The three-domain system has also been proposed: it divides Kingdom Monera into two domains, leaving the remaining eukaryotic kingdoms in the third domain.
  • This gives a six kingdom classification — to be learned in detail in higher classes.

03Why the Five Kingdom System Was Needed

Problems of the Earlier System

  • Earlier systems included bacteria, blue green algae, fungi, mosses, ferns, gymnosperms and angiosperms under 'Plants'.
  • The unifying character of this whole kingdom was that all organisms had a cell wall — which placed together groups that differed widely in other characteristics.
  • It brought the prokaryotic bacteria and blue green algae (cyanobacteria) together with eukaryotic groups.
  • It grouped unicellular and multicellular organisms together — e.g., Chlamydomonas and Spirogyra were both placed under algae.
  • It did not differentiate the heterotrophic fungi from autotrophic green plants, though they differ in wall composition: fungi have chitin, green plants have a cellulosic wall.

How the Five Kingdom System Fixed It

  • Fungi were placed in a separate Kingdom Fungi.
  • All prokaryotic organisms were grouped under Kingdom Monera.
  • Unicellular eukaryotic organisms were placed in Kingdom Protista.
  • Protista brought together Chlamydomonas, Chlorella (earlier in Algae within Plants; both have cell walls) with Paramoecium and Amoeba (earlier in the animal kingdom; lack cell wall).
  • This happened because the criteria for classification changed; such changes will take place in future too, depending on improved understanding of characteristics and evolutionary relationships.
Scope of the Chapter
  • This chapter covers Monera, Protista and Fungi of Whittaker's system; Kingdoms Plantae and Animalia are dealt with separately in Chapters 3 and 4.

04Kingdom Monera — Overview & Bacterial Shapes

General Features

  • Bacteria are the sole members of Kingdom Monera; they are the most abundant micro-organisms.
  • Occur almost everywhere — hundreds of bacteria in a handful of soil; also in extreme habitats: hot springs, deserts, snow and deep oceans. Many live in or on other organisms as parasites.
  • Though bacterial structure is very simple, they are very complex in behaviour; as a group they show the most extensive metabolic diversity.

Four Shapes of Bacteria

  • Coccus (pl. cocci) — spherical.
  • Bacillus (pl. bacilli) — rod-shaped.
  • Vibrium / Vibrio (pl. vibrio) — comma-shaped.
  • Spirillum (pl. spirilla) — spiral.

Nutrition in Bacteria

  • Some bacteria are autotrophic — synthesise their own food from inorganic substrates; they may be photosynthetic autotrophic or chemosynthetic autotrophic.
  • The vast majority are heterotrophs — depend on other organisms or dead organic matter for food.

05Archaebacteria

Archaebacteria

  • Live in some of the most harsh habitats.
  • Halophiles — extreme salty areas.
  • Thermoacidophiles — hot springs.
  • Methanogens — marshy areas.
  • They differ from other bacteria in having a different cell wall structure — this feature is responsible for their survival in extreme conditions.
  • Methanogens are present in the gut of several ruminant animals such as cows and buffaloes and produce methane (biogas) from the dung of these animals.

06Eubacteria

General Features

  • There are thousands of different eubacteria or 'true bacteria'.
  • Characterised by a rigid cell wall and, if motile, a flagellum.

Cyanobacteria (Blue-Green Algae)

  • Have chlorophyll a similar to green plants — photosynthetic autotrophs.
  • Unicellular, colonial or filamentous; freshwater/marine or terrestrial 'algae'; colonies generally surrounded by a gelatinous sheath.
  • Often form blooms in polluted water bodies.
  • Some fix atmospheric nitrogen in specialised cells called heterocysts — e.g., Nostoc and Anabaena.

Chemosynthetic Autotrophic Bacteria

  • Oxidise inorganic substances — nitrates, nitrites and ammonia — and use the released energy for their ATP production.
  • Play a great role in recycling nutrients: nitrogen, phosphorous, iron and sulphur.

Heterotrophic Bacteria

  • Most abundant in nature; the majority are important decomposers.
  • Useful: making curd from milk, production of antibiotics, fixing nitrogen in legume roots.
  • Harmful (pathogens): damage human beings, crops, farm animals and pets — cholera, typhoid, tetanus, citrus canker.

Reproduction in Bacteria

  • Mainly by fission.
  • Under unfavourable conditions they produce spores.
  • A sort of sexual reproduction by a primitive type of DNA transfer from one bacterium to another.

Mycoplasma

  • Completely lack a cell wall.
  • The smallest living cells known; can survive without oxygen.
  • Many are pathogenic in animals and plants.

07Kingdom Protista — Overview

General Features

  • All single-celled eukaryotes are placed under Protista, but the boundaries of this kingdom are not well defined — what is 'a photosynthetic protistan' to one biologist may be 'a plant' to another.
  • Groups included in this book: Chrysophytes, Dinoflagellates, Euglenoids, Slime moulds and Protozoans.
  • Members are primarily aquatic; the kingdom forms a link with plants, animals and fungi.
  • Being eukaryotes, the cell has a well-defined nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles; some have flagella or cilia.
  • Reproduce asexually and sexually (by a process involving cell fusion and zygote formation).

08Chrysophytes

Diatoms & Golden Algae

  • This group includes diatoms and golden algae (desmids).
  • Found in fresh water and marine environments; microscopic and float passively in water currents — plankton. Most are photosynthetic.
  • In diatoms the cell walls form two thin overlapping shells, fitting together 'as in a soap box'.
  • Walls are embedded with silica — hence indestructible.
  • Accumulated cell wall deposits over billions of years form diatomaceous earth; being gritty it is used in polishing, filtration of oils and syrups.
  • Diatoms are the chief 'producers' in the oceans.

09Dinoflagellates

Dinoflagellates

  • Mostly marine and photosynthetic.
  • Appear yellow, green, brown, blue or red depending on the main pigments present.
  • Cell wall has stiff cellulose plates on the outer surface.
  • Most have two flagella — one lies longitudinally, the other transversely in a furrow between the wall plates.
  • Red dinoflagellates (e.g., Gonyaulax) multiply so rapidly that they make the sea appear red — red tides.
  • Toxins released by such large numbers may even kill other marine animals such as fishes.

10Euglenoids

Euglenoids

  • Majority are fresh water organisms found in stagnant water.
  • Instead of a cell wall they have a protein-rich layer called pellicle, which makes the body flexible.
  • Have two flagella — one short and one long.
  • Photosynthetic in sunlight; when deprived of sunlight they behave like heterotrophs by predating on smaller organisms.
  • Pigments are identical to those present in higher plants.
  • Example: Euglena.

11Slime Moulds

Slime Moulds

  • Saprophytic protists; the body moves along decaying twigs and leaves, engulfing organic material.
  • Under suitable conditions they form an aggregation called plasmodium which may grow and spread over several feet.
  • In unfavourable conditions the plasmodium differentiates and forms fruiting bodies bearing spores at their tips.
  • Spores possess true walls; extremely resistant and survive for many years even under adverse conditions; dispersed by air currents.

12Protozoans

General Features

  • All protozoans are heterotrophs and live as predators or parasites.
  • Believed to be primitive relatives of animals.
  • There are four major groups of protozoans.

13Kingdom Fungi — General Features

Diversity & Habitat

  • Fungi constitute a unique kingdom of heterotrophic organisms with great diversity in morphology and habitat.
  • Seen on moist bread and rotten fruits; the common mushroom and toadstools are fungi; white spots on mustard leaves are due to a parasitic fungus.
  • Unicellular fungi, e.g., yeast (Saccharomyces), are used to make bread and beer.
  • Cause diseases in plants and animals — wheat rust-causing Puccinia; some are the source of antibiotics — Penicillium.
  • Cosmopolitan — occur in air, water, soil and on animals and plants; prefer warm and humid places.
  • Food is kept in the refrigerator to prevent spoilage due to bacterial or fungal infections.

Body Structure

  • Except yeasts (unicellular), fungi are filamentous.
  • Body consists of long, slender thread-like structures called hyphae; the network of hyphae is the mycelium.
  • Some hyphae are continuous tubes filled with multinucleated cytoplasm — coenocytic hyphae; others have septae (cross walls).
  • Cell walls are composed of chitin and polysaccharides.

Modes of Nutrition

  • Saprophytes — most fungi; absorb soluble organic matter from dead substrates.
  • Parasites — depend on living plants and animals.
  • Symbionts — live in association with algae as lichens and with roots of higher plants as mycorrhiza.

14Reproduction in Fungi

Modes of Reproduction

  • Vegetative: fragmentation, fission and budding.
  • Asexual: spores called conidia, sporangiospores or zoospores.
  • Sexual: oospores, ascospores and basidiospores.
  • The various spores are produced in distinct structures called fruiting bodies.

The Three Steps of the Sexual Cycle

  • (i) Plasmogamy — fusion of protoplasms between two motile or non-motile gametes.
  • (ii) Karyogamy — fusion of two nuclei.
  • (iii) Meiosis in zygote — resulting in haploid spores.

Dikaryon Stage

  • In some fungi, fusion of two haploid cells immediately results in diploid cells (2n).
  • In ascomycetes and basidiomycetes an intervening dikaryotic stage (n + n — two nuclei per cell) occurs; this is a dikaryon and the phase is the dikaryophase.
  • Later the parental nuclei fuse and the cells become diploid; fruiting bodies form in which reduction division occurs, giving haploid spores.
Basis of Division into Classes
  • The morphology of the mycelium, mode of spore formation and fruiting bodies form the basis for dividing Kingdom Fungi into classes.
  • Four classes: Phycomycetes, Ascomycetes, Basidiomycetes, Deuteromycetes.

15Phycomycetes

Phycomycetes

  • Found in aquatic habitats, on decaying wood in moist and damp places, or as obligate parasites on plants.
  • Mycelium is aseptate and coenocytic.
  • Asexual: zoospores (motile) or aplanospores (non-motile), produced endogenously in sporangium.
  • Sexual: a zygospore is formed by fusion of two gametes — gametes similar in morphology (isogamous) or dissimilar (anisogamous or oogamous).
  • Examples: Mucor, Rhizopus (the bread mould) and Albugo (the parasitic fungus on mustard).

16Ascomycetes

Ascomycetes (Sac-fungi)

  • Commonly known as sac-fungi.
  • Mostly multicellular, e.g., Penicillium; rarely unicellular, e.g., yeast (Saccharomyces).
  • Saprophytic, decomposers, parasitic or coprophilous (growing on dung).
  • Mycelium is branched and septate.
  • Asexual spores: conidia, produced exogenously on special mycelium called conidiophores; conidia on germination produce mycelium.
  • Sexual spores: ascospores, produced endogenously in sac-like asci (sing. ascus); asci are arranged in fruiting bodies called ascocarps.
  • Examples: Aspergillus, Claviceps and Neurospora; Neurospora is used extensively in biochemical and genetic work.
  • Members like morels and truffles are edible and considered delicacies.

17Basidiomycetes

Basidiomycetes

  • Commonly known forms: mushrooms, bracket fungi, puffballs.
  • Grow in soil, on logs and tree stumps, and in living plant bodies as parasites, e.g., rusts and smuts.
  • Mycelium is branched and septate.
  • Asexual spores are generally not found, but vegetative reproduction by fragmentation is common.
  • Sex organs are absent; plasmogamy is brought about by fusion of two vegetative or somatic cells of different strains or genotypes.
  • The resultant structure is dikaryotic and ultimately gives rise to the basidium (pl. basidia).
  • Karyogamy and meiosis take place in the basidium, producing four basidiospores, which are produced exogenously on the basidium.
  • Basidia are arranged in fruiting bodies called basidiocarps.
  • Examples: Agaricus (mushroom), Ustilago (smut), Puccinia (rust fungus).

18Deuteromycetes

Deuteromycetes (Imperfect Fungi)

  • Known as imperfect fungi because only the asexual or vegetative phases are known.
  • When the sexual forms of these fungi were discovered they were moved to the classes they rightly belong to — often to ascomycetes and basidiomycetes.
  • Also possible that the asexual/vegetative stage was given one name (under deuteromycetes) and the sexual stage another (under another class); once linkages were established the fungus was identified correctly and moved out of deuteromycetes.
  • Reproduce only by asexual spores known as conidia.
  • Mycelium is septate and branched.
  • Some are saprophytes or parasites; a large number are decomposers of litter and help in mineral cycling.
  • Examples: Alternaria, Colletotrichum and Trichoderma.

20Kingdom Plantae — A Brief Introduction

Kingdom Plantae

  • Includes all eukaryotic chlorophyll-containing organisms commonly called plants.
  • A few are partially heterotrophic: insectivorous plants — Bladderwort and Venus fly trap; parasite — Cuscuta.
  • Plant cells have eukaryotic structure with prominent chloroplasts and cell wall mainly of cellulose.
  • Includes algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms.
  • Life cycle has two distinct phases — diploid sporophytic and haploid gametophytic — that alternate: alternation of generations. Lengths of the phases, and whether they are free-living or dependent, vary among groups. (Detail in the Plant Kingdom tab.)

21Kingdom Animalia — A Brief Introduction

Kingdom Animalia

  • Heterotrophic eukaryotic, multicellular organisms whose cells lack cell walls.
  • Directly or indirectly depend on plants for food; digest food in an internal cavity; store reserves as glycogen or fat.
  • Mode of nutrition is holozoic — by ingestion of food.
  • Follow a definite growth pattern and grow into adults of definite shape and size; higher forms show elaborate sensory and neuromotor mechanism; most are capable of locomotion.
  • Sexual reproduction is by copulation of male and female followed by embryological development. (Phyla in the Animal Kingdom tab.)

22Viruses

Why Viruses Are Outside the Five Kingdoms

  • Whittaker's system has no mention of lichens, viruses, viroids and prions.
  • Viruses are not considered truly 'living' if living means having a cell structure; they are non-cellular with an inert crystalline structure outside the living cell.
  • Once they infect a cell they take over the host's machinery to replicate themselves, killing the host.
  • Viruses are obligate parasites; inert outside their specific host cell.

Discovery — Who Did What

  • 'Virus' means venom or poisonous fluid.
  • Dmitri Ivanowsky (1892) recognised certain microbes as the causal organism of the mosaic disease of tobacco; they were smaller than bacteria since they passed through bacteria-proof filters.
  • M.W. Beijerinck (1898) showed the extract of infected tobacco plants could infect healthy plants; named the pathogen 'virus' and the fluid Contagium vivum fluidum (infectious living fluid).
  • W.M. Stanley (1935) showed viruses could be crystallised; the crystals consist largely of proteins.

Structure & Genetic Material

  • In addition to proteins, viruses contain genetic material — either RNA or DNA; no virus contains both RNA and DNA.
  • A virus is a nucleoprotein and the genetic material is infectious.
  • Plant viruses: generally single stranded RNA.
  • Animal viruses: single or double stranded RNA, or double stranded DNA.
  • Bacteriophages (viruses infecting bacteria): usually double stranded DNA viruses.
  • The protein coat is the capsid, made of small subunits called capsomeres, arranged in helical or polyhedral geometric forms; it protects the nucleic acid.

Diseases Caused

  • Humans: mumps, small pox, herpes, influenza, AIDS.
  • Plants — symptoms: mosaic formation, leaf rolling and curling, yellowing and vein clearing, dwarfing and stunted growth.

23Viroids & Prions

Viroids

  • Discovered by T.O. Diener in 1971; smaller than viruses; caused potato spindle tuber disease.
  • A free RNA — lacks the protein coat found in viruses, hence the name viroid.
  • The RNA of the viroid has low molecular weight.

Prions

  • Infectious neurological diseases transmitted by an agent consisting of abnormally folded protein; similar in size to viruses.
  • Notable diseases: Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) — 'mad cow disease' in cattle — and its analogous variant Creutzfeldt–Jacob disease (CJD) in humans.

24Lichens

Lichens

  • Symbiotic associations (mutually useful) between algae and fungi.
  • Algal component = phycobiont (autotrophic); fungal component = mycobiont (heterotrophic).
  • Algae prepare food for fungi; fungi provide shelter and absorb mineral nutrients and water for the partner.
  • So close is the association that one would never imagine two different organisms in a lichen.
  • Lichens are very good pollution indicators — they do not grow in polluted areas.

Plant Kingdom — Algae to Angiosperms

Classification systems first, then each plant group in evolutionary order — features, reproduction, classes and examples under their own headings.

01Plantae — Scope & Changing Understanding

What Plantae Includes Now

  • Under Whittaker's (1969) Five Kingdom classification — Monera, Protista, Fungi, Animalia and Plantae — this chapter deals with further classification within Kingdom Plantae.
  • Fungi, and members of Monera and Protista having cell walls, are now excluded from Plantae, though earlier classifications placed them in the same kingdom.
  • Cyanobacteria (blue green algae) are no longer 'algae'.
  • Groups described under Plantae: Algae, Bryophytes, Pteridophytes, Gymnosperms, Angiosperms.

02Systems of Classification in Angiosperms

Artificial Systems

  • Earliest systems used only gross superficial morphological characters — habit, colour, number and shape of leaves, etc.
  • Based mainly on vegetative characters or on androecium structure (system given by Linnaeus).
  • Artificial systems separated closely related species because they were based on few characteristics.
  • They gave equal weightage to vegetative and sexual characteristics — not acceptable, since vegetative characters are more easily affected by the environment.

Natural Systems

  • Based on natural affinities among organisms; consider not only external features but also internal features: ultra structure, anatomy, embryology and phytochemistry.
  • Such a classification for flowering plants was given by George Bentham and Joseph Dalton Hooker.

Phylogenetic Systems

  • Presently acceptable; based on evolutionary relationships between organisms.
  • Assumes that organisms belonging to the same taxa have a common ancestor.
  • Information from many other sources is used — more important when there is no supporting fossil evidence.

Modern Aids to Taxonomy

  • Numerical Taxonomy — easily done using computers; based on all observable characteristics; numbers and codes are assigned to all characters and the data processed, so each character is given equal importance and hundreds of characters can be considered.
  • Cytotaxonomy — based on cytological information: chromosome number, structure, behaviour.
  • Chemotaxonomy — uses the chemical constituents of the plant to resolve confusions.

03Algae — General Characters

Features & Habitat

  • Algae are chlorophyll-bearing, simple, thalloid, autotrophic and largely aquatic (fresh water and marine) organisms.
  • Also occur on moist stones, soils and wood; some occur in association with fungi (lichen) and animals (e.g., on sloth bear).

Form & Size

  • Highly variable: colonial forms like Volvox; filamentous forms like Ulothrix and Spirogyra.
  • A few marine forms such as kelps form massive plant bodies.

04Reproduction in Algae

Three Methods

  • Vegetative: by fragmentation — each fragment develops into a thallus.
  • Asexual: by different types of spores, the most common being zoospores — flagellated (motile); on germination give rise to new plants.
  • Sexual: through fusion of two gametes.

05Economic Importance of Algae

Uses of Algae

  • At least half of the total CO2 fixation on earth is carried out by algae through photosynthesis.
  • Increase the level of dissolved oxygen in their immediate environment.
  • Primary producers of energy-rich compounds forming the basis of the food cycles of all aquatic animals.
  • Many species of Porphyra, Laminaria and Sargassum are among the 70 species of marine algae used as food.
  • Certain marine brown and red algae produce large amounts of hydrocolloids (water-holding substances): algin (brown algae) and carrageen (red algae) — used commercially.
  • Agar — obtained from Gelidium and Gracilaria; used to grow microbes and in ice-creams and jellies.
  • Chlorella — a unicellular alga rich in proteins; used as food supplement even by space travellers.
Three Classes of Algae
  • Algae are divided into three main classes: Chlorophyceae, Phaeophyceae and Rhodophyceae.

06Chlorophyceae — Green Algae

Chlorophyceae

  • Commonly called green algae; plant body unicellular, colonial or filamentous.
  • Usually grass green due to dominance of pigments chlorophyll a and b, localised in definite chloroplasts.
  • Chloroplasts may be discoid, plate-like, reticulate, cup-shaped, spiral or ribbon-shaped.
  • Most have one or more storage bodies called pyrenoids in the chloroplasts — contain protein besides starch; some algae store food as oil droplets.
  • Rigid cell wall: inner layer of cellulose and outer layer of pectose.

Reproduction

  • Vegetative: usually by fragmentation.
  • Asexual: flagellated zoospores produced in zoosporangia.
  • Sexual: considerable variation; may be isogamous, anisogamous or oogamous.
  • Common examples: Chlamydomonas, Volvox, Ulothrix, Spirogyra and Chara.

07Phaeophyceae — Brown Algae

Occurrence & Body

  • Found primarily in marine habitats; great variation in size and form.
  • Range from simple branched filamentous forms (Ectocarpus) to profusely branched kelps, which may reach a height of 100 metres.
  • Plant body attached to substratum by a holdfast; has a stalk, the stipe, and a leaf-like photosynthetic organ, the frond.
  • Protoplast has, besides plastids, a centrally located vacuole and nucleus.

Pigments, Food & Wall

  • Possess chlorophyll a, c, carotenoids and xanthophylls.
  • Colour varies from olive green to shades of brown depending on the amount of xanthophyll pigment fucoxanthin.
  • Food stored as complex carbohydrates — laminarin or mannitol.
  • Vegetative cells have a cellulosic wall, usually covered outside by a gelatinous coating of algin.

Reproduction

  • Vegetative: by fragmentation.
  • Asexual: in most, by biflagellate zoospores — pear-shaped with two unequal, laterally attached flagella.
  • Sexual: isogamous, anisogamous or oogamous; union of gametes in water or within the oogonium (oogamous species).
  • Gametes are pyriform (pear-shaped) and bear two laterally attached flagella.
  • Common forms: Ectocarpus, Dictyota, Laminaria, Sargassum and Fucus.

08Rhodophyceae — Red Algae

Occurrence & Pigment

  • Called red algae due to predominance of the red pigment r-phycoerythrin.
  • Majority are marine, with greater concentrations in warmer areas.
  • Occur in well-lighted regions close to the surface and also at great depths in oceans where little light penetrates.
  • Red thalli of most are multicellular; some have complex body organisation.
  • Food stored as floridean starch — very similar to amylopectin and glycogen in structure.

Reproduction

  • Vegetative: usually by fragmentation.
  • Asexual: by non-motile spores.
  • Sexual: by non-motile gametes; oogamous and accompanied by complex post-fertilisation developments.
  • Common members: Polysiphonia, Porphyra, Gracilaria and Gelidium.

10Bryophytes — General Characters

Features

  • Include mosses and liverworts, commonly growing in moist shaded areas in the hills.
  • Called the amphibians of the plant kingdom — they live in soil but are dependent on water for sexual reproduction.
  • Usually occur in damp, humid and shaded localities; play an important role in plant succession on bare rocks/soil.
  • Plant body is more differentiated than algae — thallus-like, prostrate or erect, attached by unicellular or multicellular rhizoids.
  • Lack true roots, stem or leaves — may possess root-like, leaf-like or stem-like structures.

Life Cycle

  • Main plant body is haploid — produces gametes, hence called the gametophyte.
  • Sex organs are multicellular: male — antheridium (produces biflagellate antherozoids); female — archegonium (flask-shaped, produces a single egg).
  • Antherozoids are released into water and fuse with the egg → zygote.
  • Zygote does not undergo reduction division immediately; it produces a multicellular sporophyte.
  • Sporophyte is not free-living — attached to the photosynthetic gametophyte and derives nourishment from it.
  • Some sporophyte cells undergo meiosis to produce haploid spores, which germinate to produce gametophytes.

Economic & Ecological Importance

  • Of little economic importance, but some mosses provide food for herbaceous mammals, birds and other animals.
  • Sphagnum (a moss) provides peat — used as fuel and as packing material for trans-shipment of living material because of its capacity to hold water.
  • Mosses along with lichens are the first organisms to colonise rocks; they decompose rocks, making the substrate suitable for higher plants.
  • Mosses form dense mats, reducing the impact of falling rain and preventing soil erosion.
  • Bryophytes are divided into liverworts and mosses.

11Liverworts

Liverworts

  • Grow in moist, shady habitats: banks of streams, marshy ground, damp soil, bark of trees, deep in woods.
  • Plant body is thalloid, e.g., Marchantia; the thallus is dorsiventral and closely appressed to the substrate.
  • Leafy members have tiny leaf-like appendages in two rows on stem-like structures.

Reproduction

  • Asexual: by fragmentation of thalli or by gemmae — green, multicellular, asexual buds developing in small receptacles called gemma cups on the thalli.
  • Gemmae detach from the parent body and germinate into new individuals.
  • Sexual: male and female sex organs are produced on the same or different thalli.
  • Sporophyte is differentiated into foot, seta and capsule; after meiosis, spores are produced within the capsule; spores germinate to form free-living gametophytes.

12Mosses

Gametophyte Stages

  • The predominant stage of the life cycle is the gametophyte, consisting of two stages.
  • First — protonema stage: develops directly from a spore; creeping, green, branched and frequently filamentous.
  • Second — leafy stage: develops from the secondary protonema as a lateral bud; upright slender axes with spirally arranged leaves; attached to soil by multicellular, branched rhizoids; bears the sex organs.

Reproduction

  • Vegetative: by fragmentation and budding in the secondary protonema.
  • Sexual: antheridia and archegonia produced at the apex of leafy shoots.
  • After fertilisation the zygote develops into a sporophyte of foot, seta and capsule — more elaborate than in liverworts.
  • Capsule contains spores formed after meiosis; mosses have an elaborate mechanism of spore dispersal.
  • Common examples: Funaria, Polytrichum and Sphagnum.

13Pteridophytes — General Characters

Features

  • Include horsetails and ferns; used for medicinal purposes and as soil-binders; frequently grown as ornamentals.
  • Evolutionarily the first terrestrial plants to possess vascular tissues — xylem and phloem.
  • Found in cool, damp, shady places though some flourish in sandy-soil conditions.
  • Unlike bryophytes, the main plant body is a sporophyte, differentiated into true root, stem and leaves, with well-differentiated vascular tissues.
  • Leaves are small (microphylls) as in Selaginella or large (macrophylls) as in ferns.
  • Sporophytes bear sporangia subtended by leaf-like appendages called sporophylls; in some, sporophylls form compact strobili or cones (Selaginella, Equisetum).

14Pteridophytes — Life Cycle

From Spore to Sporophyte

  • Sporangia produce spores by meiosis in spore mother cells.
  • Spores germinate into inconspicuous, small but multicellular, free-living, mostly photosynthetic thalloid gametophytes called prothallus.
  • Gametophytes require cool, damp, shady places; with the need for water for fertilisation, the spread of living pteridophytes is limited and restricted to narrow geographical regions.
  • Gametophytes bear antheridia and archegonia; water is required to transfer antherozoids to the mouth of the archegonium.
  • Fusion of male gamete with the egg gives the zygote → a multicellular, well-differentiated sporophyte — the dominant phase.

Classes of Pteridophytes

  • Four classes: Psilopsida (Psilotum); Lycopsida (Selaginella, Lycopodium); Sphenopsida (Equisetum); Pteropsida (Dryopteris, Pteris, Adiantum).

15Homospory & Heterospory

Homosporous vs Heterosporous

  • In the majority of pteridophytes all spores are of similar kinds — homosporous.
  • Genera like Selaginella and Salvinia produce two kinds of spores — macro (large) and micro (small) — and are heterosporous.
  • Megaspores and microspores germinate into female and male gametophytes respectively.
  • Female gametophytes are retained on the parent sporophytes for variable periods; zygotes develop into young embryos within the female gametophytes.
  • This event is a precursor to the seed habit — an important step in evolution.

16Gymnosperms — Vegetative Features

Features

  • Gymnos = naked, sperma = seeds — ovules are not enclosed by any ovary wall and remain exposed both before and after fertilisation; seeds are naked.
  • Include medium-sized or tall trees and shrubs; giant redwood Sequoia is one of the tallest tree species.
  • Roots generally tap roots.
  • Roots of some genera have fungal association — mycorrhiza (Pinus); in others (Cycas) small specialised coralloid roots are associated with N2-fixing cyanobacteria.
  • Stems unbranched (Cycas) or branched (Pinus, Cedrus).
  • Leaves simple or compound; in Cycas the pinnate leaves persist for a few years.
  • Leaves are well adapted to withstand extremes of temperature, humidity and wind; in conifers the needle-like leaves reduce surface area, and thick cuticle and sunken stomata help reduce water loss.

17Gymnosperms — Reproduction

Spores, Cones & Gametophytes

  • Gymnosperms are heterosporous — produce haploid microspores and megaspores in sporangia borne on sporophylls arranged spirally along an axis to form lax or compact strobili or cones.
  • Strobili bearing microsporophylls and microsporangia = microsporangiate / male strobili.
  • Microspores develop into a highly reduced male gametophyte, confined to a limited number of cells — the pollen grain, developing within microsporangia.
  • Cones bearing megasporophylls with ovules (megasporangia) = macrosporangiate / female strobili.
  • Male and female cones may be on the same tree (Pinus); in Cycas male cones and megasporophylls are on different trees.

Ovule & Female Gametophyte

  • The megaspore mother cell differentiates from one of the cells of the nucellus; the nucellus protected by envelopes forms the ovule.
  • Ovules are borne on megasporophylls, which may be clustered to form female cones.
  • Megaspore mother cell divides meiotically into four megaspores.
  • One megaspore, enclosed in the megasporangium, develops into a multicellular female gametophyte bearing two or more archegonia; it is retained within the megasporangium.

Pollination to Seed

  • Unlike bryophytes and pteridophytes, the male and female gametophytes do not have an independent free-living existence — they remain within the sporangia retained on the sporophytes.
  • Pollen grains are released from the microsporangium, carried by air currents and reach the opening of ovules on megasporophylls.
  • The pollen tube carrying male gametes grows towards archegonia and discharges contents near their mouth.
  • After fertilisation: zygote → embryo; ovules → seeds — these seeds are not covered.

18Angiosperms

Angiosperms (Flowering Plants)

  • Unlike gymnosperms, pollen grains and ovules are developed in specialised structures called flowers.
  • Seeds are enclosed in fruits.
  • An exceptionally large group occurring in a wide range of habitats.
  • Size range: smallest — Wolffia; tall trees — Eucalyptus (over 100 metres).
  • Provide food, fodder, fuel, medicines and several other commercially important products.
  • Divided into two classes: dicotyledons and monocotyledons.

Animal Kingdom — Basis of Classification & Phyla

The six fundamental features first, then each phylum, then the chordate classes — each under its own heading.

01Why Classify? — Introduction

Need for Classification

  • Over a million species of animals have been described — hence the need for classification becomes all the more important.
  • Classification also helps in assigning a systematic position to newly described species.
  • Fundamental features common to individuals: arrangement of cells, body symmetry, nature of coelom, patterns of digestive, circulatory or reproductive systems — used as the basis of classification.

02Levels of Organisation

Four Levels

  • Cellular level — cells arranged as loose cell aggregates, with some division of labour: Sponges (Porifera).
  • Tissue level — cells performing the same function arranged into tissues: Coelenterates.
  • Organ level — tissues grouped to form organs, each specialised for a particular function: Platyhelminthes and other higher phyla.
  • Organ system level — organs associated to form functional systems, each for a specific physiological function: Annelids, Arthropods, Molluscs, Echinoderms and Chordates.

Digestive System — Incomplete vs Complete

  • Incomplete — only a single opening serving as both mouth and anus; e.g., Platyhelminthes.
  • Complete — two openings: mouth and anus.

Circulatory System — Open vs Closed

  • Open type — blood is pumped out of the heart and the cells and tissues are directly bathed in it.
  • Closed type — blood circulates through a series of vessels of varying diameters (arteries, veins and capillaries).

03Symmetry

Types of Symmetry

  • Asymmetrical — no plane through the centre divides the body into equal halves: Sponges (mostly).
  • Radial symmetry — any plane passing through the central axis divides the organism into two identical halves: Coelenterates, ctenophores and echinoderms.
  • Bilateral symmetry — body can be divided into identical left and right halves in only one plane: Annelids, arthropods, etc.
Trap — Echinoderms
  • Adult echinoderms are radially symmetrical but their larvae are bilaterally symmetrical — Fig. 4.4 notes 'radial or bilateral depending on the stage'.

04Diploblastic & Triploblastic Organisation

Germinal Layers

  • Diploblastic — cells in two embryonic layers: external ectoderm and internal endoderm; e.g., coelenterates.
  • An undifferentiated layer, the mesoglea, lies between ectoderm and endoderm in diploblastic animals.
  • Triploblastic — developing embryo has a third germinal layer, the mesoderm, between ectoderm and endoderm; platyhelminthes to chordates.

05Coelom (Body Cavity)

Types Based on Body Cavity

  • Presence or absence of a cavity between the body wall and the gut wall is very important in classification.
  • The body cavity lined by mesoderm = coelom; animals possessing it = coelomates — annelids, molluscs, arthropods, echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates.
  • Pseudocoelom — body cavity not lined by mesoderm; mesoderm is present as scattered pouches between ectoderm and endoderm; animals = pseudocoelomates — aschelminthes.
  • Acoelomates — body cavity absent: platyhelminthes.

06Segmentation & Notochord

Segmentation

  • Body externally and internally divided into segments with serial repetition of at least some organs.
  • Example: earthworm — this pattern is metameric segmentation and the phenomenon is metamerism.

Notochord

  • Notochord is a mesodermally derived, rod-like structure formed on the dorsal side during embryonic development in some animals.
  • Animals with notochord = chordates; those without = non-chordates — Porifera to Echinoderms.

08Phylum Porifera

Porifera (Sponges)

  • Commonly known as sponges; generally marine and mostly asymmetrical.
  • Primitive multicellular animals with cellular level of organisation.
  • Water transport / canal system: water enters through minute pores — ostia — into a central cavity, the spongocoel, and exits through the osculum; helps in food gathering, respiratory exchange, removal of waste.
  • Choanocytes (collar cells) line the spongocoel and canals.
  • Digestion is intracellular.
  • Skeleton of spicules or spongin fibres.
  • Sexes not separate — hermaphrodite (eggs and sperms from the same individual).
  • Asexual reproduction by fragmentation; sexual by gametes. Fertilisation internal; development indirect with a larval stage morphologically distinct from the adult.
  • Examples: Sycon (Scypha), Spongilla (fresh water sponge), Euspongia (bath sponge).

09Phylum Coelenterata (Cnidaria)

Coelenterata

  • Aquatic, mostly marine, sessile or free-swimming, radially symmetrical.
  • Name cnidaria derived from cnidoblasts / cnidocytes — containing stinging capsules or nematocysts — on tentacles and body; used for anchorage, defence and capture of prey.
  • Tissue level of organisation; diploblastic.
  • Central gastro-vascular cavity with a single opening — the mouth on the hypostome.
  • Digestion is extracellular and intracellular.
  • Some (e.g., corals) have a skeleton of calcium carbonate.

Polyp & Medusa

  • Two basic body forms: polyp — sessile, cylindrical (e.g., Hydra, Adamsia); medusa — umbrella-shaped, free-swimming (e.g., Aurelia / jelly fish).
  • Those existing in both forms show alternation of generations (metagenesis) — polyps produce medusae asexually and medusae form polyps sexually (e.g., Obelia).
  • Examples: Physalia (Portuguese man-of-war), Adamsia (Sea anemone), Pennatula (Sea-pen), Gorgonia (Sea-fan), Meandrina (Brain coral).

10Phylum Ctenophora

Ctenophora (Sea Walnuts / Comb Jellies)

  • Exclusively marine, radially symmetrical, diploblastic; tissue level of organisation.
  • Body bears eight external rows of ciliated comb plates for locomotion.
  • Digestion both extracellular and intracellular.
  • Bioluminescence (property of a living organism to emit light) is well-marked.
  • Sexes not separate; reproduction only by sexual means; fertilisation external, development indirect.
  • Examples: Pleurobrachia and Ctenoplana.

11Phylum Platyhelminthes

Platyhelminthes (Flatworms)

  • Dorso-ventrally flattened body — hence 'flatworms'; mostly endoparasites in animals including humans.
  • Bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, acoelomate; organ level of organisation.
  • Hooks and suckers present in the parasitic forms; some absorb nutrients directly through the body surface.
  • Specialised cells called flame cells help in osmoregulation and excretion.
  • Sexes not separate; fertilisation internal; development through many larval stages.
  • Some members like Planaria possess high regeneration capacity.
  • Examples: Taenia (Tapeworm), Fasciola (Liver fluke).

12Phylum Aschelminthes

Aschelminthes (Roundworms)

  • Body circular in cross-section — hence 'roundworms'; free-living (aquatic and terrestrial) or parasitic in plants and animals.
  • Organ-system level; bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, pseudocoelomate.
  • Alimentary canal complete with a well-developed muscular pharynx.
  • An excretory tube removes wastes from the body cavity through the excretory pore.
  • Sexes separate — dioecious; often females longer than males.
  • Fertilisation internal; development direct (young resemble adult) or indirect.
  • Examples: Ascaris (Roundworm), Wuchereria (Filaria worm), Ancylostoma (Hookworm).

13Phylum Annelida

Annelida

  • Aquatic (marine and fresh water) or terrestrial; free-living, sometimes parasitic.
  • Organ-system level; bilateral symmetry, triploblastic, metamerically segmented, coelomate.
  • Name from Latin annulus = little ring; body surface marked into segments or metameres.
  • Longitudinal and circular muscles help locomotion; aquatic annelids like Nereis have lateral appendages, parapodia, for swimming.
  • Closed circulatory system.
  • Nephridia help in osmoregulation and excretion.
  • Neural system: paired ganglia connected by lateral nerves to a double ventral nerve cord.
  • Nereis is dioecious; earthworms and leeches are monoecious. Reproduction is sexual.
  • Examples: Nereis, Pheretima (Earthworm), Hirudinaria (Blood-sucking leech).

14Phylum Arthropoda

Arthropoda

  • The largest phylum of Animalia, including insects; over two-thirds of all named species on earth are arthropods.
  • Organ-system level; bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, segmented, coelomate.
  • Body covered by chitinous exoskeleton; body consists of head, thorax and abdomen.
  • Jointed appendages (arthros = joint, poda = appendages).
  • Respiratory organs: gills, book gills, book lungs or tracheal system.
  • Circulatory system is open type.
  • Sensory organs: antennae, eyes (compound and simple), statocysts (balancing organs).
  • Excretion through malpighian tubules.
  • Mostly dioecious; fertilisation usually internal; mostly oviparous; development direct or indirect.

Examples

  • Economically important insects: Apis (Honey bee), Bombyx (Silkworm), Laccifer (Lac insect).
  • Vectors: Anopheles, Culex and Aedes (mosquitoes).
  • Gregarious pest: Locusta (Locust).
  • Living fossil: Limulus (King crab).

15Phylum Mollusca

Mollusca

  • The second largest animal phylum; terrestrial or aquatic (marine or fresh water).
  • Organ-system level; bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, coelomate.
  • Body covered by a calcareous shell; unsegmented with distinct head, muscular foot and visceral hump.
  • A soft, spongy layer of skin forms the mantle over the visceral hump; the space between hump and mantle = mantle cavity, containing feather-like gills (respiratory and excretory functions).
  • Anterior head has sensory tentacles; the mouth has a file-like rasping organ, the radula, for feeding.
  • Usually dioecious and oviparous with indirect development.
  • Examples: Pila (Apple snail), Pinctada (Pearl oyster), Sepia (Cuttlefish), Loligo (Squid), Octopus (Devil fish), Aplysia (Sea hare), Dentalium (Tusk shell), Chaetopleura (Chiton).

16Phylum Echinodermata

Echinodermata

  • Endoskeleton of calcareous ossicles — hence the name (Spiny bodied); all marine; organ-system level.
  • Adults radially symmetrical but larvae bilaterally symmetrical; triploblastic, coelomate.
  • Digestive system complete — mouth on the lower (ventral) side, anus on the upper (dorsal) side.
  • Most distinctive feature: water vascular system — helps in locomotion, capture and transport of food, and respiration.
  • Excretory system absent. Sexes separate; reproduction sexual; fertilisation usually external; development indirect with free-swimming larva.
  • Examples: Asterias (Star fish), Echinus (Sea urchin), Antedon (Sea lily), Cucumaria (Sea cucumber), Ophiura (Brittle star).

17Phylum Hemichordata

Hemichordata

  • Earlier considered a sub-phylum under Chordata; now a separate phylum under non-chordata.
  • Have a rudimentary structure in the collar region, the stomochord — similar to notochord.
  • Small group of worm-like marine animals; organ-system level; bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, coelomate.
  • Body cylindrical, composed of anterior proboscis, a collar and a long trunk.
  • Circulatory system open type; respiration through gills; excretory organ = proboscis gland.
  • Sexes separate; fertilisation external; development indirect.
  • Examples: Balanoglossus and Saccoglossus.

18Phylum Chordata — Characters & Subphyla

Fundamental Characters

  • Presence of a notochord, a dorsal hollow nerve cord and paired pharyngeal gill slits.
  • Bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, coelomate; organ-system level.
  • Possess a post-anal tail and a closed circulatory system.

Three Subphyla

  • Chordata is divided into: Urochordata (Tunicata), Cephalochordata and Vertebrata.

  • Urochordata: notochord present only in larval tail. Examples: Ascidia, Salpa, Doliolum.
  • Cephalochordata: notochord extends from head to tail and is persistent throughout life. Example: Branchiostoma (Amphioxus or Lancelet).

Vertebrata

  • Possess notochord during the embryonic period; replaced by a cartilaginous or bony vertebral column in the adult.
  • All vertebrates are chordates but all chordates are not vertebrates.
  • Besides chordate characters: ventral muscular heart with two, three or four chambers; kidneys for excretion and osmoregulation; paired appendages (fins or limbs).

20Class Cyclostomata

Cyclostomata

  • All living members are ectoparasites on some fishes.
  • Elongated body with 6–15 pairs of gill slits for respiration.
  • Sucking and circular mouth without jaws; body devoid of scales and paired fins.
  • Cranium and vertebral column are cartilaginous; circulation is closed.
  • Marine but migrate to fresh water for spawning; die within a few days after spawning; larvae after metamorphosis return to the ocean.
  • Examples: Petromyzon (Lamprey) and Myxine (Hagfish).

21Class Chondrichthyes

Chondrichthyes (Cartilaginous Fishes)

  • Marine, streamlined body, cartilaginous endoskeleton; mouth located ventrally.
  • Notochord persistent throughout life.
  • Gill slits separate and without operculum (gill cover).
  • Skin tough, containing minute placoid scales; teeth are modified placoid scales, backwardly directed; jaws very powerful; predaceous.
  • Absence of air bladder — they must swim constantly to avoid sinking.
  • Heart two-chambered (one auricle and one ventricle).
  • Some have electric organs (e.g., Torpedo); some possess poison sting (e.g., Trygon).
  • Cold-blooded (poikilothermous) — cannot regulate body temperature.
  • Sexes separate; males have claspers on pelvic fins; internal fertilisation; many are viviparous.
  • Examples: Scoliodon (Dog fish), Pristis (Saw fish), Carcharodon (Great white shark), Trygon (Sting ray).

22Class Osteichthyes

Osteichthyes (Bony Fishes)

  • Marine and fresh water; bony endoskeleton; streamlined body; mouth mostly terminal.
  • Four pairs of gills covered by an operculum on each side.
  • Skin covered with cycloid/ctenoid scales.
  • Air bladder present — regulates buoyancy.
  • Heart two-chambered; cold-blooded; sexes separate.
  • Fertilisation usually external; mostly oviparous; development direct.
  • Examples — Marine: Exocoetus (Flying fish), Hippocampus (Sea horse); Freshwater: Labeo (Rohu), Catla (Katla), Clarias (Magur); Aquarium: Betta (Fighting fish), Pterophyllum (Angel fish).

23Class Amphibia

Amphibia

  • Name from Gr. Amphi = dual, bios = life — live in aquatic and terrestrial habitats.
  • Most have two pairs of limbs; body divisible into head and trunk; tail present in some.
  • Skin moist, without scales; eyes have eyelids; a tympanum represents the ear.
  • Alimentary canal, urinary and reproductive tracts open into a common chamber — the cloaca.
  • Respiration by gills, lungs and skin.
  • Heart three-chambered (two auricles and one ventricle); cold-blooded.
  • Sexes separate; fertilisation external; oviparous; development indirect.
  • Examples: Bufo (Toad), Rana (Frog), Hyla (Tree frog), Salamandra (Salamander), Ichthyophis (Limbless amphibia).

24Class Reptilia

Reptilia

  • Name from Latin repere / reptum = to creep or crawl.
  • Mostly terrestrial; body covered by dry, cornified skin, epidermal scales or scutes.
  • No external ear openings — tympanum represents ear; limbs, when present, two pairs.
  • Heart usually three-chambered but four-chambered in crocodiles.
  • Poikilotherms; snakes and lizards shed their scales as skin cast.
  • Sexes separate; fertilisation internal; oviparous; development direct.
  • Examples: Chelone (Turtle), Testudo (Tortoise), Chameleon (Tree lizard), Calotes (Garden lizard), Crocodilus (Crocodile), Alligator (Alligator), Hemidactylus (Wall lizard).
  • Poisonous snakes: Naja (Cobra), Bangarus (Krait), Vipera (Viper).

25Class Aves

Aves (Birds)

  • Characteristic: presence of feathers; most can fly except flightless birds (e.g., Ostrich); possess a beak.
  • Forelimbs modified into wings; hind limbs generally have scales and are modified for walking, swimming or clasping tree branches.
  • Skin dry, without glands except the oil gland at the base of the tail.
  • Endoskeleton fully ossified; long bones hollow with air cavities (pneumatic).
  • Digestive tract has additional chambers — crop and gizzard.
  • Heart completely four-chambered; warm-blooded (homoiothermous).
  • Respiration by lungs; air sacs connected to lungs supplement respiration.
  • Sexes separate; fertilisation internal; oviparous; development direct.
  • Examples: Corvus (Crow), Columba (Pigeon), Psittacula (Parrot), Struthio (Ostrich), Pavo (Peacock), Aptenodytes (Penguin), Neophron (Vulture).

26Class Mammalia

Mammalia

  • Found in a variety of habitats — polar ice caps, deserts, mountains, forests, grasslands and dark caves; some adapted to fly or live in water.
  • Most unique characteristic: mammary glands for nourishing the young.
  • Two pairs of limbs adapted for walking, running, climbing, burrowing, swimming or flying.
  • Skin unique in possessing hair; external ears (pinnae) present; different types of teeth in the jaw.
  • Heart four-chambered; homoiothermous; respiration by lungs.
  • Sexes separate; fertilisation internal; mostly viviparous with few exceptions; development direct.
  • Oviparous example: Ornithorhynchus (Platypus).
  • Viviparous examples: Macropus (Kangaroo), Pteropus (Flying fox), Camelus (Camel), Macaca (Monkey), Rattus (Rat), Canis (Dog), Felis (Cat), Elephas (Elephant), Equus (Horse), Delphinus (Common dolphin), Balaenoptera (Blue whale), Panthera tigris (Tiger), Panthera leo (Lion).

29Quick Revision — Traps & Match Table

MCQ Traps — Animal Kingdom
  • Arthropoda = largest phylum (over two-thirds of named species); Mollusca = second largest.
  • Aschelminthes = pseudocoelomates; Platyhelminthes = acoelomates with incomplete digestive system.
  • Echinoderms: adult radial, larva bilateral; water vascular system; no excretory system.
  • Hemichordata — now a separate non-chordate phylum (stomochord ≠ notochord).
  • Chondrichthyes: no air bladder, no operculum, persistent notochord, claspers; Osteichthyes: air bladder + operculum, direct development.
  • Amphibian heart three-chambered; reptile heart three-chambered except crocodiles (four).
  • Birds: only one skin gland — the oil gland; pneumatic bones; crop and gizzard.
  • Platypus = egg-laying mammal.
  • Urochordata: notochord only in larval tail; Cephalochordata: notochord head to tail, throughout life.
  • Hemichordata have an open circulatory system; Annelida closed; Arthropoda open.
