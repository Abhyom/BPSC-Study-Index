1

Babur — Ancestry, Rise & the Road to India

1483–1526 · Ferghana → Samarkand → Kabul → Delhi
Babur — identity card
AspectDetail
Full nameZahiruddin Muhammad Babur — after becoming Mughal ruler in India he adopted the name Babur (Chhattisgarh PCS 2003).
Born14 February 1483 at Ferghana (now in Uzbekistan) to Umar Shaikh Mirza and Qutlugh Nigar Khanum; he was the eldest son of Umar Shaikh Mirza.
Accession in FerghanaJune 1494 — ascended the throne of Ferghana as a minor of 11 years 4 months; his father's death was the trigger. This accession marked the beginning of the Timurid dynasty in the region.
AncestryTimur through his paternal line and Chengiz (Genghis) Khan through his maternal line — a Turko-Mongol pedigree. Because of Timur's lineage the Mughals are also called Timurids (71st & 69th BPSC).
BranchBelonged to the Chagatai branch of the Turks — named after Chagatai, the second son of Genghis Khan, ruler of Central Asia and Turkistan. Mughal rulers were therefore Chagatai Turks — not Persians (Iranians), not Afghans (U.P. UDA/LDA 2010).
Founding dateFought First Battle of Panipat on 21 April 1526; declared himself ruler on 27 April 1526 and founded the Mughal Empire in India.
Lesson of his lifeSuccess through patience and resolution — father died in childhood, yet he founded an empire (R.A.S. 1993 picks Babur for this).

Why India? — motives and the invitation

  • Babur's financial strain and the potential Uzbek threat made him consider India as a refuge and strategic base for further operations.
  • After conquering Afghanistan, Babur believed he had a rightful claim to territories such as Punjab because they had been annexed by his ancestor Timur.
  • The unstable political scenario in India and discontent of Afghans and Rajputs against Ibrahim Lodi (Sultan since 1517, after his father's death) facilitated the conquest.
  • Alam Khan — uncle of Ibrahim Lodi and a pretender to the Delhi throne — invited Babur (I.A.S. 2003: not a cousin, not Dilawar's father, not a Punjab official).
  • Daulat Khan Lodi, governor of Punjab, quarrelled bitterly with Ibrahim: when ordered to Delhi he refused and sent his son Dilawar Khan to Babur asking help to dethrone Ibrahim and enthrone Alam Khan. In the "annexation of Afghanistan" question, Daulat Khan is the invitee (U.P. RO/ARO 2021 — not Ustad Ali, Mustafa or Hasan Khan).
  • Rana Sanga of Mewar had also sent an invitation — a "golden opportunity" that assured Babur the moment to win India had come. Babur's own memoir records Sanga's promise: if the king reached Delhi from one side, Sanga would attack Agra from the other.
  • Outcome: Babur reunited Kabul and Qandahar with Northern India after defeating the Lodis and the Rajput Confederacy.
Trap — what did Babur's arrival actually introduce? (I.A.S. 2015; 69th BPSC 2023)
  • Gunpowder was already known in India before Babur (used to demolish fort walls) — Babur's credit is the skilled use of gunpowder in cannons/guns with cavalry, i.e. introduction of cannons in warfare.
  • Arch and dome were introduced in the Sultanate period — Babur did not introduce them or influence Indian architecture (he found local buildings lacking "shape and balance" in the Babur Nama).
  • Correct combination: establishment of Timurid dynasty + cannons in warfare (BPSC option c: 3 and 4).

Babur — Battles & Military Innovation

1526–1529 · Panipat, Khanwa, Chanderi, Ghaghra
Babur's four major battles

Chronology & pair traps from PYQ
  • Correct order: First Panipat (1526) → Khanwa (1527) → Chanderi (1528) → Ghaghra (1529) (Uttarakhand PCS 2024 answer: II, IV, III, I).
  • U.P. PCS Re-Exam 2015 "not correctly matched": Chanderi – 1530 is wrong (correct: 1528).
  • Wider sequences tested: Khanwa 1527 → Ghaghra 1529 → Chausa 1539 → Samugarh 1658 (Chhattisgarh 2008); Khanwa 1527 → Chanderi 1528 → Chausa 1539 → Kannauj 1540 → Talikota 1565 (U.P. RO/ARO 2016).
  • "Babur & Rana Sanga" = Khanwa; "Babur & Ibrahim Lodi" = First Panipat; "Humayun & Ibrahim Lodi" is a false pair (U.P. PCS 1996/2005/2012).

Military and warfare

  • Artillery: gunpowder was known earlier (69th BPSC), yet Babur exemplified its skilled use with artillery and cavalry (71st BPSC). Cannons were an important addition in 16th-century warfare and were used for the first time in India at First Panipat. Ustad Ali Quli led the artillery; Mustafa commanded the battalion of gunners.
  • Tulughma system: dividing the whole army into various units (centre, right, left wings, forward and rear divisions). It was originally an Uzbek technique (used by Shaybani at Sar-e-Pul). Babur adapted it and paired it with the Araba (carts/wagons) tactic (Jharkhand PCS 2016).
  • Rumi (Ottoman) method of warfare: Babur applied it at Panipat — the Ottoman system of war (Jharkhand PSC Mains 2016 → Babur).
  • Reason for victory (BPSC 39th, 1994): his skilled warfare (not horse-riding force, Tulughma alone, or Afghan disputes). U.P. PCS 1990 phrases it as skilled commandants. Babur's own verdict on Ibrahim: an inexperienced young man, careless in his movements, who marched without order, halted or retired without method and engaged without foresight.
  • Horses as strategic asset: the Mughals in control of the Khyber Pass got a consistent supply of high-quality horses from the West.

Memorials of victory

  • Kabuli Bagh Mosque — built by Babur at Panipat in 1527 to commemorate victory over Sultan Ibrahim Lodi (71st BPSC); a brick mosque.

Babur — Empire, Administration & Culture

Extent of the empire · governance · coins · buildings · religion
Babur's empire and administration
AspectDetail
ExtentKabul + Punjab + area of modern Uttar Pradesh — not Rajasthan, which stayed under various Rajput rulers (Uttarakhand PCS 2003, answer 1-2-3). Region from Ballia to Hisar-Firoza, Bayana and Gwalior was governed innovatively.
Method of ruleGoverned through allocation of fiefs/jagirs and parganas, appointing nobles/princes to territorial charges over the vast empire.
Family postingsBadakhshan → Humayun; Kabul, Qandahar, Multan → Kamran.
PunjabSeparate officers for Lahore (Mir Yusuf Ali), Sialkot, Dipalpur, Bheera, Sirhind, Hisar-Firoza.
CoinsIntroduced silver and copper coins; the silver coin was called Shahrukh (U.P. RO/ARO 2017).
RajputanaNot included; Babur mentioned two Hindu states in his memoir — Vijayanagara and Mewar (Chittor) (U.P. PCS 1995: Mewar).

Architecture

  • Introduced the Char Bagh (Persian-style garden layout); gardens Ram Bagh and Zahra Bagh at Agra (a garden at Agra ordered by him).
  • Mosques at Panipat (Kabuli Bagh), Ayodhya and Sambhal (Jama Masjid, Sambhal — U.P. PCS Mains 2011 match).
  • Mir Baqi, Babur's army chief / commandant, built the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya (U.P. PCS 1992: "it is believed" — not built by Babur, Humayun or Nizam-ul-Mulk).

Religious views

  • An orthodox Sunni but with an open-minded approach; devoted to the Naqshbandiyya Sufi Khwaja Ubaidullah Ahrar.
  • Proficient in Persian and Arabic; earned the title "Qalandar" for generosity.
Context — Babur's literary legacyBabur's writings (Tuzuk-i-Baburi / Baburnama, Diwan, Mubaiyan, Risala-i-Usaj), their language, translators and the memoir tradition he began are collected in the Mughal Literature chapter later in this dossier, so every book–author fact sits in one place.

Humayun — Rise, Fall & Restoration

1530–1540 and 1555–1556
Humayun — identity card
AspectDetail
Birth & familyEldest son of Babur, born at Kabul in 1508; mother Maham Begum (related to the Shia sect). Kamran, Askari, Hindal were Babur's sons and Humayun's half-brothers — Kamran & Askari by Gulrukh Begum, Hindal by Dildar Begum. Usman was not a brother (U.P. Lower Sub. 2008). Gulbadan Begum was Babur's daughter (his sister).
Reigns1530–1540 and 1555–1556.
Problems on accessionUnconsolidated administration, financial instability, and rising influence of Afghans in the east. Brother Kamran governed Kabul, Qandahar and Punjab (and Multan) — fragmented authority (71st BPSC).
Rajput frontHumayun wished to establish firm control by subduing Rajput states and sent Kamran against Rao Jaitsi of Bikaner; per Beethu Souza's Rao-Jaitsi-Ro-Chhanda it was Rao Jaitsi who defeated Kamran (Rajasthan PCS 2024).
CoinageAfter the fall of the Delhi Sultanate, Humayun was the first ruler to issue a gold coin; Sher Shah then also introduced a gold coin. Uttarakhand PCS 2012: UKPSC accepted option (a) Akbar — which is wrong; correct answer is Humayun.

Sequence traps — four battles of Humayun
  • Order: Devara (Daurah) → Chausa → Kannauj → Sirhind (41st BPSC 1996 answer d).
  • Wider chronologies: Daurah 1532 → Chausa 1539 → Kannauj 1540 → Samugarh 1658 (U.P. RO/ARO 2017 — Samugarh was fought between Dara Shikoh and Aurangzeb–Murad, not Humayun); Daurah → Chausa → Kannauj → Samugarh also in U.P. PCS 2025.
  • Pair traps: Chausa – 1538 and Kannauj – 1539 are wrong; correct Chausa June 1539, Kannauj May 1540 (Chhattisgarh 2016). Battle of Haldighati (1576) is the only correct pair among the options.
  • Battle of Bilgram = Battle of Kannauj (Humayun vs Sher Shah).
  • The Chausa date in one source reads 25/26 June 1539.

Exile (1540–1555) and restoration

  • Humayun left India; during his stay at Amarkot (Sindh, Pakistan) Akbar was born in 1542 (at Raja Veersaal's palace, 15 Oct 1542).
  • He travelled to Iran, seeking help from the Safavid Shah; while in Persia he met the painters Mir Sayyid Ali and Abdus Samad, and brought them to India (art details in the Painting chapter).
  • The Sur dynasty was declining; in 1555 Humayun defeated the Afghans and reclaimed Delhi with support of Bairam Khan.
  • 1556: died from a fall from the staircase of his library (Dinpanah library, Delhi).

Architecture

  • Built a new city in Delhi — Dinpanah (1533). The Purana Qila is the inner citadel of Dinpanah; Sher Shah later renamed it Shergarh and added structures.
  • Jamali mosque and the mosque of Isa Khan were built in Delhi in this period.
  • Humayun's Tomb — built by his wife Haji (Hamida Banu) Begum at Delhi, near Dinpanah, in 1565–1572. It is the first double-dome mausoleum of Mughal architecture, set inside a garden; architects Mirak Mirza Ghiyas (a Persian) and his son Sayyid Muhammad. It is a synthesis of Persian and Indian styles (R.A.S. 2003 — not Qutb Minar, Lodi's tomb or Red Fort). Haji Begum is the woman who built a mausoleum for her emperor husband (BPSC 48th–52nd, 2008 — not Mumtaz Mahal).
  • Din Panah ↔ Humayun in builder–monument matches (U.P. PCS Mains 2011; UDA/LDA 2010).

Sher Shah Suri — Rise, Conquests & Sur Dynasty

1540–1555 · the second Afghan empire
Sher Shah Suri — identity card
AspectDetail
Original nameFarid Khan, son of Hasan Khan — a Jagirdar during Lodi rule, from Sasaram (Bihar). Given the title Sher after killing a tiger.
EducationLeft home in 1494 and studied at Jaunpur — then famous as "Siraj-i-Hind" (R.A.S. 2013; U.P. UDA/LDA 2010: Sher Shah was the "highly educated" ruler among Balban, Alauddin Khalji, Ibrahim Lodi).
Early trainingManaged his father's manor — obtained essential administrative knowledge; had knowledge of Mughal military composition and finance.
TitlesAssumed Hazrat-e-Ala after defeating Nusrat Shah of Bengal, and afterwards the royal title Sher Shah after defeating Humayun at Chausa (26 June 1539), when Khutba and coins were issued in his name (44th BPSC 2000 → Sher Shah Suri, not Bahlul/Sikandar Lodi or Islam Shah).
EstablishmentDefeated Humayun in 1540 (Kannauj), marking the second Afghan Empire in India (the Sur dynasty).
Death22 May 1545 at Kalinjar — while besieging the fort (Rajput king Kirat Singh); all tactics failed, so he ordered the walls to be blown up with gunpowder and was gravely wounded by an accidental gunpowder blast. Kalinjar was his last campaign (U.P. PCS 1993, U.P. RO/ARO 2016).
SuccessorsIslam Shah (1545–53; second son, second ruler of the dynasty; ruled entire North India for 8 years) and Adil Shah Suri (1555) — the last rulers of the dynasty; Hemu was Adil Shah's wazir.

Sher Shah Suri — Administration, Reforms & Architecture

The forerunner of Akbar's system
Context — why Sher Shah mattersSher Shah is considered the antecedent of Akbar as an administrative imperialist. His five-year reign brought "wise and salutary changes in every conceivable branch of administration" — so his qualities as a ruler were more remarkable than his victories on the battlefield (U.P. RO/ARO 2021: statement 1 true, statement 2 false). Reform areas tested together (Uttarakhand PCS 2003): revenue, administrative, military and currency — all four correct.
Sher Shah's administration
LevelMachinery
Central governmentHighly centralised; the ruler was head of the central government. Ministers: Diwan-i-Wizarat (Wazir; revenue & finance), Diwan-i-Ariz (army), Diwan-i-Rasalat (foreign affairs), Diwan-i-Insha (communications).
SarkarChief Shiqdar (law & order) and chief Munsif (judge). Number of sarkars: 47 according to Dr. Kanungo.
BengalDifferent system: province divided into 19 sarkars (per Satish Chandra), each under a military Shiqdar, with a non-military official "Amir-i-Bangala" — Qazi Fajilat — appointed to assist; arrangement meant to avert the threat of revolt.
ParganaShiqdar (military officer), Amin (land revenue), Fotedar (treasurer), Karkuns (accountants).
VillageMauza — basic revenue unit; hereditary chiefs collected taxes as intermediaries. Amin also built/repaired embankments and protected cultivable land. Other officers: Khazandar, Munsif-i-khazana, Qanungo (revenue records).
JusticeQazis across the kingdom; panchayats and caste bodies gave civil law to Hindus; zamindars and shiqdars assisted in criminal justice. An orthodox Sunni reputed for unbiased justice — penalising oppressive nobles and even relatives; large-scale charity and stipends. Jizya still collected but styled a city tax (in the countryside collected within land revenue).
Sher Shah's revenue system
FeatureDetail
PhilosophyFor stability the peasant had to be satisfied; settlement directly with cultivators — Ryotwari-like; improved systematic measurement of land.
Crop-rate (Ray)State demand = one-third of the average yield per bigha, allowing for soil quality; payable in cash at current market rates.
Famine cess2.5 seers per bigha levied as safeguard against famine and calamities.
Patta & QabuliyatPeasants got a Patta (area sown, crop type and amount payable in writing, issued by the central authority with the government's share stated) and accepted it through Qabuliyat (agreement deed) — U.P. PCS 2008/2009: only Sher Shah, not Alauddin, Tughluq or Akbar.
Three assessment methodsNasq / Muktai / Kankut; Nakadi or Jabti; Galla Bakshi or Batai. Batai had three forms: Khet Batai, Lank Batai, Raasi Batai (48th–52nd BPSC 2008: "Batai" = system of calculating revenue by sharing actual produce).
Sher Shah — army, currency, trade, roads
FieldDetail
ArmyInspired by Alauddin Khalji; soldiers recruited and paid directly; Chehra (descriptive roll of each soldier) and Dagh (branding of horses with imperial mark) revived to stop fraud. Three segments: Sawars, Elephants, Footmen; personal force Royal Khalsa Khail.
CurrencyReplaced mixed-metal beaten coins by pure gold, silver and copper coins. Silver Rupiya = 178 grains (of which 173 pure silver); copper Dam = 308 grains (~20 gm; another source misprints it as 380). Exchange rate 1 Rupiya : 64 Dam — not the 1:40 of the later Mughal period (Ch. on Coinage). Name, title and mint-place inscribed in Arabic and Devanagari. V.A. Smith: basis of the existing British currency. Mint-of-silver question: Sher Shah introduced silver coin (M.P. PCS 1991, Jharkhand 2016).
TradeTaxes only at points of entry and sale; uniform weights & measures; standardised metal content of coins.
RoadsRestored the Grand Trunk Road (ancient Uttarapatha; Sadak-e-Azam) from Sonargaon/Tamralipti (Bengal) to Peshawar (Purushpur) / Kabul; built Sarais (rest houses) on trade routes. In medieval India sarais gave temporary accommodation to Indian and foreign travellers, pilgrims and merchants — hubs of cross-cultural interaction (Chhattisgarh PCS 2023).

Architecture

  • Purana Qila (Delhi) — its present form was built by Sher Shah (and Humayun raised the initial citadel); Qila-i-Kuhna mosque (1541, "famous for elegant architectural qualities") and Sher Mandal inside it. Not the work of Firuz Tughluq, Ibrahim Lodi, Babur, Akbar or Shah Jahan (U.P. PCS Mains 2016, Lower Sub. 2009, IAS 1993).
  • Atala Mosque (Jaunpur), Bara Sona Masjid (Gaur) and Quwwat-ul-Islam are not Sher Shah's (I.A.S. 1993).
  • Sasaram tomb — Sher Shah's own mausoleum in Rohtas district, Bihar: an imposing brick structure partly veneered with stone, octagonal plan, standing in the middle of a tank; regarded as one of the noblest specimens of Afghan architecture. Begun in his reign (1540–45), completed 16 August 1545 under Islam Shah. Only the Sasaram tomb and Qila-e-Kuhna mosque were his (Rajasthan PCS 2024; Tomb of Sikandar, Jama Masjid are not).
  • Buried at Sasaram (U.P. PCS Mains 2015; M.P. PCS 2016; 67th BPSC 2022) — not Kalinjar, Jaunpur, Patna.
  • Historical text: Tarikh-i-Sher Shahi by Abbas Khan Sarwani (see Literature chapter).

Akbar — Accession, Regency & Early Challenges

1542–1562 · Bairam Khan, Hemu and the end of tutelage
Akbar — identity card
AspectDetail
Birth15 October 1542 at Amarkot (Sindh) in Raja Veersaal's palace, during Humayun's refuge.
CoronationCrowned at Kalanaur (present Gurdaspur district, Punjab) on 14 February 1556 on hearing of Humayun's death (fall from the staircase of the Dinpanah library) — age less than 14 (13 years). Shah Abdul Mali refused to join the coronation; Humayun had called him "Farzand" (son). (Chhattisgarh PCS 2015 → Kalanaur, not Kabul, Lahore or Sirhind.)
ContemporariesContemporary of Queen Elizabeth I of England (reign 1558–1603); the English East India Company was formed in 1600 when Akbar was emperor (U.P. PCS Mains 2002; 63rd BPSC 2017 — not Aurangzeb, Jahangir or Humayun). Guru Ramdas, 4th Sikh Guru, was Akbar's contemporary (not Aurangzeb's).
Historical verdictCalled "an enlightened despot" — like Queen Elizabeth I and Frederick the Great, an emperor with complete authority under whom subjects were equal before law (U.P. PCS Mains 2009).
ExtentEmpire reached from Bengal to Afghanistan in the north and Kashmir to the Godavari in the south; map question (I.A.S. 1998): at his death in 1605, Gondwana was still independent (A) and Lahore (B) was within the empire.
Death1605; ended a reign of 49 years; resting place Sikandra (see Architecture chapter).

The regency of Bairam Khan (1556–1560)

  • Bairam Khan was Akbar's tutor, guardian and regent and adviser to Humayun. Akbar appointed him Wazir and gave him the title Khan-i-Khanan — the title was conferred at the time of appointment as wazir (U.P. PCS 2024: statement 1 true, statement 2 false).
  • Under him early conquests expanded the empire from Kabul to Jaunpur, including Gwalior and Ajmer.
  • External challenge — Hemu: Adil Shah (Sur) made Hemu his wazir and titled him Vikramajit to expel the Mughals. Hemu (Hem Chandra Vikramaditya) had won 22 consecutive battles against Afghan rebels and Mughal forces, defeated Tardi Beg, conquered Agra and advanced to Delhi, assuming the title Vikramaditya (Jharkhand PCS 2013).
  • Second Battle of Panipat — 5 November 1556: Bairam Khan, for Akbar, defeated Hemu; Hemu was beheaded by Bairam Khan, neutralising the Suri threat. The opponents are Hemu and Akbar — not Tipu Sultan and the Marathas (68th BPSC 2022; M.P. PCS 1994 match: 1556).
  • Internal challenge: Bairam Khan was dismissed for arrogance towards other nobles; his rebellion was swiftly crushed by Akbar. He was assassinated en route to Mecca; his family moved to Delhi, and his son Abdur Rahim rose to prominence and got the title Khan-e-Khanan.

Harem and noble intrigue

  • Adham Khan (son of Maham Anga) assassinated Akbar's prime minister Atgah Khan — Akbar personally killed Adham Khan in 1562 (U.P. PCS Mains 2010 — not Bairam Khan, Baz Bahadur, Pir Muhammad Khan).
  • Maham Anga built the Khair-ul-Manzil mosque opposite the Purana Qila, Delhi; it later served as a madrasa and is called Madarsa-e-Begum (U.P. PCS Mains 2012; Pre 2018 — not Gulbadan, Hamida Banu, Salima Sultan, Jiji Anga).

Prince Salim's rebellion and the murder of Abul Fazl

  • Prince Salim behaved as an independent king at Allahabad (Prayagraj); Akbar summoned his friend Abul Fazl from the Deccan in 1602. Vir Singh Bundela assassinated Abul Fazl on the way, in a plot contrived by Salim (Jharkhand PCS 2013 → Salim, not Rahim, Murad or Daniyal). After accession Jahangir rewarded Bir Singh Bundela with high rank (U.P. PCS Mains 2003).
  • Death-sequence trap (U.P. PCS 2024): Sheikh Mubarak (1593, father of Abul Fazl & Faizi) → Faizi (1595) → murder of Abul Fazl (1602) → Daniyal (1605).
Akbar — assessment-type questions
  • Why popular (U.P. PCS 1990, answer d): Mansabdari, religious policy, land-revenue system and social reforms — all four.
  • Akbar as "nationalist" (R.A.S. 1994): administrative and legal unity, cultural unity and religious policy helped — but the false factor is "Akbar had forsaken Islam"; he did not abandon Islam.
  • Reign known for (Uttarakhand 2003): annexation of regions, provincial administration and judicial administration — not religious intolerance.
  • Historians: Stanley Lane-Poole — the "assimilation of the Hindu chiefs" was the most conspicuous feature of the reign; Dr. R.P. Tripathi — Akbar was both a child and a father of his era.
  • Educational reforms (M.P. PCS 2014): Akbar included arithmetic, logic, mensuration, geometry, astronomy, physiognomy, accountancy, public administration and agriculture in the primary curriculum — despite having received no formal education himself.

Akbar — The Mansabdari System

Rank, salary and cavalry in one number
Mansabdari — features
FeatureDetail
MeaningMansab (Arabic) = position / rank. A grading system fixing (1) rank, (2) salary, (3) military responsibility (65th BPSC).
IntroducedBy Akbar — dated 1574–75 (19th year of reign, 1575 in one source). Dagh and Chehra revived in 1573–74. The dual system of Zat and Sawar implemented in the 40th year (1595-ish; text notes 1591–95).
OriginBased on the decimal system and taken from Mongolia / Central Asia — Genghis Khan's army division (I.A.S. 1994 answer: Mongolia; not Afghanistan, Turkey, Persia) — though the Mughal system was largely new.
PurposeClean and better administration (I.A.S. 1996; 65th BPSC 2019 — not merely army recruitment, revenue collection or religious harmony). Common to military and civil departments — not all mansabdars were military officers; civil post-holders were also graded (R.A.S. 2013 both true).
Zat / SawarZat = personal rank/salary/prestige; Sawar = number of cavalrymen (and their horses) the mansabdar had to maintain. Mansabdars brought troopers for review, had them registered, horses branded, then received money as salary.
Horse ratio20 horses for every 10 cavalrymen (rested horses on march; replacements in war) — I.A.S. 1999 A and R true, R explains A.
Grades33 classes in practice (Abul Fazl lists 66); lowest commanded 10, highest 10,000 (U.P. PCS Mains 2009; UDA 2010). Ranks 10–5,000 under Akbar; Raja Man Singh and Mirza Aziz Koka held 7,000.
Three categoriesMansabdars below 500 zat; Amirs 500 to below 2,500; Amir-i-Umda / Umda-i-Azam 2,500 and above.
AppointmentThe king himself appointed, promoted, demoted or removed (on Mir Bakshi's recommendation); mansabdars could be given any civil or military duty; recruited on merit/qualification (Chhattisgarh 2008: A and R both true, R not the explanation).
PaymentSalary via jagirs (like iqtas, but mansabdars did not reside in or administer them — servants collected revenue; jagirs transferable and reverted to the state on death); salary of troopers added to the mansabdar's own; from it he maintained a quota of horses, elephants, camels, mules and carts. Mansabs were not hereditary (U.P. PCS 2019: statement 1 true, 2 false; U.P. UDA 2010 answer d).
NobilityInitially Central Asians & Persians; later Rajputs and Shaikhzadas (Indian Muslims).
Mansabdari traps
  • "Sawar rank could exceed Zat rank" is the statement marked false/"not true" in U.P. PCS Mains 2009 (option c). The nuance in the explanation: in difficult terrain or against rebels the state increased sawar rank without altering zat — this is the Mashrut (conditional) rank, an increase of sawar for a temporary period.
  • "Salary paid by the Diwan's office" — false (UDA Spl 2010). Mansabs were not hereditary. Only statements "Zat and Mansab designations were granted" and "three classes of mansabdars" were true (answer d).
  • Jahangir added Do-aspa / Sih-aspa (Jahangir chapter); Shah Jahan's expansion of mansabdars consumed >¾ of revenues (Shah Jahan chapter).

Akbar — Religious Policy, Sulh-i-Kul & Social Reforms

Ibadat Khana, Mahzar, Din-i-Ilahi
Context — why Akbar's policy matteredAkbar was the first Muslim ruler who realised that Mughal rule could not be established and consolidated in India without winning the confidence and active support of the Hindu majority. Religion had been a divisive force since the coming of Islam; Akbar tried to separate religion from politics and build Hindu–Muslim communities into national unity through Sulh-i-Kul — peace with all (U.P. PCS 1990/2004; R.A.S. 1994). Yet he had not forsaken Islam.
Reform steps of Akbar (year-wise)

1563Pilgrimage tax abolishedAbolished the pilgrimage tax (the theory text lists jizya 1564, pilgrim tax 1563; PYQ text is transposed — jizya 1564, pilgrim tax 1563). Akbar is the Muslim ruler who abolished it (53rd–55th BPSC 2011 — not Bahlul Lodi, Sher Shah, Humayun).

—Social measuresForced sati outlawed; widow remarriage allowed; child marriage discouraged; permitted Hindus to repair/build temples. Ordered that a man should marry only one wife, taking a second only if the first proved barren (U.P. PCS Mains 2002). StepDetail
Ibadat Khana (Hall of Prayers), Fatehpur SikriBuilt in 1575 for philosophical and theological discussions with Ulema, Sayyids and Shaikhs (M.P. Din-i-Ilahi / Tauhid-i-Ilahi (1582)"Divine Monotheism"; belief in one God and Sulh-i-Kul, taking positive aspects of all faiths on a rational basis (as documented by Badayuni). Faded after his death. Not a religion but an ideology based on Sufi pantheism. Among Hindus Birbal alone accepted it (with Abul Fazl, Faizi in theory); Raja Bhagwan Das and Man Singh refused bluntly. Abul Fazl is described as its prophet in one PYQ explanation. Mohsin Fani (Jahangir's contemporary) first described it as an independent religion in Dabistan-i-Mazahib — hence the question "which historian said Din-i-Ilahi is a religion?" (U.P. PCS 2000) has "none of these" (not Abul Fazl, Badauni or Nizamuddin). V.A. Smith: "monument of Akbar's foolishness, not of his wisdom".
Sulh-i-KulIdeal of universal peace (Abul Fazl): all religions and schools have freedom of expression provided they do not undermine the state or fight among themselves; result of political generosity, religious tolerance and liberal cultural attitude (U.P. PCS 1998, Jharkhand 2003).

Chishti devotion, Jains and Christians

  • Chishti sect: Akbar frequently visited the Dargah of Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti (Ajmer; U.P. PCS Mains 2012) and the shrine of Shaikh Salim Chishti at Sikri.
  • Hari Vijay Suri — Jain monk who stayed a few years at Akbar's court and was honoured with the title Jagadguru (U.P. PCS Mains 2002; Chhattisgarh 2024; U.P. PCS 2012) — Akbar was so influenced by Jain non-violence that he became vegetarian and prohibited slaughter of animals and birds. Jin Chandra Suri — another Jain scholar — was titled Yug Pradhan.
  • Jesuit missions from Goa (Chhattisgarh 2022): the 1st mission reached Fatehpur Sikri in 1580; the 2nd reached Lahore in 1591; the 3rd (1595) also met Akbar at Lahore, led by Jerome Xavier (nephew of Francis Xavier) and included Antonio Monserrate, whom Akbar hired as tutor for his son Murad. European paintings arrived through the Portuguese (see Painting chapter).
  • Fatwa (1580): Mulla Muhammad Yazdi of Jaunpur issued a fatwa against Akbar asking Muslims to rebel (U.P. PCS Mains 2011).
  • Nauruz: the Mughals adopted the Nawruz festival from Persia/Zoroastrians (not Jews, Mongols, Turks); a national festival celebrated for 19 days; called Nawruz-i-Jalali by Badauni (U.P. PCS 2001).

The Later Mughals — Bahadur Shah I to Bahadur Shah II

Context — after AurangzebThe empire, born of Panipat in 1526, flourished nearly three centuries and shaped India's cultural and administrative fabric — but after Aurangzeb's death in 1707 it declined, culminating in its end in 1857. Nobles became king-makers and emperors their puppets.
The later Mughal emperors — at a glance
Emperor & reignKey facts
Bahadur Shah I (Muazzam) 1707–1712Ascended in his 60s; won the succession war against Muhammad Azam Shah at the Battle of Jajau (1707) (U.P. PCS 2019). Foreign invasions

  • Nadir Shah (Iranian; expelled Afghans from Iran) — decisive victory at the Battle of Karnal in 1739 over Muhammad Shah; plundered Delhi (massacre), looting treasures including the Kohinoor and the Peacock Throne. It exposed Mughal vulnerability, diminished prestige and emboldened regional powers (M.P. PCS 1994: massacre 1739).
  • Ahmad Shah Abdali (successor of Nadir Shah): invaded multiple times (1748–69 range; last invasion 1767). He seized Delhi and acknowledged Alamgir II as emperor, leaving an Afghan caretaker and appointing Najib-ud-Daula, the Rohilla chief, as "supreme agent".
  • 1758: Maratha chief Raghunath Rao expelled Najib-ud-Daula and conquered Punjab. 1759: Abdali returned; Third Battle of Panipat — 14 January 1761: Afghans under Abdali decisively defeated the Marathas led by Sadashiv Rao Bhau.

  • Rulers in order: Jahangir (1605–27) → Muhammad Shah (1719–48) → Ahmad Shah Abdali (Third Panipat, 14 Jan 1761) → Bahadur Shah II (1837–57) (U.P. PCS 2001/2002/2003).

  • Babur did not establish the Delhi Sultanate — Qutbuddin Aybak did (GIC 2010); Shah Jahan did not fight the war of succession; Jahangir was a lover of beauty and nature.

Reasons for the Decline of the Mughal Empire

1707–1857 · structural causes
Causes of decline — theory book
CauseExplanation
Weak leadership & succession conflictsNo clear law of succession → wars of succession, especially after Aurangzeb.
Aurangzeb's policiesReligious orthodoxy strained relations with non-Muslim subjects.
Economic strain & administrative inefficiencyProlonged southern campaigns drained the treasury; the empire became too vast to manage; distant provinces gained independence; corruption and internal strife eroded the administration.

Mughal Painting

Humayun → Akbar → Jahangir → regional schools
Context — character of Mughal paintingMughal painting was a unique blend of Indian, Persian and Islamic traditions; it illustrated battle scenes, animals, birds, natural scenes and portraits of courtiers (U.P. PCS 1991 — "all of the above"). The Mughal School formed the "spinal cord" of Indian miniature art and spread to regional centres — giving rise to Pahari, Rajasthani and Kangra styles — but the Kalighata school was indigenous and not affected by it (I.A.S. 1995). Pahari, Rajput, Mughal, Kangra are styles of medieval painting (U.P. PCS 1994).
Mughal painting — reign by reign
ReignDevelopment
Babur / HumayunHumayun laid the foundation of Mughal painting during exile in Persia and Afghanistan, where he met Khwaja Abdus Samad and Mir Sayyid Ali; Humayun and his son Akbar took drawing lessons from them; he founded the Nigar Khana (painting workshop attached to his library) and brought the two masters to India; an advanced atelier was set up in the palace (U.P. PCS Mains 2009/2012; U.P. UDA/LDA 2010).
AkbarPainting organised as an imperial Karkhana — Tasvir Khana (initially under Abdus Samad). Karkhanas at Agra 1558–72, Fatehpur Sikri 1572–85, Lahore 1585–1601, and again Agra 1603–05 (66th BPSC 2020 → Akbar). Two themes: daily events of the court and portraits of leading personalities (66th BPSC). Introduced European painting through the Portuguese (63rd BPSC 2017); Mughal painters gained (1) portraiture and (2) depiction of distant objects smaller in size (perspective); Mishkin was a European painter in the court.
JahangirPainting reached its zenith — "Golden Age of Mughal Painting" (I.A.S. 1996; 65th BPSC 2019; U.P. PCS 2016 — Jahangir mainly patronised painting; U.P. Lower Sub. 2016). He freed painting from manuscript illustration; shifted emphasis to albums (Muraqqas) and individual portraits (I.A.S. 2019); encouraged paintings of events from his own life, portraits and studies of birds, flowers and animals; naturalism and scientific accuracy; introduced halos behind the head in portraits; kept a huge aviary and recorded every specimen, organising experiments; patronised European and Persian arts. His influence reached Rembrandt, who drew inspiration from Mughal miniatures.
